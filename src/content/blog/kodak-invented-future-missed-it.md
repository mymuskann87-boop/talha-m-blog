In 1975, inside the headquarters of the most powerful photography company on Earth, a young engineer switched on a strange, heavy box he had built with his own hands. The box did something no camera in history had ever done: it took a photograph without any film at all.

The company was Kodak. The box was the world's first digital camera. And the strangest part of the story is what Kodak did next: instead of showing its invention to the world, it kept it quiet, protected its old business — and slowly watched that business, and finally the whole company, slip away.

This is the true story of how the king of photography invented the future, and then missed it.

## The King Of Photography

To feel the weight of this story, you first have to understand how mighty Kodak was.

The company began with George Eastman, who started his business in Rochester, New York, in 1880. In 1888 he brought out the Kodak camera, simple enough for ordinary families to use — before that, photography had been a job for professionals with plates and chemicals. In 1900 came the cheap Brownie camera, which put photography into the hands of children and grandparents. Colour film, home movies, the famous Kodachrome, the Instamatic cameras of the 1960s — decade after decade, when the world took a picture, it very often took it on Kodak film.

And here is the business secret hidden inside that success: the money was not mainly in the cameras. Cameras were sold once. Film was bought again and again. Every roll of film, every box of photographic paper, every bottle of developing chemical was another sale. A camera was a machine for making customers return forever. For most of the twentieth century, Kodak stood at the centre of photography like a king on a throne — a pioneer, an innovator, and the leading supplier of the films, papers and chemicals the whole world used.

Kings, however, should be careful. Sometimes the danger to a throne is not an invading army. Sometimes it is a young man in your own laboratory.

## A Small Question In 1974

Steven Sasson was an electrical engineer from Brooklyn, New York. He had studied at Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute and joined a Kodak research laboratory in 1973, doing the thing he had loved since boyhood: playing with electronics.

In 1974, his supervisor, Gareth Lloyd, gave him a question to explore. A new invention called the charge-coupled device — the CCD — had recently appeared. It was a small electronic chip that could sense light and turn it into electrical signals. Could such a chip, Lloyd wondered, be used as the eye of a camera?

Sasson was given freedom to tinker, and he threw himself at the puzzle the way he had taken apart radios as a boy. What if a camera needed no film at all? What if light could become electricity, electricity could become numbers, and numbers could be stored — like music on a tape — until you wanted to see your picture?

By December 1975, he had his answer, and he was holding it in both hands. It took both hands. It was that big.

## December 1975: A Camera With No Film

The world's first self-contained digital camera was, by any modern measure, a lovable monster.

It weighed about 3.6 kilograms — roughly 8 pounds, about the size of a toaster. Inside it, Sasson had packed a lens borrowed from a Super 8 movie camera, sixteen nickel-cadmium batteries, an electronic converter, the little CCD chip as its eye, and dozens of circuits wired across six boards. There was even a cassette tape recorder built in, because the camera saved its pictures the way songs were saved in 1975: onto tape.

And the picture itself? By today's standards it was almost nothing. The chip saw the world in just 100 by 100 dots — 0.01 megapixels, in black and white. A modern phone camera captures thousands of times more detail. The camera needed 23 seconds just to write one picture onto its cassette. Then, to look at your photograph, you fed the tape into a separate playback unit connected to a television, and waited another 23 seconds for the image to appear on the screen.

Slow. Heavy. Grainy. And yet — it worked. A photograph had been taken, turned into numbers, stored, and shown again, without a single millimetre of film. Sasson and Lloyd were granted a patent for the "electronic still camera", filed in 1977 — the founding document of the digital age of pictures.

The future had arrived at Kodak, thirty years early, wearing a toaster's body.

## The Future Goes Into A Drawer

So what did the king of photography do with the invention that would one day rule the world?

It kept it quiet. The prototype was not presented to the public. In fact, Kodak did not publicly disclose Sasson's camera until the year 2000 — a quarter of a century later. The patent was filed, the research continued at a gentle pace, but the digital camera was not pushed toward the shops.

Why? Follow the money, and the answer is painfully human. Film was not just Kodak's product; film was Kodak's fortune, renewed with every roll sold. A digital camera threatened that fortune at its root. Why would anyone buy film, paper and chemicals for a camera that needed none of them? To the men guarding the throne, the new invention did not look like an opportunity. It looked like an enemy — an enemy built in their own house.

Fourteen years later, the pattern repeated. In 1989, Sasson and his colleague Robert Hills built the first modern-style digital single-lens reflex camera — a real, self-contained DSLR of the kind professionals would one day swear by. Kodak initially declined to sell it. The reason, recorded plainly by the National Inventors Hall of Fame, was to protect the company's film sales.

Twice, Kodak's own people built the future. Twice, Kodak chose the past.

## The World Moved On

The tragedy for Kodak is that the future did not wait for permission.

Through the 1990s, other companies — companies with no film fortune to protect — pushed digital cameras forward: sharper, smaller, cheaper, year after year. Kodak, late and hesitant, eventually joined the digital race too, and even sold large numbers of digital cameras. But it was chasing a race it could have led from the front since 1975, and it was chasing it while its real money-maker, film, was melting away beneath it.

Then came the final blow: the telephone. As mobile phones gained good cameras of their own, the ordinary digital camera itself began to shrink as a business. Kodak was squeezed from both sides — film dying below, phones rising above — a company that had once owned every step of photography now struggling to own any of it.

## Bankruptcy, And A Much Smaller Kodak

In January 2012, the Eastman Kodak Company filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection. Britannica records the reasons: rising pension costs, falling profitability, and heavy competition in the digital era.

There is one detail in that sad chapter so ironic that no storyteller would dare invent it. During the bankruptcy proceedings, Kodak — the company that had invented the digital camera in 1975 — stopped making digital cameras.

Kodak came out of bankruptcy in September 2013, alive but transformed: a much smaller company, no longer the king of family photographs, now focused mainly on commercial printing and imaging services for business customers. The empire of film had become a modest workshop — respected, surviving, but a shadow of the giant that once decided how the whole world remembered its birthdays, weddings and holidays.

And Steven Sasson? History was kinder to the engineer than to the empire. For sparking the digital age of photography, he received the National Medal of Technology and Innovation in 2009 — the highest honour the United States gives its inventors — and in 2011 he was inducted into the National Inventors Hall of Fame. The man had been right all along. Only his company had refused to listen.

## The Lesson Every Business Should Learn

Kodak's story is now taught wherever business is studied, because its mistake is one that anyone — a giant company, a small shop, even a single person — can make.

Kodak did not fail because it lacked brilliant people. It had Steven Sasson. It did not fail because it missed the new technology. It invented the new technology, patented it, and held it in its hands in 1975. It failed because it loved its present success so much that it treated the future as a threat instead of a gift.

Inventing the future is only the first step. The harder step is daring to change — daring to let a new thing grow, even when it endangers the old thing that feeds you today. Kodak saw tomorrow clearly, earlier than anyone on Earth. It simply could not bring itself to walk into it.

The camera in the drawer took its revenge in the end.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Who invented the first digital camera?**
Steven Sasson, an engineer at Eastman Kodak. In December 1975 he completed the first self-contained digital camera, working from a task set by his supervisor Gareth Lloyd, who is named with him on the patent.

**How good was the first digital camera?**
By modern standards, extremely basic. It weighed about 3.6 kg, captured black-and-white images of just 100 by 100 pixels (0.01 megapixels), took 23 seconds to record a picture onto a cassette tape, and needed a separate unit and a television to show the photo.

**Did Kodak patent the digital camera?**
Yes. Sasson and Gareth Lloyd were granted US patent 4,131,919 for an "electronic still camera", filed in 1977. But Kodak did not publicly disclose the prototype until 2000, and it held back digital products — including a DSLR in 1989 — to protect film sales.

**Why didn't Kodak sell digital cameras right away?**
Because film was where its money was: film, paper and chemicals sold again and again with every photograph. Digital photography threatened that income, so the company protected film instead of leading the change — and competitors with nothing to protect took the lead.

**When did Kodak go bankrupt?**
Kodak filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection in January 2012, and during the proceedings it stopped making digital cameras. It emerged in September 2013 as a much smaller company focused on commercial printing and imaging services.

**Is Kodak still in business today?**
Yes. Kodak still exists, but as a far smaller company than the photography giant of the twentieth century, working mainly in commercial printing and imaging for business clients rather than consumer film and cameras.
