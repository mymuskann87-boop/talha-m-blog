Note: This is a fictional horror story. It is not real, and any resemblance to real people or places is purely coincidental.

If you are reading this, it means I have left the house, and it means I was right to leave. I am writing it down because I need someone to know, and because writing it down is the only way I can be sure it happened the way I remember it, and not the way the house wants me to remember it. My name is Clara. I am twenty-nine years old. Three weeks ago I inherited my grandmother's house, and I have not slept properly since the day I walked into it.

The house is old in the way that only houses loved by one person for a very long time can be old. It smells of lavender and dust. The furniture is heavy and dark. The hallway that runs from the front door to the kitchen is long and narrow, and at its very end, facing the door, there used to hang a mirror. I say "used to" because when I arrived, the mirror was not hanging. It was standing against the wall, covered with a white cloth, and my grandmother had left a note on top of it, in her careful, slanted handwriting. The note said: "Do not uncover this. Ever. Love, Gran."

I obeyed the note for six days. I want that to be clear. I am not the kind of person who ignores warnings. I left the mirror covered. I walked past it every day on my way to the kitchen, and I tried not to think about why a woman who had never been afraid of anything in her life would cover a mirror and write a note about it in the same careful hand she used for birthday cards.

On the seventh day, the cloth moved.

## The Mirror Reflection That Moved First

I was standing in the hallway with a basket of laundry when I saw it out of the corner of my eye. The cloth over the mirror shifted, just slightly, as if something beneath it had breathed. There was no wind. The front door was shut. Every window in the house was closed. And yet the cloth moved, a slow ripple from top to bottom, the way cloth moves when a hand passes beneath it.

I stood there for a long time, laundry in my arms, telling myself that old houses settle, that cloth slips, that I had imagined it because I had been thinking about the mirror too much. Then the cloth moved again. This time it did not ripple. It lifted at one corner, as if someone beneath it had pinched the fabric between two fingers and raised it, just enough to look out.

I dropped the basket. Clothes scattered across the hallway floor. I did not pick them up. I walked backward, step by step, until my shoulders touched the front door, and I stood there with my hand on the handle, ready to run, and I watched the covered mirror for a full minute. It did not move again. The cloth hung still. The hallway was silent. And I told myself, firmly, that I would call someone in the morning, a friend, anyone, and ask them to come and take the mirror away, covered, without ever looking under the cloth.

That night I dreamed of my grandmother. She was standing in the hallway, young again, the way she looked in the photographs from before I was born, and she was pointing at the mirror and shaking her head. She did not speak. She only pointed, and shook her head, and then she turned and walked into the kitchen, and I woke up with her name on my lips and the feeling that I had been warned twice, once in writing and once in a dream, and that I should listen.

I did not listen. I wish I could tell you that I did. But curiosity is a stubborn thing, and it grows strongest in the places where we have been told not to look, and by the third morning I had convinced myself that the note was simply my grandmother's superstition, that old people cover mirrors when someone dies, that it was a custom, nothing more. I told myself I would lift the cloth for one second. Just one second. Just enough to see that it was an ordinary mirror, and then I would cover it again and laugh at myself.

## The Mirror Reflection That Was Not Mine

The cloth was heavier than I expected. It smelled of dust and lavender, like the rest of the house, and when I lifted it, the mirror beneath was dark at first, clouded with age, and then it cleared, and I saw myself standing in the hallway.

Except it was not myself.

The woman in the mirror had my face. She had my hair, my height, the same grey sweater I was wearing. But she was standing too still. I was breathing hard, my chest rising and falling, my hands shaking on the cloth. She was not breathing at all. She was simply standing there, looking at me, with an expression I have never worn in my life, an expression of terrible patience, the way someone looks when they have waited a very long time and are not in any hurry anymore.

I raised my hand. She did not raise hers. I stepped back. She stayed where she was. And then, very slowly, she smiled, and her smile was wrong in a way I cannot describe without feeling sick, because it was my smile, and it was not my smile, and it knew things about me that I had never told anyone, the way a reflection knows the things you only say when you think you are alone.

I dropped the cloth. It fell back over the mirror, and I stumbled backward down the hallway, and I did not stop until I was outside, standing in the garden in the rain, shaking so hard I could barely hold my phone. I called my friend. I told her I needed her to come, right now, and I did not explain why, because I did not have words for it yet. She came within the hour. She walked into the hallway with me, and we stood together in front of the covered mirror, and I told her what I had seen, and she listened the way good friends listen when you tell them something impossible, without interrupting, without laughing.

Then she did something I will never forget. She reached out and lifted the cloth herself, quickly, before I could stop her, and we both looked into the mirror together.

It showed the hallway. It showed my friend. It showed me. It showed everything exactly as it should have been, ordinary and empty and still. There was no other woman. There was no wrong smile. There was only us, two frightened people staring at a perfectly normal mirror, and my friend laughed, relieved, and said I had scared myself with an old house and too little sleep, and I laughed too, because I wanted more than anything for her to be right.

## The Reflection That Stayed Behind

She left that evening. I walked her to the door. I watched her car disappear down the lane. And then I went back inside, and I walked down the hallway, and I stopped in front of the mirror, which my friend had left uncovered, because neither of us had thought to cover it again.

The mirror showed the hallway. It showed the doors. It showed the rug. It did not show me.

I stood there for a long time, staring at a reflection of a hallway with no one standing in it, and I understood, with a calm that frightened me more than the fear had, that the woman in the mirror had not been a trick of the glass. She had been waiting for someone to look long enough, and when I did, something had changed places. I was still here, in the house, walking its hallways, writing this letter. But the mirror no longer knew me, because the thing that belongs in a mirror was no longer standing where I stood.

That night, I covered the mirror again. I used a heavier cloth this time, and I tied it down, and I pushed the mirror to face the wall, so that even if the cloth slipped, it would show nothing but plaster. And then I packed a bag, and I left, and I have not gone back. I am writing this from a friend's flat in the city, where the mirrors are new and shallow and show only what is in front of them.

But sometimes, when I pass a shop window at night, I catch my reflection, and it takes a fraction of a second too long to catch up with me. And sometimes, when I wake up, I am not sure whether I am the woman who left the house, or the woman who stayed in the hallway, smiling patiently, waiting for someone else to lift the cloth and look long enough to take her place.

If you ever inherit a house, and you find a mirror covered with a cloth, and a note that says do not uncover it, believe the note. Believe it completely. Because some mirrors do not show you yourself. Some mirrors show you the person who will replace you. And once you have seen her smile, she has already seen you.
