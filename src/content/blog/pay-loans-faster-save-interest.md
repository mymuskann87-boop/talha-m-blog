The ledger had four lines, and the owner had been paying all four for years. Every month the same amounts went out, every month the balances went down by a little, and every month the interest came back like a tide. Nothing was late. Nothing was in default. And yet the loans seemed to last forever, because the payments were spread evenly — like water poured equally on four plants, when one of them was on fire.

Paying loans faster is not about paying more than you can afford. It is about paying in the right order, with the right targets, so that every extra rupee kills the most expensive debt first. This post explains that order plainly, step by step, so a person with more than one loan can see the finish line getting closer.

## List every loan, with its rate

You cannot fight what you cannot see. The first step is a simple list, on paper or on a phone, with three columns for every loan: what it is, how much is left, and what interest rate it charges. Include everything — bank loans, card balances, store instalments, borrowed money from any formal lender.

The interest rate column is the one that matters most. A loan at a high rate costs far more per month than a bigger loan at a low rate, even though the bigger loan looks scarier. For example, a small balance at 30% yearly interest can cost more each month than a balance three times larger at 8%. The rate, not the size, decides which loan burns your money fastest.

Write the list once, and update it when a loan changes. Most people are surprised by what they find — usually one loan quietly eating far more than the rest, hiding behind a small balance.

## Attack the highest rate first, keep minimums everywhere else

This is the heart of the method, often called the avalanche order. Pay the minimum amount on every loan, every month, without exception — minimums keep every account in good standing. Then take every extra amount you can manage, however small, and put it all on the highest-rate loan only, until that loan is gone. Then move the whole extra amount to the next highest rate, and so on.

Why this order? Because interest is charged on the balance, and the highest rate grows fastest. Every rupee taken off the highest-rate balance stops the fastest-growing interest. Paying extra on a low-rate loan while a high-rate loan still grows is like bailing water from the shallow end while the deep end floods.

Some people prefer the opposite order — clearing the smallest balance first for a quick win. That method has a real psychological benefit: an early victory keeps motivation alive. If motivation is your main problem, the smallest-first order is a fair choice. But if saving the most interest is the goal, highest-rate-first wins, and this post recommends it plainly.

For example, if you can manage 2,000 extra a month, putting all 2,000 on the highest-rate loan every month clears it far sooner than splitting 500 across four loans, because the extra money concentrates where the interest grows fastest.

## Put windfalls against the loan, not the lifestyle

Windfalls are the secret weapon of fast payoff. A bonus, a tax refund, a gift, money from selling something unused — these amounts arrive outside the monthly routine, which makes them easy to spend without noticing. Redirect them, whole, against the highest-rate loan, and the balance drops in a way monthly payments never achieve alone.

In my view, a windfall spent on the loan is the only windfall you remember with gratitude a year later, because it is still working for you in lower interest every single month after.

This does not mean living joylessly. Keep a small, fixed celebration from any windfall if you like — a good meal, a small treat. But decide the split before the money arrives, not after it is already half spent. A simple rule many people use: half to the loan, a small part to enjoy, the rest to savings. The exact split matters less than having one.

## Ask about early repayment terms before paying extra

Before sending large extra payments, ask the lender one question: "Are there any penalties or conditions for paying this loan off early?" Some loans charge a fee for early settlement, and a few have rules about how extra payments are applied — whether they reduce the balance or merely prepay future instalments.

Get the answer in writing if you can, even a short message. If there is a penalty, compare it with the interest you would save — sometimes paying the penalty still saves far more, and sometimes it does not. If extra payments only prepay future instalments without reducing the balance, ask how to make a payment that reduces the principal instead. Lenders handle this differently, and asking first prevents the frustration of paying extra and seeing nothing change.

Also ask whether the interest is calculated on the reducing balance. Loans that charge interest on the original amount, no matter how much you repay, behave very differently from loans that charge on what is left. Knowing which kind you hold changes the whole strategy.

## Never take a new loan to pay an old one blindly

This warning deserves its own section, because it is the trap that turns a payoff plan into a deeper hole. Borrowing from one lender to pay another only helps if the new loan is genuinely cheaper — a lower rate, no hidden fees, and a repayment plan you can actually keep. Too often, the new loan carries fees, a longer term, or a rate that looks lower but costs more in total.

Before any such move, write down the full cost of both loans: rate, fees, term, and total amount to be repaid. If the new loan does not clearly cost less in total, walk away. And never borrow from informal lenders at punishing rates to cover a formal loan — that trade turns a manageable debt into a dangerous one.

If loans ever feel unmanageable, that is not a personal failure. Talk to the lender about restructuring, or to a trustworthy financial adviser, before the situation worsens. Asking for help early is a financial skill, not a weakness.

## Honest limits of paying loans faster

Paying faster works only with money that actually exists. Skipping meals, ignoring medical needs, or falling behind on rent to pay a loan faster is not a strategy — it trades one problem for worse ones. Essentials come first, minimums on every loan second, and extra payments third, from what remains.

Also, interest is not the only cost of a loan. Fees, insurance bundled with the loan, and penalties all matter, and the cheapest-looking loan is not always the cheapest in total. Read the full terms, not just the rate.

## My Take: Attack The Highest Rate, Ignore The Rest First

The post opens with a ledger of four loans, paid faithfully for years, with balances falling slowly and interest returning like a tide, because payments were spread evenly across all four. Its advice is clear: list every loan with its rate, attack the highest rate first while keeping minimum payments everywhere else, put windfalls against the loan rather than the lifestyle, ask about early repayment terms before paying extra, and never take a new loan to pay an old one blindly. It also states the honest limits of paying loans faster, and it warns against spreading effort equally when one loan is, in the post's image, on fire while the others merely wait.

My judgement is that the highest-rate-first rule is the most valuable piece of advice in the whole post, and also the hardest to follow, because it asks for patience in the wrong place. Human nature prefers quick wins. Paying off a small loan first feels good, because a line disappears from the ledger and the borrower feels progress. Attacking the highest rate first often means paying a larger, slower-moving balance for months without the pleasure of closing an account. I think that discomfort is the price of saving real money. Interest is charged on what you owe, at the rate you owe it, and every extra payment sent to a low-rate loan while a high-rate loan burns is a payment that works less hard than it could. The post's water image is exact: pouring equally on four plants helps the burning one least, and the burning one is the one that costs you most.

Readers should compare two uses of a windfall, because windfalls test character more than paydays do. One use spreads the money across pleasures and small debts, and feels generous in the moment. The other sends the whole windfall, or the largest share of it, against the highest-rate loan, and feels dull on the day. The dull choice is the one that shortens the life of the debt, cuts the total interest paid, and frees future income sooner. The same comparison applies to the warning about new loans. Borrowing to pay borrowing can make sense only when the new terms are clearly better, the fees are known, and the old debt truly closes. Done blindly, it merely moves the fire to a new room and adds a fee for the move. The post is right to insist on asking about early repayment terms first, because a penalty for paying early can quietly eat the saving the extra payment was meant to create.

What should you do differently? List the loans, circle the highest rate, and make that loan the target while protecting every minimum payment. Send windfalls to the target, check terms before each extra payment, and review the list when one loan closes. Paying loans faster is not about working harder. It is about aiming the same effort at the costliest fire first, and letting the other fires wait their turn.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is the avalanche method of paying off loans?**

The avalanche method means paying the minimum on every loan, then putting all extra money on the highest-interest loan first until it is gone, then moving to the next highest. It saves the most interest because it attacks the fastest-growing debt first, though it can take longer to clear the first loan than other methods.

**Is it better to clear the smallest loan first instead?**

Clearing the smallest balance first, called the snowball method, gives a quick victory that keeps some people motivated, which is a real benefit. But it usually costs more interest overall than the avalanche method. Choose snowball if motivation is your main struggle, and avalanche if saving the most money is the goal.

**Should I use a bonus to pay off a loan?**

Yes, in most cases. A windfall put against the highest-rate loan reduces the balance immediately and cuts interest every month after. Decide the split before the money arrives — for example, most to the loan and a small part to enjoy — so it is not spent without a plan.

**Can I be charged for paying a loan off early?**

Some lenders charge an early-settlement fee, and some apply extra payments to future instalments rather than reducing the balance. Always ask the lender about penalties and how extra payments are applied before sending large extra amounts, and get the answer in writing if possible.

**Is it wise to take a new loan to pay off an old one?**

Only if the new loan clearly costs less in total — lower rate, no heavy fees, and a term you can keep. Compare the full cost of both loans first. If the new loan is not clearly cheaper, or comes from an informal lender at a punishing rate, do not do it — it turns a manageable debt into a deeper one.
