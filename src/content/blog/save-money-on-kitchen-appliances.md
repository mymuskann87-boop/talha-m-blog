On the kitchen counter stood a parade of appliances, each claiming to be essential. The mixer, the juicer, the sandwich maker, the fancy kettle, the machine that made one specific kind of bread. Most of them wore a thin layer of dust. One of them — the plain mixer, bought years ago — was the only one with a clean cord and a warm motor, because it was the only one that worked every single week.

The parade had cost a small fortune, one shiny box at a time. The lesson it taught was simple and slightly embarrassing: the most expensive appliance in a kitchen is usually the one nobody uses.

Kitchen appliances earn their place the way everything else in a saving household does: by working often, lasting long, and costing little to run. Everything else is decoration with a plug.

## The most expensive appliance is the unused one

Before buying any appliance, ask one question and answer it honestly: how many times a week will I actually use this?

A mixer used four times a week is cheap at almost any price, because its cost spreads across hundreds of uses. A specialised gadget used twice a year is expensive at any price, because its whole cost sits on two uses. For example, a 12,000 gadget used twice a year for five years costs 1,200 per use, while a 6,000 mixer used two hundred times a year costs almost nothing per use. Those are example figures, but the maths behind them is the whole point: frequency decides value, not the price tag.

In my view, the dusty gadget on the top shelf is the real luxury in most kitchens, because it took money, space, and attention and gave back almost nothing.

Wait a month before buying a specialised appliance. If you still want it after thirty days of cooking without it, it may be a real need. If you forgot about it, the kitchen just saved you its price without any effort at all.

## Compare running costs, not just price tags

Two appliances with the same job can cost very different amounts to run. One fridge sips electricity. Another drinks it. One kettle boils fast and switches itself off. Another keeps drawing power in standby. Over five or ten years, the running cost can quietly overtake the purchase price.

Efficient appliances use less electricity for the same work. That is worth checking before you buy, not after the first shocking bill. Look for the energy rating where one is shown, ask the seller about typical consumption, and compare it with the model you already own. A slightly dearer appliance that uses much less power is often the cheaper appliance by the second year.

Standby power deserves attention too. Appliances left plugged in and glowing — a microwave clock, a coffee machine light — draw small amounts all day and all night. Switching them off at the socket when not in use costs nothing and saves a little every month, which is the best kind of saving: free.

## Simple and repairable beats feature-heavy

A machine with twenty functions has twenty ways to break. A machine with three functions, built simply, has three. When the feature-heavy machine fails, the repair often costs more than the machine is worth, or the part is no longer available, and the whole thing becomes waste.

Simple models are usually cheaper to buy, cheaper to repair, and easier for a local technician to fix. Ask before buying: can this be repaired locally? Are spare parts available? Is there a service centre nearby? A "yes" to those questions is worth more than five extra functions you will never use.

This is not an argument against good technology. A genuinely useful feature — a safety switch, a timer that prevents burning, a jar that fits your hand — is worth paying for. The test is whether the feature serves your real cooking, or only the advertisement.

## Maintenance makes appliances last

Most appliances do not die of old age. They die of neglect. A mixer with a never-cleaned jar strains its motor. A fridge with blocked vents works twice as hard. A kettle thick with scale takes longer to boil and uses more power every time.

Maintenance is mostly small, dull work: clean the filters, descale when needed, wipe the vents, follow the manual's simple instructions. It takes minutes, and it adds years. An appliance that lasts ten years instead of five has effectively cost half as much — and it saved you the whole bother of shopping for a replacement.

Keep the manuals, or at least the model numbers, in one place. When a part is needed, the model number turns a week of searching into a single phone call. When a technician visits, a short history — "it started making this noise two weeks ago" — turns guessing into diagnosis, and diagnosis into a smaller bill.

## Wait for real need and real sales

Impulse displays exist to make waiting feel foolish. The shining appliance at the shop entrance, the festival discount shouting from a poster, the "today only" offer — all of them are designed to hurry a decision that deserves a week.

Two waits protect a kitchen budget. First, wait for real need: buy when the old appliance fails, or when a genuine new need appears, not when a display looks exciting. Second, wait for a genuine sale: festival seasons and clearance periods do bring real discounts, and a planned purchase timed to a real sale saves money without any sacrifice.

The difference between a bargain and a trap is whether you wanted the item before you saw the discount. If the discount created the want, the discount won, not you.

A useful habit is a waiting list on the fridge: write the appliance you want, the date, and the price you saw. Check the list after a month. Items still on the list with a genuine use are worth buying. Items you forgot were ever there have already saved you their price.

## The honest limits

No appliance strategy can beat a genuinely tiny kitchen budget, and no maintenance can save a machine that was poorly built. Sometimes the right decision is a professional repair, and sometimes it is replacement — the manual, the technician, and the age of the machine will tell you which. What this post offers is not magic. It is the habit of buying for use, checking running costs, choosing repairable simplicity, and maintaining what you own — habits that quietly cut kitchen costs year after year.

## Common questions

### What is the most expensive kitchen appliance to own?

Usually the one nobody uses. A specialised gadget bought for a high price and used twice a year costs far more per use than a plain, often-used mixer. Before buying, ask honestly how many times a week you will really use it, and wait a month — if you forget about it, you never needed it.

### Why should I compare running costs of kitchen appliances?

Because over five or ten years, electricity use can quietly overtake the purchase price. An efficient appliance that costs slightly more at the shop but uses much less power is often cheaper by the second year. Check energy ratings where shown and ask about typical consumption before buying.

### Are simple appliances better than feature-heavy ones?

Often, yes. A machine with twenty functions has twenty ways to break, and repairs can cost more than the machine is worth. Simple, repairable models are cheaper to buy, easier for a local technician to fix, and more likely to last. Pay for features that serve your real cooking, not the advertisement.

### How does maintenance save money on kitchen appliances?

Most appliances die from neglect, not old age. Cleaning filters, descaling, wiping vents, and following the manual add years to an appliance's life — an appliance lasting ten years instead of five has effectively cost half as much, and it saves the bother of shopping for a replacement.

### Should I buy kitchen appliances during sales?

Yes, when the purchase was already planned. A genuine festival or clearance discount on an item you wanted before seeing the offer is a real saving. If the discount itself created the want, it is a trap, not a bargain — wait, and buy when the need is real.
