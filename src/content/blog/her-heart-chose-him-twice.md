Amara knew, the very first second she saw him, that something was wrong with her memory. It was a Tuesday morning at the little railway station café, rain on the windows, the smell of coffee and old wood in the air. The man in line ahead of her turned to pick up his cup, and he smiled at her — a quick, easy, apologetic smile, the smile of a stranger making room in a crowded place.

Her heart stopped. Not in a poetic way. In a real, frightening, breath-catching way, as if her body had recognised something her mind had completely forgotten. She stood there holding her ticket, staring at a man she had never met, feeling as if she had known him all her life.

"Sorry," he said, stepping aside. "I think I took your sugar."

"You didn't," Amara managed to say. "I mean, it's fine. It's just sugar."

He laughed, and the laugh did something worse to her. It sounded like home. Like a song she had not heard in years but could still hum without thinking.

"I'm Noah," he said.

"Amara," she said, and shook his hand, and felt a strange, warm shock run up her arm, as if she had touched something electric and kind at the same time.

He got on her train. He sat two rows ahead. She spent the whole journey staring at the back of his head and trying to solve the puzzle of him. His hair, dark and untidy. His coat, worn at the cuffs. The way he drummed his fingers on the window when the train slowed down. Everything about him felt like a word on the tip of her tongue.

## A Second Chance Love Story Starts Again

Amara was thirty-one, a schoolteacher, practical to her bones, not the kind of woman who believed in fate or signs or any of that nonsense. She believed in lesson plans and lunch boxes and things you could explain. So she explained the feeling away. He probably looked like an actor. Or a cousin of a friend. Or someone she had once seen in a shop. She got off the train, walked to school, and told herself to forget him.

She could not forget him.

That night, she dreamed of the sea. Not a grand, stormy sea, but a small, friendly one, the kind with gentle waves and a pebbled beach and gulls crying overhead. In the dream, she was twelve years old, and a boy with dark untidy hair was building a castle out of wet sand beside her, and he was laughing, and she was laughing, and the castle kept falling down and they kept building it again. She woke up with sand in her dream-shoes and tears on her face and no idea why.

The next day, Noah was at the station café again. And the day after that. On the fourth day, he sat at her table.

"Do I know you?" he asked, and his voice was careful, the way you speak when you are afraid of sounding foolish. "I keep feeling like we've met before. Somewhere with... the sea, maybe?"

Amara's spoon stopped halfway to her cup. "I dreamed about the sea last night," she whispered. "A beach. With pebbles."

Noah went very pale. "And a sandcastle that kept falling down?"

She nodded, slowly, her whole body trembling. "And a boy who laughed every time it fell."

They stared at each other across the small table while the station announcements echoed and the rain ran down the windows, and the years between them folded up like paper, smaller and smaller, until there were hardly any years left at all.

## The Second Chance Love Story Remembers

It came back in pieces, the way light comes back through a window in the morning — first a little, then all at once. When Amara was twelve, her family had spent one summer in the coastal town of Seabrook, because her father was working there for three months. There she had met a boy named Noah who lived next door to their rented cottage. For one whole summer, they had been inseparable. They built sandcastles. They collected shells. They made a solemn promise, written on a flat white stone, that they would be friends forever, no matter where life took them.

Then summer ended. Amara's family moved back to the city. Noah's family moved to another town the following year. Letters were written for a while, then fewer, then none. The stone with their promise was left on the beach, and the sea, in its patient way, washed the words away. They both grew up. They both forgot names and faces. But somewhere deep down, in the part of the heart that keeps what the mind throws away, they had never forgotten each other at all.

"I kept the shell," Noah said suddenly, reaching into his coat pocket. He held out a small, pink-and-white shell, worn smooth by years of being carried. "You gave it to me on our last day. You said it would bring me luck. I have carried it in every coat I have ever owned."

Amara burst into tears, right there in the station café, in front of the sugar bowls and the sleepy commuters, and she did not care at all. She opened her own bag with shaking hands and pulled out a small cloth pouch. Inside it was a flat white stone. On it, in faded childish letters, were two names and one word: "Amara. Noah. Forever."

"I kept it too," she whispered.

Noah reached across the table and took her hand. His hand was warm and steady, and it felt exactly like coming home after a very long journey. Around them, the café went on with its ordinary morning noise, but for Amara and Noah, the world had gone beautifully, impossibly quiet.

"I think," Noah said, his voice thick, "that my heart knew you before I did."

"And mine knew you twice," Amara said. "Once when we were children. And again, on a Tuesday, in a station café, when you smiled at me."

He laughed through his tears, and she laughed through hers, and then, gently, as if they had all the time in the world — and now, at last, they did — he leaned across the table and kissed her hand, soft as a promise made twice and kept twice.

## The Days After the Station

The days after the station felt like waking up from a long sleep into a morning that was already golden. Amara and Noah met for coffee the next day, and the day after that, and the day after that, until the station café staff began setting aside their usual table without being asked. They talked the way old friends talk, and the way new sweethearts talk, both at once — finishing each other's sentences, laughing at memories that belonged to two twelve-year-olds, and discovering, with wonder, the adults those children had become. Noah was a railway engineer now, a man who loved timetables and bridges and the honest way a train arrives exactly when it promises to. Amara told him about her students, about the noisy classroom that exhausted her and filled her up in equal measure, about the small flat she had painted yellow because she liked waking up to sunshine even on grey days.

One Saturday, Noah asked her a question that made her heart beat fast all over again. "Will you come back to Seabrook with me? Just for the day. I want to see if the beach is still there."

The beach was still there. Smaller than they remembered, as childhood places always are, but the pebbles were the same, and the gulls were the same, and the sea came in with the same patient, foamy sigh. They walked the shoreline together, holding hands like teenagers, until they reached the spot where the sandcastle had stood. Noah knelt down and began to build one, right there, with the same serious concentration he had shown at twelve. Amara laughed until tears ran down her face, and then she knelt beside him and helped, and the castle rose, crooked and beautiful, and this time, when a wave came for it, Noah put his arm around her and said, "Let it fall. We'll build it again. We have time now."

On the train home that evening, as the fields rolled dark past the windows, Amara leaned her head on Noah's shoulder and thought about how strange and generous life could be. It had taken her heart away from someone at twelve, kept the memory safe for nineteen years, and then, on an ordinary Tuesday, in an ordinary station café, handed it back to her, smiling over a cup of coffee. She had chosen him once as a child, without knowing what choosing meant. Now she was choosing him again, with her whole grown-up heart, and that, she decided, was the most romantic thing that had ever happened to her.
