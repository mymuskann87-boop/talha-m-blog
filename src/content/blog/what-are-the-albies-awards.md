In October 2026, a golden gown walked a red carpet in London, and the photographs travelled around the world. Amal Clooney wore gold Gucci to an event at the Natural History Museum, beside her husband George Clooney in a black tailored suit, and the headlines called the event "the Albies." Readers who had never heard the name were left with an obvious question: what are the Albies? The answer is not a film prize, a fashion award, or a celebrity party with a clever name. It is a human rights awards ceremony with a serious purpose, named after a man who spent his life fighting for justice.

The Albies — formally the Albie Awards — are an annual fundraiser organised by the Clooney Foundation for Justice, the organisation George and Amal Clooney co-founded in 2016. The awards honour human rights defenders, journalists, and activists who have worked for justice, often at great personal risk. They are named after Justice Albie Sachs, the South African anti-apartheid figure and former Constitutional Court judge. This guide explains who Albie Sachs was, how the awards began, who was honoured in 2026, and why a glamorous evening in a museum matters to people in danger far from any red carpet.

## Who was Albie Sachs

Albie Sachs was a South African lawyer, writer, and judge whose life was shaped by the fight against apartheid. As a young lawyer, he defended people persecuted under apartheid laws, and he was himself raided by the security police, subjected to banning orders, and placed in solitary confinement without trial. In 1988, while living in exile in Mozambique, he lost an arm and the sight in one eye when a bomb planted in his car by South African security agents exploded. He survived, returned to South Africa, and helped write the country's new constitution after apartheid ended, serving as a judge on its Constitutional Court.

Arab News, reporting on the 2026 ceremony, describes Sachs as a South African anti-apartheid figure and former Constitutional Court justice, and notes that he received a lifetime achievement award at the inaugural Albies ceremony. Naming the awards after him was a choice with meaning: the awards do not honour celebrity, wealth, or fame. They honour the kind of courage Sachs showed — defending justice when doing so costs safety, comfort, and sometimes health itself.

Sachs's story also explains the tone of the evening. The Albies are glamorous, because glamour draws cameras, and cameras draw attention to people the world might otherwise ignore. But the glamour is a vehicle, not the point. The point is the defenders on the stage, and the risks they took to earn their place there.

## The Clooney Foundation for Justice

The Clooney Foundation for Justice was co-founded in 2016 by George and Amal Clooney, and the Albies are its annual fundraiser. Amal Clooney is a British-Lebanese human rights lawyer, a barrister at Doughty Street Chambers in London, who has represented clients before the International Criminal Court and the European Court of Human Rights. Her family left Lebanon for the United Kingdom during the country's civil war, when she was a toddler. George Clooney is the actor whose fame brings the world's cameras to the foundation's work.

The foundation works on justice in places where courts are weak, corrupt, or dangerous: monitoring trials, supporting defenders, and pressing for accountability for human rights abuses. The Albies raise money for that work and, just as importantly, raise the profile of the people the foundation supports. A journalist imprisoned for reporting, an activist threatened for defending girls' rights, or an organisation documenting abuses in a war zone gains a measure of protection when the world knows their name. Attention is a form of safety, and the Albies manufacture attention deliberately.

The event launched in New York in 2022 and has been held in London since the previous year, according to Arab News. Gucci joined as the event's fashion partner in 2026, which explains the coordinated looks on the red carpet — Amal's gold sequinned gown and George's black tailored suit were both by Gucci. The fashion is part of the fundraising machine: the photographs carry the names of the honourees further than any press release could.

## What the Albies honour

The Albies honour human rights defenders, journalists, and activists who have worked for justice, often at great personal risk. That description, from Arab News, is the whole definition, and it is deliberately broad. The honourees are not chosen for fame. They are chosen because their work made a difference and cost them something, and because honouring them publicly helps them continue it.

The 2026 honourees show the range. They included Kyrgyz journalists Bolot Temirov and Makhabat Tazhibek Kyzy; Malawian girls' rights advocate Vanessa Mwangala; Pulitzer Prize-winning American photojournalist Lynsey Addario; Justice Plus, a Congolese human rights organisation; and Brad Smith, Microsoft's Vice Chair and President. A journalist, an advocate, a photographer, an organisation, and a technology executive: five very different kinds of defender, united by the risks they took and the justice they served.

The breadth is the point. Human rights work is not one job. It is reporting, photographing, organising, litigating, and building the systems that make justice possible. The Albies honour the whole ecosystem, from the journalist in a Kyrgyz courtroom to the executive deciding how a global company handles government demands for data. Each honouree's story is told on the night, and each story reaches an audience that would never have found it otherwise.

## Why an awards night matters to defenders

It is fair to ask whether a glamorous evening in a museum helps anyone in real danger. The answer lies in what attention does for a defender. A journalist whose name is known internationally is harder to imprison quietly. An activist whose story has been told on a London stage is harder to harm without consequence. An organisation honoured by a global foundation gains donors, lawyers, and allies it could never reach alone.

The money matters too. The Albies are a fundraiser, and the funds support the foundation's work: trial monitoring, legal support, and advocacy in places where justice is fragile. A ticket, a donation, or a bid at the event becomes a lawyer in a courtroom, a monitor at a trial, or a report that documents abuse for the record. The chain from red carpet to courtroom is long, but it is real, and it is the reason the event exists.

There is also a quieter effect: the honourees meet each other. A Kyrgyz journalist, a Malawian advocate, and a Congolese organisation rarely share a room, and the connections made on such a night — the shared advice, the solidarity, the knowledge that others face the same risks — sustain people in work that is often lonely and frightening. The Albies are a network as well as a ceremony, and networks protect people.

## The Albies and the Clooneys' public role

The Albies also show how George and Amal Clooney have divided their public work. George's fame opens doors and draws cameras; Amal's legal expertise gives the foundation its credibility and its cases. The partnership is visible on the night: he presents, she speaks about the law, and the honourees stand between them, at the centre of the attention both bring.

The arrangement draws criticism as well as praise, as all celebrity activism does. Critics ask whether glamour trivialises suffering, and whether a fundraiser in a museum can speak for people in prisons and war zones. The foundation's answer is practical: the glamour is not the message, it is the megaphone, and the message is the honourees' work. The test is not whether the evening is elegant. It is whether the defenders it honours are safer, better funded, and better known afterwards than they were before.

The Albies will return each year, in London or New York, with new honourees and new headlines. The name will become more familiar, and the question "what are the Albies?" will be asked less often. The hope behind the event is that the names it honours become familiar too — not as victims, but as defenders, whose courage the world finally noticed.

## The first ceremony and the lifetime award

The Albies launched in New York in 2022, and the inaugural ceremony set the pattern every later ceremony has followed: a glamorous room, a serious purpose, and a stage given to defenders. Justice Albie Sachs himself received a lifetime achievement award at that first ceremony, a fitting opening for awards carrying his name. The choice honoured the man before the awards honoured anyone else, and it told every guest what the evening valued: a lifetime of courage in the face of power, recognised while the person who showed it could still stand on the stage. The awards have been held in London since the previous year, and the move across the Atlantic has not changed the formula. The room changes, the city changes, and the purpose stays exactly where the first ceremony placed it.

## What to watch for at future ceremonies

Future Albies ceremonies will follow the same shape: a host, performers, a famous guest list, and a small group of honourees whose names most of the audience will not know. The honourees are the part worth watching. Their stories, told on the night and reported afterwards, are the reason the event exists, and they are the names to remember when the fashion coverage fades. A reader who wants to follow the Albies should look past the red carpet photographs to the citations: who was honoured, what they risked, and what their work needs next. The gowns will change every year. The courage will not, and it is the courage the awards were built to honour.
