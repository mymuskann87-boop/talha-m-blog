Monday was the day the heat moved in properly. By three in the afternoon the front room felt like the inside of a parked car, the ceiling fan was spinning at full speed and losing, and the electricity meter outside was ticking away like it had somewhere important to be. I stood in the doorway with a glass of water, sweating through my shirt, and did the maths nobody wants to do in summer: if the house stayed like this for a week, the bill at the end of the month would hurt just as much as the heat.

That week taught the house to breathe. Not all at once, and not through one big purchase. A few small changes, stacked on top of each other, turned the same rooms from ovens into places a person could sit, work, and sleep. None of them needed an expert. All of them together cut the cooling bill in a way that showed up on paper, not just in feeling.

## The Fan Does the Heavy Lifting

Start with the cheapest machine in the house. A fan does not cool air the way an air conditioner does. It moves air across your skin, and moving air carries heat away from your body, so you feel cooler even though the room temperature barely changes. That difference matters because a fan uses only a small fraction of the power an air conditioner drinks. Running a fan all evening costs pennies next to running a compressor all evening.

The trick is placement. A fan blowing straight at an empty chair cools nobody. Put it where people actually sit, and let it run while the room is occupied. At night, a fan near the bed, aimed low across the body, does more for sleep than a cold room does. Many people sleep better slightly warm with moving air than freezing still, and the fan keeps the bill small while it works.

Ceiling fans deserve a look too. Most have a small switch on the body that changes the direction of the blades. In summer the blades should push air downward, so you feel the breeze standing underneath. Dust on the blades also matters more than people think. A thick coat of dust makes the motor work harder and throws dirty air around the room, so a quick wipe at the start of summer is a job worth doing.

## Shade First, Cooling Second

The cheapest cool air is the heat that never enters the house. Sunlight pouring through a bare window turns a room into a greenhouse by noon, and once the walls and furniture have soaked up that heat, no machine can cheaply undo it. Curtains and shades closed against direct sun are the first line of defence, not an afterthought.

The rule is simple: keep the sun off the glass during the hours it hits hardest. On the side of the house that faces the afternoon sun, close the curtains before lunch, not after the room is already hot. Light-coloured curtains reflect more heat than dark ones, and a curtain hung close to the window traps a layer of air that slows the heat down. Outside shade works even better when it exists — a tree, a bamboo screen, or an awning stops the sun before it touches the glass at all.

Personally, I believe the curtain does the real work long before the air conditioner ever switches on. It is a quiet piece of cloth doing a job that would otherwise cost electricity all afternoon. People who cool their homes cheaply almost always shade first and chill second, because fighting heat at the window is cheaper than fighting it with a machine.

## Let the Evening Air In

Houses need to breathe, and summer evenings are when they can. Once the outside air turns cooler than the inside air — usually after sunset — open windows on opposite sides of the house. Air flows in one side and out the other, carrying the day's trapped heat with it. This cross-breeze can drop a stuffy room by several degrees without a single unit of electricity.

The mistake is leaving everything shut all night out of habit, or opening only one window so the air has nowhere to go. Two openings make a path. A window and a door to a ventilated corridor can work the same way. In the morning, close up again before the sun climbs, trapping the cooler night air inside like water in a jug.

On still nights with no wind at all, a fan placed in one window, blowing outward, pulls cooler air in through the other openings. It is an old trick, and it works because the fan creates the path the wind did not provide. Security matters, of course — ground-floor windows left open at night need grills or locks that still allow airflow, and common sense about the street outside always comes first.

## If You Run the AC, Run It Sensibly

Air conditioning cools indoor air using a machine, and it uses significant electricity to do it. That does not make it the enemy. On the hottest days it is the only thing that works. The goal is to run it like a tool, not like a tap left open.

Set a moderate temperature, not the coldest the machine allows. Every degree colder costs more power, and a room set to a comfortable level cools people just as well as a freezing one. A room at 26 degrees with a fan running feels as pleasant as a room at 22 degrees without one, and the combination uses far less electricity. The fan spreads the cool air around, so the machine does not have to work as hard to reach every corner.

Cool the rooms people use, and close the doors to the rest. Cooling an empty guest room all day is money spent on nobody. A timer helps here: set the machine to switch off an hour before bedtime, when the room is already cool and the night air can take over. Many machines also have an energy-saving or sleep mode that eases off once the target temperature is reached, and using it is free money.

## Clean Filters, Cool People

A dirty filter is a slow leak in the budget. The filter is the mesh that catches dust before it enters the machine, and when it clogs, the machine strains to pull air through, runs longer, cools worse, and drinks more power. Cleaning it takes ten minutes: slide it out, rinse or brush it according to the manual, let it dry fully, and slide it back.

Do this every few weeks in heavy summer use. The difference is often obvious the same evening — stronger airflow, faster cooling, a machine that switches off instead of grinding on. While you are there, check that the outdoor unit, if you can reach it safely, is not buried in leaves or dust. A machine that can breathe cools a room for less.

Cooling the person, not the whole house, is the last piece. A cold drink, light cotton clothes, a damp cloth on the neck, and bare feet on a cool floor all lower what your body feels. A person sitting quietly needs far less cooling than a person rushing around a hot kitchen, so timing heavy chores for cooler hours helps twice: less heat generated indoors, and less cooling needed afterwards.

## When Heat Stops Being About Bills

One limit matters more than all the tips above. In extreme heat, cooling is health, not luxury. Small children, older people, and anyone unwell suffer first, and no saving is worth a dangerous room. If the heat becomes severe and the house will not cool, go somewhere cool — a shaded public place, a relative's home, a community building — and do not feel guilty about the bill that day. Bills can be paid in parts. Heatstroke cannot be undone.

The same honesty applies to expectations. These steps will not make a tin-roofed room in a heatwave feel like a mountain resort. They will make an ordinary home noticeably cooler and noticeably cheaper to run, which is a real result. The house that learned to breathe that Monday week is still not cold in the afternoon. It is liveable, and the bill at month's end no longer stings the way it used to.

## Common questions

### Does a fan actually cool a room?

A fan cools people, not rooms. It moves air across skin, which carries body heat away, so you feel cooler while the room temperature stays almost the same. That is why a fan in an empty room wastes power, and a fan aimed at the person sitting there is money well spent.

### What AC setting feels comfortable and saves power?

A moderate setting, around 25 to 27 degrees, with a fan running, feels comfortable for most people and uses much less power than the coldest setting. Every degree colder adds to the electricity use, so comfort, not coldness, is the target.

### Do curtains really make a difference?

Yes, a large one. Sunlight through bare glass heats a room fast, and curtains closed before the sun hits hardest stop much of that heat from entering. Light-coloured curtains reflect more heat, and outside shade like an awning or tree works even better.

### How often should I clean the AC filter?

Every few weeks during heavy summer use is a good habit. A clogged filter makes the machine work harder, cool slower, and use more power. Cleaning takes about ten minutes and often improves cooling the same evening.

### Is it safe to sleep with windows open at night?

It can be, with care. Use windows with grills or locks that allow airflow, and judge the street outside. A fan in one window blowing outward can pull cool air through other openings without leaving the house wide open.
