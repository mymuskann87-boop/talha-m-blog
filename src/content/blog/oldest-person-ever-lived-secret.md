The oldest person ever lived, by verified record, was Jeanne Louise Calment of France, who lived 122 years and 164 days, from 21 February 1875 to 4 August 1997. She still holds that record, more than 25 years after her death, and the next verified person, Kane Tanaka of Japan, died at 119 years and 107 days, about three years short. Calment's own answer to the secret was disarmingly simple: olive oil, a sense of humour, a calm attitude, and pleasure taken when she could. Researchers add a less quotable answer: genetics, a life without hard manual work or financial stress, a Mediterranean diet, and luck. There is no single secret, and anyone selling one — a food, a pill, a routine — is selling a simplification of a life that lasted through two world wars, the invention of cinema, and the dawn of the internet.

## Born in Arles in 1875, Into a Long-Lived Family

Jeanne Calment was born on 21 February 1875 in Arles, Bouches-du-Rhône, Provence, in southern France. Her father, Nicolas Calment, a shipbuilder, lived to 93, dying in 1931. Her mother, Marguerite Gilles, from a family of millers, lived to 86, dying in 1924. Her older brother, François, lived to 97, dying in 1962. That family pattern is the first clue that genetics mattered: Calment did not come from a family where people died young. She came from a family where reaching 85, 90, and 97 was already happening before modern medicine, antibiotics, or advanced cardiac care.

She married her wealthy cousin Fernand Calment in 1896, and, because the family was well off, she never had to work for wages. That fact, often mentioned as a curiosity, is actually central to her longevity story. A woman who never worked a factory shift, never carried heavy loads, never faced food insecurity, and never had to choose between rest and rent, starts life with a health advantage that no diet can fully replicate. She had help with cooking and shopping, time for cycling, swimming, tennis, and roller skating, and time to rest when ill. Her life was, by the standards of 1875 to 1997, remarkably low in chronic physical stress, even though it contained great personal loss: she outlived her husband, her daughter Yvonne, and her grandson Frédéric.

She also claimed, as a teenager, to have met Vincent van Gogh in 1888, when he came into her father's shop in Arles. She reportedly did not find him charming, describing him, in later retellings, as dirty and disagreeable. That claim is often repeated, but it should be treated as Calment's own memory, not as a documented meeting with a receipt or a letter to prove it. Its value is not as proof of a meeting. Its value is as a measure of time: a woman who could, plausibly, have seen van Gogh in Arles in 1888, lived long enough to be interviewed about it on television in the 1990s. That span — 1888 to 1997 — is the record in human terms.

## Olive Oil, Chocolate, Wine, and Cigarettes: The Famous Contradiction

Calment's diet, as reported by Guinness World Records and by profiles summarising her own accounts, centred on olive oil. She cooked with it, poured it over food, and, by her own account, rubbed it on her skin. She lived in Provence, surrounded by Mediterranean food: vegetables, fruit, fish, and healthy fats, with olive oil as the staple fat rather than butter or lard. Modern research on the Mediterranean diet — lower heart disease risk, lower inflammation, lower early death rates in population studies — supports olive oil as a plausible contributor, but it does not support olive oil as a cause of 122 years. Millions of people in Provence eat olive oil daily and do not live to 110, let alone 122.

The contradiction that made her famous is the rest of the diet: about two pounds, about one kilogram, of chocolate a week until she was about 119, port wine with meals, and cigarettes. She reportedly smoked from about age 21, introduced to cigarettes by her husband after meals, but, by several accounts, never smoked outside those meal times, and she quit at 117, when she could no longer see well enough to light a cigarette herself and refused help to do so out of pride, according to some retellings. She also enjoyed fried food and, in the nursing home, reportedly preferred it to the bland food served there.

That combination — olive oil and chocolate, wine and cigarettes, activity and indulgence — breaks every simple longevity rule. It is precisely why her story is misused: a reader who wants permission to smoke, drink, and eat chocolate will quote Calment, while a reader who wants a strict diet will quote only the olive oil. Both quotations are selective. The honest reading is that Calment's habits, taken together, did not follow a health plan. They followed pleasure, moderation in timing if not in quantity, and a life where she was not punished for indulgence by poverty, stress, or hard work. Her body, for reasons that may be largely genetic, tolerated habits that would harm most people, and her habits did not, on their own, create that tolerance.

## Activity Without Exercise: Cycling to 100, Fencing at 85

Calment was not, by modern standards, an exerciser. She did not go to a gym, follow a training plan, or count steps. She was, however, active for most of her life in the way people were active before gyms: she cycled until she was 100, took up fencing at 85 by some accounts (88 by others — the exact starting age varies in profiles, and should not be quoted as certain), played tennis, swam, and walked. In the nursing home, after moving there at 110, having lived alone until then, she did seated gymnastics in her armchair, wearing a headset, flexing and stretching hands and then legs, and nurses noted she moved faster than other residents almost 30 years younger.

That pattern — lifelong, moderate, enjoyable activity, rather than intense, short-term exercise — fits what population research finds about longevity: regular movement, maintained into old age, matters more than peak fitness in youth. Cycling to 100 is not a training achievement. It is a habit achievement: a person who cycles for transport and pleasure for 80 years will still be cycling at 100 if health allows, while a person who trains hard for five years and then stops will not. Calment's activity was also social and pleasurable, not punitive. She cycled, fenced, and played because she enjoyed them, not because a doctor ordered them, and enjoyment is what makes a habit last for decades.

Her medical history reinforces that point. At 20, incipient cataracts were found after conjunctivitis. At 115, she had a moderately weak heart, chronic cough, and rheumatism, but tests showed verbal memory and language fluency comparable to people in their eighties and nineties with the same education, even as other memory faded. She remained mentally sharp, by doctors' accounts, until near the end, though almost deaf and blind in her final years. She did not live 122 years in perfect health. She lived 122 years with accumulating, manageable problems, and with a body that, for a long time, compensated for them. That is a more realistic model of extreme age than “perfect health until 122.”

## The Apartment Deal at 90 and the Man Who Lost

At 90, Calment signed a viager deal — a French arrangement where a buyer pays a monthly sum for life in exchange for inheriting a property on death — with a lawyer, André-François Raffray, who agreed to pay her 2,500 francs a month for her apartment in Arles. Raffray was 47 at the time. He died in 1995, aged 77, having paid Calment more than double the apartment's value, and his widow continued payments until Calment's death in 1997. That deal, often told as a joke about a bad investment, is actually a useful illustration of longevity risk: a contract priced on average life expectancy loses money badly when the seller lives to 122.

The deal also shows Calment's practical, unsentimental side. She did not give her apartment away, nor did she sell it outright and risk outliving the money. She kept living in it, received a monthly income, and let the buyer take the risk. That arrangement gave her financial security in her nineties, hundreds, and hundreds-teens, without requiring her to move or to depend on family. Financial security, in turn, reduced stress, allowed good food, good care, and independence until 110, and removed a major cause of ill health in old age: worry about money. Longevity research consistently finds that financial security, social support, and a sense of control predict better health in old age, even when diet and exercise are held constant.

Her personal losses, however, were severe. She outlived her husband Fernand, who died in 1942, her daughter Yvonne, who died in 1934, and her grandson Frédéric, who died in 1963. A woman who lives to 122 will, by definition, outlive almost everyone she loves, and Calment's humour and calm attitude, which she credited for her longevity, must be understood against that background. Humour, in her case, was not a light personality trait. It was a coping strategy for grief, developed over decades, that allowed her to keep engaging with visitors, journalists, and nursing home staff, rather than withdrawing. Social engagement, in turn, is one of the strongest predictors of cognitive health in old age, and Calment, famous from 111 onwards, had more visitors, letters — over 5,000 birthday cards on her 120th birthday by one account — and conversation than most centenarians.

## The Age Dispute: Is 122 Really 122?

Calment's record has been challenged. Some researchers, notably in a 2018 paper by Russian mathematician Nikolay Zak and others, argued that Jeanne Calment died in 1934 and her daughter Yvonne assumed her identity to avoid inheritance tax, making the “122-year-old” actually Yvonne, who would have been 99 at death in 1997. That theory was widely reported, but it has been examined by French demographers, by the team that originally validated Calment's age, and by subsequent research, which found the identity-switch theory inconsistent with civil records, photographs, witness testimony, and the detailed life history Calment gave in interviews, including memories that only Jeanne, not Yvonne, could have had.

Guinness World Records and the Gerontology Research Group continue to recognise Calment as the oldest verified person, at 122 years and 164 days, based on birth certificate, civil records, and corroborating documents. The dispute should be reported, because a reader who searches for Calment will find it, but it should not be presented as equal to the validation. The validation rests on multiple, independent records over a lifetime, while the challenge rests on a statistical argument about the improbability of 122 and on reinterpreted photographs, without a document proving a switch. Improbability is not impossibility, and a record that is improbable is exactly what a world record is: the extreme end of a distribution, not the average.

That dispute also teaches a broader lesson about extreme age claims. Many claims of 130, 140, or 150 years, from regions without birth registration, fail validation because they lack documents. Calment passes validation because France in 1875 had civil registration, and because Arles kept records that can be checked. The secret, in that sense, is not only in her body. It is in her paperwork: a life documented from birth to death, in a country that recorded births, marriages, and deaths systematically, allows a record to be trusted. Without that paperwork, even a true 122-year life could not be verified, and with it, a false claim is hard to sustain.

## What Science Actually Says About Living to 122

Population research on supercentenarians — people over 110 — finds that they are not, as a group, perfect eaters, non-smokers, or athletes. Many smoked, drank, ate fatty foods, and did little formal exercise, as Calment did. What they share, more consistently, is genetics that protect against major diseases, a life with relatively low chronic stress, social engagement, and a body that ages more slowly in key systems, especially cardiovascular and immune function. In other words, supercentenarians are often people whose bodies are unusually resilient, not people whose habits are unusually perfect. Habits matter, but they matter within a genetic range, and Calment's range appears to have been unusually wide.

That conclusion is disappointing for anyone seeking a recipe, but it is honest, and it has practical implications. A person cannot change their genes, but can change the factors that, in population studies, shift average lifespan by years, even if they do not produce 122: not smoking, moderate alcohol or none, a Mediterranean-style diet rich in vegetables, fish, and olive oil, regular moderate activity, good sleep, social connection, and financial and emotional security in old age. Those factors will not make most people live to 122, and claiming they will is dishonest. They will, on average, make people live longer and healthier than they otherwise would, and that average gain, across millions of people, is more valuable than one record.

Calment's own words, as quoted by The Guardian and other profiles, capture that balance: “I took pleasure when I could. I acted clearly and morally and without regret. I'm very lucky.” Pleasure, clarity, morality, and luck — in that order, pleasure first, luck last — is a better summary than any diet list. Pleasure kept her engaged, clarity kept her decisions simple, morality kept her relationships intact, and luck, which she acknowledged, did the rest. A longevity secret that includes luck is not a marketing slogan, but it is a truthful one, and truthful advice is more useful than a slogan that promises 122 years for the price of olive oil.

## My Take:

Calment's story is misused every time it is reduced to a single food, and olive oil is the usual victim. Olive oil did not make her live to 122. If it had, Provence would be full of 122-year-olds, and it is not. What olive oil did, plausibly, was contribute to a diet that did not actively harm her heart, in a life where her heart was already genetically protected, where she was not stressed by poverty, where she moved daily, and where she laughed often. That is a chain, not a cause, and chains are harder to sell than bottles, which is why bottles get sold. A reader who buys olive oil expecting 122 years will be disappointed. A reader who cooks with olive oil, eats vegetables and fish, walks daily, and keeps friends, will be healthier, even if they live to a normal, not record, age. That second outcome is the real prize, and it does not need a record to be worthwhile.

I think her cigarettes and chocolate are the most important part, not because they should be copied, but because they prove that longevity is not a morality test. Calment smoked, drank port, ate two pounds of chocolate a week, and lived to 122, while many people who never smoked, never drank, and ate perfectly die at 70. That does not mean smoking is safe. It means a single person's habits do not prove a rule, and a rule does not predict a single person. Population data, not anecdotes, should guide health choices, and population data is clear: smoking shortens life on average, even if one smoker lives long. Quoting Calment to justify smoking is like quoting a lottery winner to justify buying tickets: it confuses an outlier with a strategy. The outlier is interesting precisely because she beat the odds, not because she shows the odds are wrong.

Her humour and calm attitude deserve more weight than her diet, in my view, because they are the factors most people can actually practise, regardless of genes. Calment was famous for witty replies in her hundreds, for laughing at her own deafness and blindness, and for treating visitors with charm rather than complaint. That humour was not a personality accident. It was, I believe, a skill she had practised for decades, through grief for her husband, daughter, and grandson, through war, through loss of sight and hearing, and through the strange experience of becoming famous for simply staying alive. Humour, in old age, is not denial. It is a way to stay socially engaged when your body is failing, and social engagement is one of the few factors that consistently predicts cognitive health. A person who can laugh with a nurse, a visitor, or a grandchild is a person who is still connected, and connection keeps the brain active in a way that puzzles and supplements do not.

The apartment deal at 90 is, for me, a quiet masterclass in planning for old age. Calment did not assume she would die at 90, 95, or 100. She made a deal that paid her for life, kept her in her home, and gave her monthly income without work. That deal worked because she lived long, but it would also have worked if she had died at 92, because it gave her security for whatever time remained. Too many people plan for old age as if it were a short, predictable period, and then run out of money, housing, or support when they live longer than expected. Calment's deal, and her independence until 110, show the opposite planning: assume you might live a long time, keep your home if you can, secure a monthly income, and keep social ties, so that extra years are a gift, not a crisis. That planning does not require wealth. It requires thinking about housing, income, and company in advance, while you can still choose.

Finally, the age dispute should not make readers cynical about all records, but it should make them demand documents. Calment's record survives because France kept birth, marriage, and death records that can be checked, and because her life was witnessed by many people over many decades. Claims without such records, however sincerely believed, cannot be verified, and should not be ranked above verified records. That standard protects both the record holder and the reader: it prevents a false claim from displacing a true one, and it prevents a true life from being dismissed because it seems improbable. Calment's life was improbable. That is why it is a record. Improbable, documented, and still, after all the chocolate, wine, cigarettes, olive oil, cycling, fencing, humour, and luck, not a recipe. It is a life, lived fully, for 122 years and 164 days, and the secret, if there is one, is that she lived it for pleasure, with clarity, without regret, and with the luck to be born into a family, a place, and a body that allowed her to keep living it longer than anyone else ever has.

There is also a lesson in how Calment handled being famous for age alone, which most supercentenarians never experience at her scale. From 111 onwards, she received journalists, researchers, and well-wishers, and she answered them with wit rather than complaint, even when questions repeated. That social role gave her days structure, conversation, and a reason to stay mentally active, which may have helped her cognition in a way that isolation would not have. It also shows a risk for very old people in general: fame, or even frequent family visits that treat age as a spectacle, can be tiring, and Calment's ability to set boundaries — answering on her terms, with humour, and then resting — was itself a skill. Longevity advice that only lists foods misses this social skill entirely, yet it may matter as much as any nutrient for staying mentally present at 110 and beyond.

Finally, her record should change how we set expectations for our own ageing. Most people will not live to 122, and planning as if they will is unrealistic, but planning as if old age will be short is also a mistake, because average life expectancy keeps rising and many people now live 20 to 30 years after retirement. Calment's viager deal, her independence until 110, and her continued activity show a middle path: keep moving, keep learning, keep company, secure housing and income for a long horizon, and accept that some decline — hearing, sight, memory — will come even in a record life. A plan built for a long, imperfect old age, rather than for a perfect one or a short one, is the most useful thing a reader can take from 122 years, even if they live to 85.
