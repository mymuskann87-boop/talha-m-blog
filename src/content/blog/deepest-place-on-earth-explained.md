Stand on the highest mountain on Earth, and you are about five and a half miles above sea level. Now imagine a place deeper below the sea than that mountain is high, a crack in the ocean floor so deep that the mountain, dropped into it, would disappear entirely, with more than a mile of water still above its peak. That place is real. It lies in the western Pacific Ocean, it is called the Mariana Trench, and at its bottom is a valley called Challenger Deep, the deepest known point on Earth. This post explains where it is, how deep it is, how it formed, what the pressure is like down there, who has visited, and what lives in a darkness no sunlight has ever reached.

## Where The Trench Is, And How Big It Is

The Mariana Trench is a crescent-shaped trench in the floor of the western Pacific Ocean, near the Mariana Islands, a chain of small islands that gives the trench its name. It is enormous by any measure: more than 1,500 miles long, and about 43 miles wide on average, running in a great curve across the seafloor, like a scar left by forces far larger than anything on the surface. To picture its length, imagine a trench stretching from one end of a large country to the other, and then further, all of it underwater, all of it dark, and all of it far beyond the reach of ordinary ships, divers, or cables.

The trench is not a hole punched in the seafloor. It is a valley, with sloping sides that descend gradually, then more steeply, toward its floor, and the floor itself is not flat. It contains valleys within valleys, ridges, and depressions, mapped only in recent decades by sonar surveys from ships, which bounce sound off the seafloor and measure the time it takes to return. The deepest part of the trench, and the deepest point in any ocean, is Challenger Deep, a small, relatively flat depression near the southern end of the trench, whose depth has been measured, refined, and argued over for more than a century, as instruments improved.


## How Challenger Deep Got Its Name

Challenger Deep is named after the British survey ship HMS Challenger, whose expedition in the 1870s first sounded its depths, using weighted ropes lowered over the side, and found a depth far greater than anyone had expected. The name has stuck, through every remeasurement, as a reminder that the deepest place on Earth was found not by modern technology, but by sailors lowering ropes into dark water, and being astonished at how long the ropes kept going.

## How Deep Is Deep: Almost Seven Miles Down

The Mariana Trench is almost seven miles deep. More precisely, its greatest depth is just under 36,201 feet, measured in fathoms by early surveys and refined by later ones. Challenger Deep itself has been measured at 36,070 feet below the surface, a figure that places it nearly seven miles beneath the waves, and more than a mile deeper than Mount Everest is tall. If Everest were placed in the trench, its summit would still be under more than a mile of water, invisible from the surface, in a darkness no sunlight reaches.

Numbers like these are hard to grasp, because human experience offers no comparison. Seven miles is a long drive, a distance you might cover in ten minutes by car, but vertically, downward, through water, it is a journey into conditions no human body can survive unprotected. The water above Challenger Deep weighs down with a force that would crush an unprotected person instantly, and the temperature is barely above freezing, and the light is absent entirely, below the first few hundred metres. Depth, in the ocean, is not only distance. It is pressure, cold, and darkness, increasing together, until the environment is more alien than the surface of the Moon.


## Why Depth Measurements Vary

Measurements have varied over the years, as instruments improved and techniques changed, and different surveys have produced slightly different figures for the exact greatest depth. That variation is normal in science: each generation measures more precisely than the last, and the "deepest point" is refined rather than overturned. What has not changed is the order of magnitude. Every measurement, from rope soundings to modern sonar, agrees that the Mariana Trench is the deepest place in the ocean, and that Challenger Deep is its deepest point, at a depth just under 36,201 feet.

## How The Trench Formed: One Plate Diving Under Another

The Mariana Trench was formed by subduction, one of the great processes that shape the Earth's surface. The Earth's outer shell is divided into tectonic plates, vast slabs of rock that move slowly over the hot interior, and where two plates meet, one may dive beneath the other, sinking into the mantle below. At the Mariana Trench, the Pacific Plate, a slab of dense oceanic crust, is diving beneath the smaller Mariana Plate, bending downward as it sinks, and the bend forms the trench: a long, curved valley where the seafloor plunges into the Earth.

Subduction is slow, measured in centimetres per year, but over millions of years, it moves plates thousands of kilometres, builds mountains, causes earthquakes, and recycles the ocean floor. The Pacific Plate is the largest tectonic plate on Earth, and it is being consumed, at its western edge, in the Mariana Trench and in other trenches around the Pacific, in a process that has been running for tens of millions of years. The trench is therefore not a permanent feature, in geological terms. It is a place where the Earth's crust is being destroyed, and where new crust, formed at mid-ocean ridges elsewhere, is eventually returned to the mantle.


## The Islands Beside The Trench

The same process that forms the trench also produces the Mariana Islands, the volcanic arc that runs alongside it, and the earthquakes that shake the region. As the diving plate sinks, it heats up, releases water and other substances, and causes melting in the rock above, which rises to form volcanoes. The islands are the surface expression of the subduction below, and the trench is the surface expression of the plate bending down. Together, they are one system: a plate diving, a trench forming, magma rising, and islands growing, all driven by the slow, unstoppable movement of the Earth's shell.

## Pressure: A Thousand Times The Surface, And More

The most extreme condition in Challenger Deep is pressure. At the surface, the air presses down on you with a force of about one atmosphere, the weight of the air above you. At the bottom of the Mariana Trench, the water above presses down with a force roughly a thousand times greater: about 1,000 times the atmospheric pressure at sea level, or, in everyday terms, the equivalent of about 50 jumbo jets pressing down on a single person. It is a pressure that would crush a car, flatten a submarine built for shallow water, and kill an unprotected human instantly.

That pressure is why visiting Challenger Deep is so difficult, and why so few people have done it. A vessel that descends there must be built as a pressure sphere, thick-walled and small, with no weak points, because a single flaw, at a thousand atmospheres, means instant destruction. The pressure does not merely squeeze. It seeks out every gap, every seal, every window, and tests it with a force no ordinary engineering can withstand. Building a craft for Challenger Deep is building a craft for an environment more hostile, in pressure terms, than the vacuum of space, where the difference is only one atmosphere, outward, rather than a thousand, inward.

Yet pressure, for the creatures that live there, is normal. Life in the trench has evolved under a thousand atmospheres for millions of years, and its bodies are built for it: soft, flexible, without air-filled cavities that would collapse, and with biochemistry that functions at pressures that would denature the proteins of surface animals. Bringing a trench creature to the surface, where pressure falls to one atmosphere, can kill it, just as taking a surface creature to the trench would kill that creature. Pressure is not an absolute enemy of life. It is a condition, and life adapts to the conditions it finds, however extreme they seem to us.

## The First Descent: Twenty Minutes At The Bottom

The first people to reach Challenger Deep did so in 1960, in a vessel called the Trieste, a bathyscaphe designed by Auguste Piccard and operated with the United States Navy. Aboard were Jacques Piccard, Auguste's son, and Don Walsh, a US Navy lieutenant. They descended for nearly five hours, through darkening water, into a darkness no sunlight had ever reached, and they spent about twenty minutes on the bottom, nearly seven miles down, before ascending. They saw, through a small window, a flat, pale seafloor, covered in fine sediment, and, famously, a small flatfish, a reminder that life reaches even here, though later accounts have questioned whether the creature they saw was truly a fish.

The Trieste's descent was a triumph of engineering and courage, and it remained, for more than half a century, the only crewed descent to Challenger Deep. The vessel itself was a strange craft: a large float filled with petrol, for buoyancy, slung beneath a small, thick-walled steel sphere that held the two men, with no room to stand, little room to move, and only a small window to see through. It was, in essence, a deep-sea elevator, built to survive pressure by being small, round, and immensely strong, and it worked, once, proving that humans could reach the deepest place on Earth and return alive.

That no one repeated the feat for 52 years says much about its difficulty and cost. The Trieste was retired, and no successor was built for decades, because the scientific return seemed small compared with the expense and risk, and because uncrewed vehicles could explore the deep sea more cheaply, if less dramatically. The trench waited, visited only by robots and remote instruments, while human attention turned to space, to the Moon, and to Mars, places that, in some ways, are easier to reach than the bottom of our own ocean.

## The Return: Cameron, And The Age Of Exploration

In 2012, the filmmaker and explorer James Cameron descended to Challenger Deep alone, in a submersible called the Deepsea Challenger, spending about three hours on the bottom, far longer than the Trieste's twenty minutes, and collecting samples and footage. Cameron's descent was a solo dive, in a vessel designed specifically for the purpose, small, green, and torpedo-shaped, built to withstand the pressure while giving its single occupant a view through a window and a set of cameras. It proved that crewed descent to the trench was possible again, with modern materials and engineering, and it renewed public interest in the deep sea.

Since then, exploration has accelerated. In 2015, the NOAA ship Okeanos Explorer surveyed the trench with advanced sonar and remote vehicles, mapping areas never before seen in detail, and discovering new features, new species, and new geological structures. Uncrewed vehicles, operated from ships on the surface by cable, now descend regularly, collecting samples, filming creatures, and measuring conditions, without risking human lives. The trench is no longer a place visited once in a lifetime. It is a place under active, if still limited, scientific study, and each expedition adds to a picture that, a generation ago, was almost entirely blank.

Yet even now, the trench is less explored than the surface of Mars. The deep sea is Earth's largest habitat, and the least known, and Challenger Deep, its deepest point, has been visited by fewer people than have walked on the Moon. That comparison is often made, and it is true in spirit, if not in exact numbers: the deep ocean is hard to reach, hard to see, and hard to work in, and our knowledge of it remains fragmentary, built from a small number of dives, surveys, and samples, spread over a vast, dark, three-dimensional world.

## What Lives In The Deepest Place On Earth

Despite the pressure, the cold, and the darkness, Challenger Deep is not lifeless. It hosts creatures adapted to its extremes: amphipods, small shrimp-like crustaceans, that scavenge the seafloor; holothurians, sea cucumbers, that feed on sediment; single-celled organisms called foraminifera, whose shells accumulate on the bottom; and bacteria that form the base of a food web with no sunlight at all. The food that sustains them comes from above: dead plants and animals, faecal matter, and organic debris, sinking slowly from the sunlit surface, in a rain of particles called marine snow, that feeds the deep, grain by grain, over months and years.

The creatures of the trench are small, slow, and soft, built for pressure and scarcity. They grow slowly, reproduce slowly, and live long, because energy is scarce and conditions are stable. Some are found nowhere else on Earth, having evolved in isolation in the trench's unique environment. Others are relatives of species found in other deep trenches, connected by the slow circulation of deep water that carries larvae and nutrients between them. The trench is not a desert. It is a sparse, cold, dark ecosystem, functioning on a trickle of energy from above, and sustaining life that, to surface eyes, seems impossible, yet persists, generation after generation, in the deepest place on Earth.

Studying that life matters beyond curiosity. Trench organisms produce chemicals and proteins that function under extreme pressure, and those substances may have uses in medicine, industry, and biotechnology. Their ecology teaches us about the limits of life, and about how life might exist in extreme environments elsewhere, in the subsurface oceans of Europa or Enceladus, where pressure, cold, and darkness may be similar. The deepest place on Earth is therefore not only a geographical extreme. It is a laboratory for understanding life itself, and what it needs, and what it can endure, when every condition seems against it.

The Mariana Trench is a crescent-shaped valley in the western Pacific, more than 1,500 miles long and about 43 miles wide, formed by the Pacific Plate diving beneath the Mariana Plate, and at its southern end lies Challenger Deep, just under 36,201 feet down, the deepest known point on Earth. Its pressure is about a thousand times that at the surface, its darkness is total, and its cold is near freezing, yet life persists there, fed by marine snow from above, and humans have visited it, first in 1960 for twenty minutes, and again in 2012 for three hours, with robots now exploring it regularly. It is a place that reminds us how much of our own planet remains unknown, and how much life can endure, in conditions we once thought impossible.
