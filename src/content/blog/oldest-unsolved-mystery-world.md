Calling something the oldest unsolved mystery in the world is a bold claim, because old mysteries come in different kinds, and the oldest depends on how you define mystery, evidence, and solved. Is it the oldest crime with no known perpetrator, the oldest text no one can read, the oldest disappearance, or the oldest scientific puzzle? Each category has a different winner, and each winner carries a different kind of uncertainty. This article explores the leading contenders, explains why they remain unsolved, and asks what solved would even mean for puzzles that are thousands of years old, where witnesses are gone, evidence is fragmentary, and the original context may be lost forever.

The value of these ancient puzzles is not only in their age. It is in what they reveal about the limits of knowledge. Modern science can read DNA, date objects, and scan beneath the earth, yet some questions resist every method, because the evidence needed to answer them no longer exists, or because the question itself was framed by people whose worldview we can only partly reconstruct. The oldest unsolved mystery, whatever it is, teaches humility as well as curiosity.

## The Voynich Manuscript: A Book No One Can Read

The Voynich manuscript is often cited as one of the world's most famous unsolved puzzles, though it is not the oldest by calendar age. It is a handwritten book, dated by radiocarbon analysis of its parchment to the early fifteenth century, filled with illustrations of plants, astronomical diagrams, and human figures, written in a script and language that no one has convincingly deciphered. The manuscript is held at Yale University's Beinecke Library, and its pages have been studied by cryptographers, linguists, historians, and computer scientists for over a century, without an agreed translation.

The mystery has several layers. First, the language itself is unknown: it may be a natural language written in an invented script, a cipher for a known language, an artificial language, or, as some researchers have suggested, a sophisticated hoax designed to look meaningful. Statistical analysis shows patterns that resemble natural language, such as word frequencies and structures, but those patterns have not led to a readable text. Second, the illustrations are puzzling: many plants do not match known species, astronomical diagrams do not follow familiar conventions, and the sections of the book do not clearly correspond to a known genre such as a herbal, a medical text, or an astrological manual.

Many claimed solutions have been announced, especially in recent years, and most have been rejected by specialists because they translate only a few words, rely on selective readings, or fail to produce a coherent text across many pages. A convincing solution would need to explain the whole manuscript consistently, account for its illustrations, and survive peer review, not merely produce a plausible phrase from a single page. Until that standard is met, the Voynich manuscript remains unsolved in the strong sense: we do not know what it says, who wrote it, or why.

## Undeciphered Scripts: Voices We Cannot Yet Hear

Older than the Voynich manuscript are undeciphered ancient scripts, which represent a different kind of mystery: whole writing systems whose sounds and meanings remain unknown. The most famous example is Linear A, a script used by the Minoan civilisation on Crete in the second millennium BCE, which has resisted decipherment despite decades of work, partly because the surviving texts are short, administrative, and lack a bilingual key such as the Rosetta Stone that helped decipher Egyptian hieroglyphs. Another example is the Indus script, used in the Indus Valley civilisation in the third millennium BCE, whose short inscriptions on seals and tablets have not been convincingly read, and whose language affiliation remains debated.

These scripts matter because they are not puzzles for their own sake. They are the written voices of civilisations, containing records of trade, religion, administration, and daily life that we cannot yet hear. Deciphering a script requires enough text, a hypothesis about the underlying language, and, ideally, a bilingual inscription or a known related language. When those conditions are absent, even brilliant analysis may stall, because many different readings can fit a small set of short inscriptions. The Indus script debate illustrates this: some scholars argue it is a full writing system for a language, others argue it may be a system of symbols rather than a language in the modern sense, and the evidence is too limited to settle the question conclusively.

Other undeciphered or partly deciphered systems include the Rongorongo script of Easter Island, the Cypro-Minoan syllabary, and various Bronze Age and early historic scripts known from only a handful of inscriptions. Each new discovery, such as a longer text or a bilingual fragment, could change the picture, which is why archaeologists treat undeciphered scripts as open problems rather than as permanent impossibilities. The oldest unsolved mystery, if defined as the oldest unreadable writing, may therefore be a script from the third millennium BCE, older than any single book, crime, or disappearance with a known date.

## Ancient Disappearances and Lost Peoples

Ancient disappearances form another category of old mystery, where a people, a city, or an expedition vanishes from the record without a clear explanation. The collapse of the Indus Valley civilisation, the abandonment of major Maya cities, and the disappearance of the Roanoke colony in the late sixteenth century are all examples, though they differ greatly in age and evidence. The Roanoke case, often called the Lost Colony, involves English settlers who vanished from Roanoke Island by 1590, leaving only the word Croatoan carved into a post, and it remains unsolved in the sense that no single, fully evidenced account explains what happened to every settler, though historians now favour explanations involving integration with Indigenous peoples, relocation, and hardship, rather than a single dramatic event.

Older disappearances are harder to define, because the record itself is fragmentary. A city abandoned over decades due to climate change, trade shifts, and political change does not vanish in a single night, and calling it a mystery can mislead readers into expecting a single cause. Archaeologists increasingly explain collapses as processes, not events, involving drought, deforestation, warfare, disease, and migration over generations. The mystery, in those cases, is not where the people went in a literal sense, but how multiple pressures combined to transform a society, and why some societies adapted while others did not.

The Sea Peoples, groups who appear in Egyptian and Near Eastern records of the late Bronze Age as invaders and migrants, are another example of an ancient mystery that is partly a problem of sources. Egyptian inscriptions name several groups, but their origins, languages, and ultimate fates are debated, and the records were written by their opponents, not by the peoples themselves. That bias is a general problem for ancient mysteries: we often have only one side's account, written for political purposes, and the other side left no texts. Solving such a mystery may require archaeology, climate science, and linguistics together, and even then the answer may be a complex, multi-causal account rather than a single revelation.

## The Oldest Crimes: Murder Without a Witness

If oldest unsolved mystery means oldest unsolved crime, the candidates become even older and more fragmentary. Human remains from prehistory sometimes show signs of violence, such as fractures or embedded weapons, but determining whether a death was murder, warfare, accident, or ritual is often impossible from bones alone, and there is no written record, no suspect, and no trial. Those cases are unsolved in a trivial sense, because they were never investigated in a modern sense, and calling them mysteries may stretch the term too far. A more meaningful category is historical crimes with a record but no resolution, such as assassinations, poisonings, and disappearances from antiquity and the medieval period, where sources exist but are contradictory, biased, or incomplete.

Examples often discussed include the fate of the Princes in the Tower, Edward V and his brother Richard, who disappeared from the Tower of London in 1483 and whose remains have never been conclusively identified, despite the discovery of bones in the Tower in the seventeenth century and repeated historical debate. Another is the death of Alexander the Great in 323 BCE, where ancient sources offer different accounts involving fever, poisoning, and other causes, and modern retrospective diagnosis cannot settle the question without the body and without reliable medical records. These are old mysteries in the sense that they have been debated for centuries, but they are not crimes in the modern forensic sense, because the evidence standards of their times were entirely different.

The lesson from old crimes is about evidence decay. Witnesses die, documents are lost or forged, bodies decompose or are moved, and political interests shape what is recorded. A mystery that is unsolved after five hundred years is unlikely to be solved by a single new document, though new methods, such as DNA analysis of remains, can sometimes narrow possibilities. The Princes in the Tower case, for example, could in principle be advanced by DNA testing of remains, but access, permission, and the condition of the bones present obstacles, and even a DNA result would need historical context to be interpreted. Age alone does not make a mystery more important, but it does make it harder, because every year removes evidence that will never return.

## Scientific Mysteries Older Than History

Some of the oldest unsolved mysteries are not human crimes or texts at all, but questions about the natural world that humans have asked for millennia. What caused the mass extinctions of the past, how life began, and what dark matter is, are all ancient in the sense that the phenomena are old, even though the questions were framed in modern scientific terms only recently. Those puzzles are unsolved in a different way: they await new data, new instruments, or new theories, and progress is often incremental rather than dramatic. A mystery that has lasted thousands of years in human curiosity may be solved by a future discovery, or it may be reframed as the wrong question entirely.

The origin of the universe, the nature of consciousness, and the existence of other life are examples of questions that have been asked in religious, philosophical, and scientific forms for centuries, and that remain open despite enormous progress. Their age reflects the depth of the question, not the failure of inquiry, because each generation refines the question and rules out some answers, even without reaching a final one. That kind of unsolved mystery is valuable because it drives research, even when it does not yield a neat solution, and it reminds us that not knowing is a normal state of science, not a scandal.

It is worth distinguishing these scientific mysteries from historical puzzles. A scientific mystery can be solved by a future experiment or observation that no one today can perform, while a historical mystery may be permanently unsolvable because the evidence is gone. The Voynich manuscript could, in principle, be solved by a future decipherment, while the exact thoughts of a Bronze Age scribe may never be recoverable. That difference matters for expectations: some old mysteries are open because we have not yet found the key, and others are open because the door itself no longer exists.

## Why Some Mysteries May Never Be Solved

Several factors make permanent insolubility likely for the oldest mysteries. The first is evidence loss: texts burn, bones decay, buildings collapse, and oral traditions change with retelling. The second is context loss: even when an object survives, the knowledge needed to interpret it, such as a language, a ritual, or a technology, may have vanished with its users. The third is source bias: surviving accounts were written by winners, elites, or outsiders, and they omit or distort the perspectives that would explain a mystery. The fourth is question framing: a mystery posed in modern terms, such as who killed a medieval prince, may not map onto the categories people of the time used, making a modern answer impossible without anachronism.

That does not mean inquiry is pointless. Studying an unsolved mystery often yields valuable knowledge even without a final answer: new dating methods, new understanding of a civilisation, new appreciation of a script's structure, or new awareness of how sources bias our view. The Voynich manuscript, for example, has advanced the study of medieval manuscripts, cryptography, and statistical linguistics, even though it remains unread. The Indus script debate has advanced archaeology and linguistics, even though it remains undeciphered. Progress in understanding a mystery is itself a form of partial solution, and it should be recognised as such.

For readers, the practical lesson is to judge claims about old mysteries by their evidence standard. A solution that explains a few details, ignores contradictions, and cannot be tested by others is not a solution, however exciting the headline. A solution that accounts for most evidence, survives peer review, and predicts new findings is worth taking seriously, even if it is less dramatic. The oldest mysteries attract the most sensational claims precisely because they are old and open, and scepticism is therefore not cynicism. It is the discipline that keeps curiosity honest.

## My Take:
My view is that the search for the oldest unsolved mystery says more about us than about the past, because we want a single winner, a single age, and a single answer, while the past offers categories, fragments, and processes. I think that desire for a neat ranking is understandable, but it misleads us. The Voynich manuscript, Linear A, the Indus script, the Princes in the Tower, and the collapse of ancient cities are not competing for the same prize. They are different kinds of unknown, and each teaches a different lesson about evidence, language, power, and time. Ranking them by age alone flattens those lessons into a trivia question, when the real interest lies in why each remains unknown and what that unknown reveals about how knowledge survives.

I also think we should be more comfortable with permanent uncertainty, especially for the oldest questions. Modern culture treats unsolved as a temporary state, as if every puzzle must have a solution waiting for a clever person to find it. For ancient mysteries, that assumption is often false, because the evidence needed for a solution may have burned, decayed, or never been written down. Accepting that some questions will remain open is not giving up. It is recognising the difference between a puzzle with a missing piece and a puzzle whose pieces no longer exist. That recognition frees us to value partial knowledge: a better dating, a clearer context, a ruled-out theory, or a new method, all of which advance understanding even without a final answer.

There is a humility lesson here that I find personally useful. When I read a confident claim that an ancient script has been deciphered, a lost colony explained, or a medieval murder solved, I now ask three questions: what is the evidence, what does it not explain, and who has checked it? If the claim answers only the first question, I treat it as interesting but unproven. If it answers all three, I take it seriously. That habit protects me from sensational headlines, and it also protects the people and cultures behind these mysteries from being reduced to a twist in someone else's story. Ancient peoples were not puzzles designed for our entertainment. They were communities with lives, languages, and losses that deserve respectful, careful study.

Finally, I believe the oldest unsolved mysteries should inspire curiosity without demanding closure. A child who learns about the Voynich manuscript, an undeciphered script, or a lost city may become a linguist, an archaeologist, or a historian, not because they expect to solve the mystery single-handedly, but because the mystery shows that knowledge is still being made. That is a healthier inspiration than the promise of instant fame for a claimed solution. The past will keep some of its secrets, and that is not a failure. It is a reminder that history is not a finished book waiting to be read, but a partial record we must interpret with care, patience, and respect for what has been lost. The mystery endures, and so should the curiosity it inspires, tempered by honesty about its limits.

One more consideration is the ethics of studying ancient mysteries, particularly when they involve human remains, sacred objects, or the heritage of living communities. A mystery about a lost people or an undeciphered script is not only an academic puzzle. It touches the identity of descendants, the stewardship of cultural heritage, and the question of who has the right to interpret the past. Researchers today increasingly work with descendant communities, museums, and local authorities to ensure that investigation respects cultural protocols, returns findings to the communities concerned, and does not treat human remains or sacred items as mere curiosities. That ethical dimension does not make a mystery easier to solve, but it makes the attempt more legitimate, and it ensures that a solution, if found, benefits those whose history it concerns.

I also think the oldest mysteries offer a useful corrective to the idea that newer technology automatically solves old problems. Radiocarbon dating, DNA analysis, satellite imaging, and computer modelling have transformed archaeology and history, yet they have not deciphered Linear A, translated the Voynich manuscript, or explained every ancient collapse. Technology extends what we can observe, but it does not replace the need for historical context, linguistic knowledge, and careful interpretation. A new tool may rule out a theory or narrow a date range, which is real progress, but it rarely delivers a complete answer on its own. Recognising that limit helps us use technology wisely, as one part of a multidisciplinary effort, rather than expecting a single machine or algorithm to close a puzzle that has resisted centuries of human thought.

Finally, there is a personal reason I value these old mysteries: they remind us that our own time will one day be ancient, and that future historians will face similar gaps in understanding us. What will survive of our digital records, our languages, and our daily lives, and what will be lost when formats change, servers fail, or contexts are forgotten? That thought should encourage us to preserve records thoughtfully, to document languages and cultures, and to write clearly about our own world, not only for ourselves but for those who will one day try to understand it. The oldest unsolved mysteries are, in that sense, a mirror: they show us what happens when preservation fails, and they urge us to preserve better, so that fewer of today's stories become tomorrow's unsolved puzzles.

That preservation duty extends to ordinary records, not only to famous manuscripts or monuments. Family letters, local newspapers, community photographs, and everyday documents are the raw material from which future historians will reconstruct our time, and they are easily lost when houses are cleared, formats become obsolete, or archives lack funding. Supporting local archives, digitising important papers, and keeping copies in more than one place are small, practical acts with long consequences. If we do them well, future generations may still face mysteries, but they will face fewer needless ones, and they will have a richer record from which to understand the lives we lived.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is the oldest unsolved mystery in the world?**
There is no single agreed answer, because oldest depends on category: oldest undeciphered script, oldest unreadable book, oldest unsolved crime, or oldest scientific question each have different contenders. The Voynich manuscript, Linear A, and the Indus script are among the most cited, with the Indus script dating to the third millennium BCE.

**Has the Voynich manuscript been deciphered?**
No, despite many claimed solutions, no decipherment has gained specialist agreement or produced a coherent translation across the manuscript. A convincing solution would need to explain the whole text and illustrations consistently and survive peer review.

**What is the oldest undeciphered script?**
Candidates include the Indus script from the third millennium BCE and Linear A from the second millennium BCE, though dating and definition affect the ranking. Both lack a bilingual key and sufficient long texts for a conclusive decipherment, which is why they remain open.

**What happened to the Lost Colony of Roanoke?**
No single fully evidenced account explains every settler, though historians now favour explanations involving relocation, integration with Indigenous peoples, and hardship over a single dramatic event. The carved word Croatoan is a clue, not a complete explanation.

**Will ancient mysteries ever be solved?**
Some may be advanced by new discoveries, such as longer texts, bilingual fragments, or DNA analysis, while others may remain permanently unsolved because evidence and context have been lost. Partial progress, such as ruling out theories or improving dating, is still valuable.

**Why do people keep claiming to solve old mysteries?**
Old, open mysteries attract attention, and a claimed solution can gain media coverage even without peer review, especially when it explains only a few details. Readers should check whether a claim accounts for most evidence, survives specialist scrutiny, and predicts new findings.
