Somewhere out there, an army once assembled in total silence. It had millions of soldiers spread across more than 190 countries. It sat inside navy computers, hospital networks and ordinary home machines. The whole world knew it was there. The whole world waited to see what it would do. And then... it did almost nothing. No big attack. No final order. Just a giant, quiet mystery that experts still argue about today. That army was a computer worm called Conficker.

## What Conficker is and what it actually does

Conficker is a computer worm, which means it is a program that copies itself from machine to machine without needing a person to carry it. It first appeared in November 2008, and within months it had grown into one of the largest worm infections in history. Estimates of the total number of infected computers range from about 9 million to about 15 million, spread across more than 190 countries. Nobody knows the exact figure, which somehow fits this worm perfectly. Almost everything about Conficker is a range, a guess or a question mark.

Once Conficker landed on a computer, it did not smash anything or steal anything obvious. Instead, it settled in like a patient spy. It turned the machine into part of a botnet — a hidden network of infected computers that can all receive orders from one controller far away. Think of each infected computer as a soldier who has quietly joined an army and is now waiting for instructions. The worm also protected itself with real cleverness. It blocked security websites on the infected computer, so the owner could not easily reach antivirus help. It kept updating itself into newer, harder-to-remove versions. And instead of taking orders from one fixed address that police could simply shut down, it generated hundreds of fresh web addresses every single day and checked them for instructions. There was no single head to cut off. To me, the scariest part is that its owner may still be out there somewhere, never caught, never even named.

## How Conficker spreads, and how it can reach you

Conficker travelled by three roads, and each one teaches a simple lesson.

The first road was a flaw in Windows, known by the code name MS08-067. Microsoft had actually released a fix for this flaw about a month before Conficker appeared. Every computer whose owner had installed that update was safe. Every computer that had skipped it was a wide-open door. The worm simply walked through millions of those open doors all by itself, with no clicks and no mistakes needed from the user.

The second road was weak passwords. On office and home networks, computers often share folders with each other. Conficker tried to break into those shared folders by guessing passwords, working through lists of simple, common ones. If your network password was something easy, the worm got in the same way a thief gets in by trying the obvious hiding places for a spare key.

The third road was the humble USB stick. Windows had a feature called AutoRun that automatically ran programs from a plugged-in device. Conficker copied itself onto USB sticks, and when that stick was plugged into a clean computer, the worm jumped across. It even disguised itself in the little menu that popped up, so it could look like a normal, safe option.

So how can something like this reach an ordinary person today? The same three roads still exist in modern forms: software that never gets updated, weak or reused passwords, and removable drives passed from computer to computer. The worm is old. The doors it used are still built into human habits.

## The damage story: a worm inside armies and hospitals

Conficker did not only infect home computers. It found its way into some of the most serious networks on Earth. The French Navy was hit, and its systems had to be isolated while the infection was cleaned out. In the United Kingdom, the Ministry of Defence was infected, and it was reported at the time that even some Royal Navy warships were affected. Computers in the German military were infected too. British hospitals also suffered, with some having to isolate parts of their networks to stop the worm spreading further, which disrupted the normal running of their systems.

The security industry was so concerned that companies which normally compete with each other formed a special alliance, called the Conficker Working Group, just to fight this one worm. Microsoft offered a reward of 250,000 dollars for information leading to the creator's arrest. A quarter of a million dollars, for one name. That name has never been publicly announced. The creator of one of the biggest infections in history simply stayed invisible.

Then came the day everyone feared. Security experts noticed that the worm was set to change its behaviour on 1 April 2009, and newspapers around the world warned that this might be the day the giant sleeping army would finally receive its orders and attack. April the first came. And went. Quietly. The most feared activation in internet history turned out to be a whisper. The worm kept updating itself for a while, kept its army standing in silence — and never launched the one huge attack the world had braced for. Experts still debate what it was ever built for. Was it a weapon that was never fired? A business that got too big to use safely? A test? Nobody knows.

## How to protect yourself from worms like Conficker

The good news buried inside this strange story is that Conficker's three roads can all be blocked with ordinary habits.

**Install updates promptly.** Conficker's main door had already been fixed by Microsoft a month before the worm appeared. The only infected computers were the ones that had not installed the fix. When your phone, computer or apps offer a security update, it is not a decoration. It is a lock being repaired. Install it the same day if you can.

**Use strong passwords, especially on shared things.** The worm guessed weak passwords on shared network folders. Your Wi-Fi router, your shared home folders and your accounts all deserve long passwords that are not a name, a date or a simple word. A password made of several random words joined together is both strong and easy to remember.

**Be careful with USB sticks.** Conficker rode from computer to computer on borrowed memory sticks. Do not plug in USB drives from people you do not trust, and do not let strangers use yours. If your system asks whether to run something automatically from a plugged-in device, the safe answer is no.

**Keep an antivirus switched on and updated.** Conficker actively blocked security websites to stop people getting help, which shows how much worms fear good security software. A respected, updated antivirus can catch most known worms before they settle in.

**Back up your important files.** This worm did not destroy files, but many of its cousins do. A copy of your photos and documents on a separate drive or in cloud storage means no worm can ever truly take them from you.

## Common questions

### Is Conficker still around today?

Small numbers of very old, forgotten computers are still believed to carry it, because a machine that is never updated and never cleaned can stay infected for years. For a normal person using an updated computer, Conficker itself is not a realistic danger anymore. Its real importance today is the lesson it teaches about updates and passwords.

### Who created Conficker?

Nobody knows, at least not publicly. Microsoft offered a 250,000 dollar reward for information about the creator, and the security world investigated for years. The creator has never been publicly identified, which is one of the main reasons this worm remains such a famous mystery.

### What was Conficker built to do?

That is the unanswered question at the heart of the story. It built one of the largest botnets in history, armies like that are usually rented out for spam, theft or attacks, yet this one was never used for one huge strike. Experts still debate whether it was a weapon, a business or an experiment.

### Could a worm like Conficker spread today?

A worm using the exact same tricks would struggle, because modern systems update themselves automatically and old features like AutoRun have been made much safer. But a new worm that finds a fresh, unpatched flaw could still travel very fast at first. That is exactly why installing updates quickly matters so much.

### How would I know if a computer is infected?

Worms like Conficker are designed to be invisible, but warning signs can include security websites mysteriously refusing to open, antivirus software failing to update, and a computer feeling slow or behaving strangely on a network. If several of those signs appear together, run a full scan with trusted security software or ask a technician to check the machine.
