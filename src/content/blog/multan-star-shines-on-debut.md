Every great cricket story starts somewhere small. A street corner where the ball is a taped-up tennis ball. A dusty ground where the pitch is more dust than grass, and the stumps might be a stack of bricks or an old chair. A boy who stays out long after the call for dinner, because one more over never feels like enough.

For Arafat Minhas, that place is Multan.

Multan is not a city that whispers. It is the city of saints and shrines, of blue pottery and blazing summer heat, of sweet mangoes and streets that have watched history pass for thousands of years. Life there moves to its own proud rhythm. And like almost everywhere in Pakistan, cricket is not just a game in Multan. It is a language. It is spoken in every mohalla, every schoolyard, every empty plot where boys gather with a bat that has seen better days and dreams that have seen none yet.

From those grounds has come a young all-rounder who, this October, stepped onto the biggest stage his sport can offer — and, according to those who profiled him this week, took international cricket by storm.

## A City That Raises Fighters

To understand Arafat Minhas, it helps to understand where he comes from.

Multan sits in southern Punjab, close to the Chenab River, and it is one of the oldest continuously lived-in cities in South Asia. The shrines of great Sufi saints watch over it. The summer sun tests everyone who lives under it. People from Multan will tell you, usually with a smile, that their city makes you tough without ever asking your permission.

That toughness matters in cricket. The young players of Multan do not grow up on soft, perfect outfields. They grow up on hard grounds where the ball can bounce in surprising ways, where the heat drains you by noon, where you learn to value your wicket because nothing out there is given to you for free.

Cricket in such a place is not a hobby. It is an escape route, a pride point, a family ambition. When a boy from Multan does well, the whole street feels it. His success is shared like sweets after Friday prayers. Neighbours claim a small piece of it. Old coaches nod like they always knew. Tea stalls replay his highlights in conversation long after the television has moved on.

So when a son of this city pulls on the green shirt of Pakistan, he does not carry only his own hopes onto the field. He carries a whole city's.

## The Junior Stage Where He First Announced Himself

Before the senior team, before the television cameras and the roaring crowds, there is the long ladder of junior cricket. Every professional will tell you the same thing: thousands of talented boys start climbing that ladder. Very few reach the top.

Arafat Minhas first made his name on that ladder's most famous rung — the Under-19 World Cup, playing for Pakistan's junior side.

The Under-19 World Cup is a special tournament, and anyone who follows cricket closely knows why. It is where tomorrow's stars give the world its first real look at them. Scarcely a generation passes without that tournament introducing a teenager who goes on to define an era. Scouts watch it. Selectors watch it. Fans in cricket-mad countries watch it with one eye on the present and one eye firmly on the future.

It is also a pressure cooker. You are still a boy, really, but you are wearing your country's colours in a world tournament, with your name on the back of the shirt and your family's hopes stitched into it. Many fine young players freeze at that level. The occasion is simply too big, too soon.

Minhas did the opposite. In junior cricket's brightest shop window, he made people remember his name. That is how journeys like his begin — not with a headline in the senior game, but with a quiet, growing murmur among those who watch the young game closely: keep an eye on this one. The boy from Multan can play.

## What Kind of Player Is He?

Minhas is an all-rounder, and that word carries weight in cricket.

An all-rounder is a player who refuses to be only one thing. He contributes with the bat and with the ball. He gives his captain options, balance, and breathing room. When the top order fails, an all-rounder can rescue the innings. When the main bowlers are tired or the match needs a change of rhythm, an all-rounder can step in and turn the game.

Teams treasure such players because they are so hard to find. A genuine all-rounder is really two players folded into one shirt, and every era of Pakistan cricket has leaned on them — the match-winners who could change a game twice in the same afternoon, once in each skill.

For a young player, being an all-rounder is also a statement of character. It means double the work in training. Double the practice sessions. While others rest after batting practice, the all-rounder goes and bowls. While others finish their bowling spells, he picks up the bat again. The boys who survive that workload are usually the ones who were never going to let a small-city beginning become a small-career ceiling.

That is the player Multan produced: not a specialist in one narrow skill, but a cricketer built for the whole game.

## The Debut Every Young Cricketer Dreams About

Then came the moment the whole journey had been pointing towards: a one-day international debut for Pakistan, against Australia.

Pause on that sentence for a moment, because it deserves it. Australia. In the history of one-day cricket, few opponents carry more weight. Generations of Pakistani players have measured themselves against Australian teams, and the contest between the two nations has given the sport some of its most famous afternoons. For a young man playing his very first ODI, there is no gentle introduction, no easy first step. You walk out against one of the strongest cricketing nations on earth, and the world finds out what you are made of.

Most debutants, honestly, just want to survive it. Get through the day. Not make a mistake that lives forever on the internet. Walk off with your dignity and a story for your grandchildren.

Minhas, by every account of that match, did far more than survive it. His first appearance in Pakistan's one-day side was a match-winning performance — the kind of debut players dream about in the nets and rarely get in real life. The profile of him published this week describes that debut as a record debut, and describes the young all-rounder in glowing terms: a player who has taken international cricket by storm.

Think about what that means for the boy from the dusty grounds. A first match. A giant opponent. And instead of shrinking under the lights, he grew. The same city that watched him in junior cricket now watched him decide a game for his country at the very first attempt.

Debuts like that change how a career is talked about. Before that match, Arafat Minhas was a promising young name, one of many. After it, he became a story — the Multan boy who arrived ready.

## Why This Story Matters Beyond One Match

One match, of course, is only one match. Wise cricket people are always quick to say that, and they are right. A long career is built over years, through failures and comebacks, through bad tours and hard lessons. No sensible person crowns a young player after a single afternoon.

But the meaning of a debut like this goes deeper than selection debates, and it reaches far beyond the boundary rope.

It matters in Multan first of all. Somewhere in that city tonight there is a twelve-year-old bowling with a taped ball in a narrow street, stopping every few deliveries because a motorbike needs to pass. That boy has now seen proof — fresh, recent, undeniable proof — that the distance between his street and a Pakistan shirt is real but crossable. A boy from his own city did it. Not from a famous academy in a rich neighbourhood. From Multan. From grounds like his.

It matters for Pakistan cricket too. A team is always healthiest when its talent keeps arriving from every corner of the country, not just from a few big centres. When smaller cities keep producing international cricketers, it tells you the heartbeat of the game in that country is strong. The pipeline is alive. The dream is still being chased in places where chasing it is hardest.

And it matters because sport, at its best, is a reminder that beginnings do not decide endings. Your city does not decide your ceiling. Your ground does not decide your future. Work does, and patience, and the courage to take your chance on the day it finally arrives.

Personally, I find stories like this one better than any big-money transfer or record auction, because a hometown debut reminds every small-city kid that the distance between a dusty street and a national shirt is long — but it can be walked.

## What Happens Next for the Multan Boy?

The hardest part of a dream debut, strange as it sounds, is everything that comes after it.

Now the world knows his name. Opposition analysts will study him. Bowlers will plan for him. Every future innings and every future spell will be judged against the standard of that first magical match. The "promising youngster" label, which forgives mistakes, falls away quickly once you have won a game for your country. From here, he will be expected to do it again, and again.

That is not a burden unique to Minhas. It is the tax every exciting young player pays for a brilliant beginning. The truly special ones treat it as fuel. They go back to training, back to the unglamorous work, and they build a career brick by brick on top of the beautiful first day.

Pakistan fans, who love their heroes fiercely and loudly, will hope for exactly that: not just a debut to remember, but the start of a long, proud career. Multan will hope for it loudest of all. In that city, he is not just a cricketer now. He is proof.

Whatever comes next, one thing is already written into the story and cannot be taken away. A young all-rounder from the city of saints walked into international cricket against Australia, in the biggest moment of his young life, and he did not blink. He shone.

The star from Multan has risen. Now the whole cricket world gets to watch how brightly he burns.
