The tea had gone cold before the argument ended. Three friends, one table, three different recharge plans — and each man was certain his was the clever one. One paid for a mountain of data he never finished. One paid for unlimited calls he barely made. The third paid the least of all, and he was the quietest, because he had done one thing the others had not: he had looked at his own bills before choosing.

That is the whole secret of a good recharge plan. The shop poster shouts about the biggest bundle. Your bill whispers about your real life. Listen to the bill.

## What a recharge plan really sells you

A recharge plan is a bundle you buy for a fixed number of days. Inside it are usually three things: mobile data, call minutes, and text messages. Some plans add extras — a music app, a video app, a small insurance cover. The price looks simple. The value is not, because you only benefit from the parts you actually use.

A plan with 100 gigabytes means nothing if you use 12. A plan with unlimited calls means nothing if your calls last ten minutes a day. The unused part is not a bonus. It is money you gave away for nothing, and the shop will never remind you of it.

## Step one: read your real use, not your memory

Open the last two bills, or the usage screen in your operator's app, and write down three numbers: how much data you used each month, how many call minutes, and how many texts. Do this for two months, not one, because one month can lie — a travelling week, a family emergency, a holiday all bend the numbers.

Most people guess their data use wrong. They remember the one heavy day and forget the twenty light ones. The bill remembers everything. When you see the real numbers, the right plan often becomes obvious within a minute, and it is usually smaller than the one you are paying for now.

## Step two: compare plans by your use, not by the poster

Lay two or three plans side by side and check each against your numbers only. Does the data allowance cover your real use with a little room to spare? Do the call minutes match your calling habit? Ignore the parts you will never touch. A plan is not better because it contains more. It is better because it contains what you need and nothing expensive that you do not.

This is where the tea-table argument falls apart. The friend with the giant data plan was paying for a lake and drinking a glass. The friend with unlimited calls was paying for a river and sipping from a cup. The quiet friend had matched his plan to his life, and his life was the only measure that mattered.

## Step three: compare the daily cost

Here is the arithmetic that settles most arguments. Divide the plan price by the number of days it lasts. That gives the daily cost, and the daily cost is the honest price.

For example, a plan costing 300 for 28 days works out to about 10.7 per day. A plan costing 500 for 56 days works out to about 8.9 per day. The second plan looks more expensive on the poster, but it is cheaper every single day you use it. Smaller validity packs often cost more per day, which is exactly why shops push them — they look small and friendly while quietly charging more.

In my view, the daily cost is the only honest price on the whole poster, and every other number is decoration.

Always do this division before you pay. It takes ten seconds and it has saved more money than any other habit in this post.

## Step four: drop the add-ons you never use

Plans love to carry passengers: a streaming subscription you opened once, a gaming pack you forgot, a caller tune from two years ago. Each one is small. Together they are a second bill hiding inside the first.

Go through the plan details line by line and cancel anything you have not used in the last month. If you truly want a music or video app, buy it separately only after checking whether you use it enough to justify the price. An add-on you never open is not a feature. It is a leak with a friendly name.

## Step five: re-check every few months

Operators change their plans regularly, and your life changes too. A plan that was perfect when you worked from home may be wrong now that you travel daily. A new plan may appear that fits you better for less money. Set a small habit: every three or four months, repeat step one and step three. Ten minutes, four times a year, and your plan never quietly goes stale.

One caution before you switch: check the terms first. Some plans carry conditions — a minimum period, a fee for early change, a benefit that ends if you leave. Read those lines before you move, not after. A cheaper plan with a nasty condition can cost more than the expensive plan you left.

## The honest limits of a cheap plan

The cheapest plan is not always the best plan, and this post will not pretend otherwise. Network coverage matters more than price. A plan on a network that drops your calls in your own street is no bargain at any price. Ask neighbours and colleagues how the signal holds up where you live and work before you chase the lowest number.

Family needs matter too. A plan that fits one person may not fit a household sharing one connection or one bill. And some months are genuinely heavy — a new job, a sick relative, a child studying online. In a heavy month, the right plan is the one that covers you, not the one that wins a maths contest. The goal is the cheapest plan that fits your real life, not the cheapest plan on the wall.

## A small monthly routine that keeps the plan honest

A plan chosen once can drift out of shape quietly. Your habits change, the operator changes its offers, and the plan that was perfect in January may be wrong by June. The fix is a tiny monthly routine that takes five minutes and saves real money across a year.

On the first weekend of each month, open the usage screen and answer three questions. How much data did you actually use last month? Did the minutes run out or sit unused? Did any add-on charge appear that you do not recognise? Write the three answers in a small note on your phone, beside the plan's price and its daily cost.

After three months, that note becomes more useful than any poster in any shop. If data runs out every month, the plan is too small and the top-ups are eating the saving. If half the data remains every month, the plan is too big and you are paying for air. The note shows the pattern plainly, without memory or guesswork.

One more habit belongs in this routine: when you switch plans, keep the old plan's details until the new one has worked for a full cycle. A switch that fails halfway through a month — a wrong allowance, a missing benefit, a surprise condition — is worse than a slightly expensive plan that works. Patience for one month beats a hasty switch you regret.

## Common questions

### How do I check my real mobile data use?

Open your operator's app and look at the usage section, or read the last two bills. Write down the data, minutes, and texts for each month. Two months give a fairer picture than one, because a single unusual week can bend the numbers.

### Is the biggest bundle always the best value?

No. A bundle is only good value if you use what is inside it. Unused data and unmade calls are money spent for nothing. Compare plans against your real use, and let the daily cost decide between close options.

### How do I compare the daily cost of two plans?

Divide the plan price by the number of days it lasts. For example, 300 for 28 days is about 10.7 per day, while 500 for 56 days is about 8.9 per day. The lower daily cost wins, provided the plan covers your real use.

### Should I cancel plan add-ons I never use?

Yes. Streaming packs, gaming packs, and caller tunes you never open are small charges that add up every month. Cancel anything unused in the last month, and buy an app separately only if you truly use it enough.

### How often should I re-check my recharge plan?

Every three or four months is enough. Operators change plans, and your habits change too. A ten-minute check a few times a year keeps your plan matched to your life instead of quietly going stale.
