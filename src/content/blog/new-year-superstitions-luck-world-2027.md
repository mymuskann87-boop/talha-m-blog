At midnight on 31 December, millions of people do things that, looked at coldly, seem illogical: they eat a precise number of grapes, wear a particular colour of underwear, jump over waves, burn a paper figure, or refuse to take out the rubbish. These are New Year superstitions, and they survive not because people are foolish, but because the turn of the year feels uncertain and important. A new year brings hope and anxiety in equal measure, and a small ritual gives people a sense of agency. Eating, wearing, or doing something lucky does not guarantee a good year, but it marks the moment, focuses intention, and connects a person to family and cultural history.

Superstitions also work socially. When a whole family eats lentils together, or a whole square counts down and eats grapes in time with bells, the ritual creates shared experience. People laugh when they choke on a grape, argue about the correct colour to wear, or compete to be the first to step through a door. Those shared moments become memories, and the superstition becomes a reason to gather, even for people who do not literally believe that a grape controls fate. In that sense, luck rituals are less about magic and more about meaning: they turn an abstract date change into a concrete, embodied celebration.

This guide explores lucky New Year customs from around the world, explaining what people do, why the custom is thought to bring luck, and where it comes from. Some customs are widely practised, others are regional, and all vary by family. Treat them as cultural stories, not as guarantees. No ritual can replace planning, health care, or financial care, but understanding these traditions can enrich your own New Year, whether you adopt one, adapt one, or simply enjoy learning how other people welcome a new year.

## Spain, Latin America and the Twelve Grapes

In Spain, the most famous New Year custom is eating twelve grapes at midnight, one with each stroke of the clock. The grapes, traditionally a variety known as Aledo, are eaten in the final twelve seconds of the year, a challenge that requires speed, concentration, and often laughter. Each grape is said to represent a month of the coming year, so eating all twelve is thought to bring good luck for all twelve months. The custom is broadcast from Madrid's Puerta del Sol, where crowds gather, and television coverage helps families at home count along.

The tradition is often traced to the early twentieth century, when grape growers in the Alicante region promoted the custom after a surplus harvest, though similar grape-eating customs may have older roots. Whatever its commercial origin, the practice is now deeply embedded in Spanish New Year culture. Families buy special packs of twelve grapes, prepare them in advance, and practise the timing, especially with children who may be given smaller or seedless grapes. The ritual is social as well as symbolic: everyone eats together, counts together, and starts the year with a shared, slightly chaotic moment.

In many Latin American countries, variations of grape eating, lentil dishes, and coloured clothing also appear, often blended with local customs. In Mexico, for example, some people eat twelve grapes and make a wish with each one, while others focus on lentils, fireworks, or a special dinner. In Brazil, people traditionally wear white for peace and luck, and many gather on beaches, notably Copacabana in Rio de Janeiro, to watch fireworks and make offerings to the sea. These variations show how a core idea — using food and colour to invite luck — adapts to local climate, religion, and history, producing customs that feel both familiar and distinct.

## Italy, Lentils and Eating for Prosperity

In Italy, lentils are a classic New Year food because their small, round, coin-like shape is said to symbolise money and prosperity. Eating lentils, often served with cotechino or zampone, a type of pork sausage, on New Year's Eve or New Year's Day is thought to bring wealth in the coming year. The more lentils eaten, the more prosperity expected, according to the playful logic of the custom. The dish is hearty, warming, and well suited to winter, which helps explain why it has endured: it is both symbolic and satisfying as a meal.

The lentil custom is not unique to Italy. In many cultures, round foods, beans, and grains are associated with coins, abundance, and fertility, because they resemble money or because a full store of food once meant survival through winter. In the southern United States, for example, black-eyed peas and greens are traditional New Year foods, with peas said to represent coins and greens to represent paper money. In parts of Germany and Poland, carp, herring, or other fish may be eaten for luck, with fish scales sometimes kept in a wallet as a token of prosperity. These parallels show a common human pattern: people use the foods they have to imagine the wealth they hope for.

For a modern host, lentils offer a practical and inclusive New Year dish. They are inexpensive, easy to cook in large quantities, suitable for many diets when served without meat, and easy to adapt with spices, vegetables, and herbs. A lentil soup or stew can anchor a New Year's Eve buffet, and a small bowl served at midnight can become a ritual in itself. Whether or not you believe lentils bring wealth, sharing a warm, affordable dish with guests at the turn of the year is a generous way to start January, and it connects a contemporary party to a long line of similar meals eaten in hope.

## Colour, Clothing and First Footing

Colour is one of the most widespread New Year superstitions. In many Latin American countries, the colour of underwear worn on New Year's Eve is said to influence the coming year: red for love, yellow or gold for wealth, white for peace, and green for health, though associations vary by country and family. The custom is playful and private, but it is taken seriously enough that shops stock brightly coloured underwear in December, and families may gift a specific colour to a loved one. Wearing the colour is a small, personal act of intention, visible only to the wearer, which makes it feel intimate rather than performative.

In the Philippines, round shapes are considered lucky, because circles resemble coins. People may wear polka dots, fill bowls with round fruits, and scatter coins around the home. In China, red is a lucky colour associated with joy and protection, and red envelopes, decorations, and clothing appear at Lunar New Year, which follows a different calendar but shares the theme of welcoming luck through colour and ritual. These examples show that colour superstitions are not random: they draw on cultural associations built over centuries, where red may mean joy in one place and love in another, but always means something positive and intentional.

First footing is a Scottish and northern English custom with a specific rule: the first person to enter a home after midnight should bring good luck, especially if that person is a tall, dark-haired man carrying gifts such as coal, bread, salt, whisky, or a coin. These gifts symbolise warmth, food, flavour, prosperity, and cheer. The custom, known in Scotland as first footing, is linked to Hogmanay, the Scottish New Year celebration, and it reflects older beliefs about strangers, hospitality, and the importance of the first visitor. In modern practice, friends may arrange to be the first footer, and the custom becomes a reason to visit neighbours, share a drink, and wish each other well in the first minutes of the year.

## Fireworks, Noise and Driving Away Bad Luck

Fireworks are now a global New Year spectacle, but their origins as a luck ritual are older than modern displays. Loud noise was traditionally thought to scare away evil spirits and bad luck, a belief found in many cultures. Firecrackers in China, fireworks in Europe, and banging pots and pans in some communities all share this logic: make a loud, bright noise at the boundary between years, and the old year's troubles will leave with the sound. Modern city fireworks displays retain that drama, even when audiences no longer think in terms of spirits. The countdown, the burst of light, and the cheer of a crowd still feel like a collective clearing of the air.

In Denmark, a playful custom involves jumping off a chair at midnight, literally leaping into the new year, and throwing old plates at friends' doors for luck. The more broken plates outside a door, the more popular and lucky the household is said to be. In Ecuador and some other Latin American countries, people burn a scarecrow-like figure, known as Año Viejo, representing the old year, as a way of leaving behind its problems. In parts of Spain and Latin America, people may also burn lists of bad memories or wishes. These customs share a theme: the old year must be actively left behind, not merely waited out, and fire, noise, or jumping gives that leaving a physical form.

Safety must be part of any modern discussion of fireworks and fire rituals. Professional displays are far safer than home fireworks, and local laws vary widely, with some places banning private fireworks entirely. Burning figures or paper should only be done where it is legal and safe, away from buildings, dry vegetation, and crowds, with water nearby. A luck ritual that causes injury or fire brings the opposite of good fortune, so the wisest modern version of these customs is to watch a professional display, make noise with party horns or music, and symbolically leave the old year behind through writing, conversation, or a simple toast, rather than through risky fire.

## Food, Drink and Household Rituals for the New Year

Beyond grapes and lentils, many household rituals focus on the home itself. In some traditions, people clean the house thoroughly before New Year's Eve, to start the year fresh, but avoid sweeping or taking out rubbish on New Year's Day, for fear of sweeping away luck. In other traditions, the first action of the year matters: opening a door to let the new year in, eating a particular food first, or avoiding arguments on 1 January, because the first day is thought to set the tone for the whole year. These rules vary, and families often follow only the ones they grew up with, but they share a common idea: beginnings matter, and the way a year starts deserves attention.

Food rituals also include cakes and breads with hidden objects. In Greece, a Vasilopita cake, cut on New Year's Day, may contain a coin, and the person who finds it is said to have luck for the year. Similar hidden-coin cakes appear in other cultures, and the principle is the same as the Swedish almond in rice pudding at Christmas: a small surprise in shared food creates excitement and a story. In some places, people eat a particular number of dishes, or avoid certain foods on New Year's Day, such as chicken, because a chicken scratches backward and might symbolise moving backward in the new year. These food rules are not universal, but they show how everyday eating becomes symbolic at a boundary moment.

Drinks also play a role. Toasting at midnight with sparkling wine, champagne, or a local festive drink is a near-universal New Year act, and the toast itself — a spoken wish for health, happiness, or prosperity — is the ritual, not just the drink. In some cultures, people throw a glass or drop a coin into a drink for luck, though these customs should be adapted safely and hygienically. For people who do not drink alcohol, sparkling water, juice, or a festive mocktail serves the same social purpose: everyone raises a glass together, makes a wish aloud, and marks the moment. The shared toast, like the shared grape or lentil, turns a private hope into a communal celebration.

## Lucky Objects, Wallets and Everyday Tokens

Beyond food and clothing, many New Year luck customs involve small objects carried into the new year. In parts of Europe, fish scales kept in a wallet are said to attract money, because scales resemble coins and because fish were once a valuable food. In other places, a coin carried in a pocket, a new wallet received as a gift, or money placed in a red envelope is thought to ensure that money will not run out during the year. These tokens are rarely valuable in themselves; a single coin or scale costs almost nothing, but the act of carrying it deliberately, from the old year into the new, gives it meaning. The wallet becomes a small, private ritual object, checked and remembered throughout January.

Other tokens focus on the home rather than the person. A loaf of bread, a bottle of drink, or a piece of coal left by the door on New Year's Eve may symbolise that the household will not lack food, warmth, or cheer in the coming year, echoing the gifts brought by a first footer. In some traditions, people place a bay leaf, a sprig of greenery, or a small charm above a door for luck, or they hang a new calendar on New Year's Day as a sign of a fresh start. These home tokens are often made from ordinary materials, which is part of their appeal: luck is not bought, it is arranged from things already at hand, with intention and care.

For modern readers, these tokens offer a gentle, low-cost way to mark the new year without superstition pressure. Placing a coin in a new wallet, writing a wish on a card and keeping it in a drawer, or putting a sprig of greenery by the door can serve as a reminder of a goal or hope for 2027, even for people who do not believe the object itself brings luck. The value lies in the reminder: each time the wallet is opened or the door is passed, the person recalls what they hoped for at midnight. That daily reminder, repeated over weeks, may do more practical good than any magical claim, because it keeps a goal visible in ordinary life, where goals are usually forgotten.

## My Take:

I think New Year superstitions endure because they give people a way to act on hope when hope alone feels too vague. At the end of a difficult year, telling someone to simply be optimistic can feel empty. Telling them to eat twelve grapes, wear yellow, or welcome a first footer gives them something to do with their hands, their table, and their friends. That action does not change the economy, cure illness, or fix a broken relationship, but it does change the feeling of the moment: from passive waiting to active welcoming. I believe that feeling matters, especially at a time when many people feel that large events are beyond their control. A small ritual restores a small sense of control, and that is a humane and useful thing, even for people who do not believe in magic.

My strongest view is that we should not mock superstitions, even when their origins are commercial or their logic is playful. The Spanish grape custom may have been promoted by growers, the fried chicken Christmas in Japan may have begun as marketing, and coloured underwear may be sold as a seasonal product. None of that makes the resulting family memory less real. Traditions are not validated by ancient origins; they are validated by repetition, affection, and meaning. A custom that a family has practised for thirty years, laughed about, and passed to children is a tradition, regardless of how it started. Mocking it as silly misses the social work it does: gathering people, creating stories, and giving a year a memorable beginning.

I also believe that luck rituals reveal what different cultures value most. Lentils and coins reveal a concern with prosperity, especially in times when money was scarce and winter survival was uncertain. White clothing in Brazil reveals a longing for peace. First footing gifts of coal, bread, and salt reveal a concern with warmth, food, and flavour — the basics of a household. Red underwear reveals a hope for love. Reading superstitions this way turns them from curiosities into a kind of cultural autobiography: a list of what people, in a particular place and time, most wanted for the future. That reading is far more interesting than asking whether the ritual works, because it tells us about history, economy, and daily life, not just about belief.

Another point I feel strongly about is safety and adaptation. Older luck rituals involving fire, fireworks, and large crowds need modern updating, not blind repetition. A family can honour the idea of driving away bad luck with noise by watching a professional display, playing music, or cheering together, without handling dangerous fireworks. A household can honour the idea of leaving the old year behind by writing down what they want to release and recycling the paper, rather than burning a large figure unsafely. Adaptation is not betrayal. It is how traditions survive in new cities, new housing, and new laws. The families who adapt wisely are the ones whose customs will still be practised in fifty years, because they will not have been banned, injured, or abandoned as impractical.

I also think there is value in choosing one ritual deliberately, rather than collecting many at random. A person who eats grapes, wears yellow, jumps off a chair, burns a figure, and avoids sweeping, all in one night, may end up exhausted and confused, with no single moment to remember. A person who chooses one ritual that resonates — perhaps lentils with family, or a first footing visit to a neighbour, or a midnight toast with a spoken wish — will remember that moment clearly and may repeat it next year. That repetition is what turns a one-off party trick into a tradition. I would encourage readers to pick the custom that fits their household, their budget, and their beliefs, practise it with intention, and let it become theirs, rather than trying to perform every lucky custom in the world in a single night.

Finally, I believe the best New Year luck is not found in a grape or a coin, but in the gathering itself. The people who eat together, laugh at a mistimed grape, visit a neighbour at midnight, or share a lentil stew are already practising the things that make a year better: connection, generosity, humour, and attention to each other. Those qualities do not guarantee prosperity or health, but they do make hardship easier to bear and good fortune easier to share. If a superstition brings people together, it has already delivered a kind of luck, regardless of what happens in the months that follow. That, in my view, is the real reason these customs survive: not because they control fate, but because they remind us, at the exact moment a new year begins, that we do not face it alone.

I also think luck rituals can be a bridge between generations, in a way that more serious New Year resolutions rarely are. A grandparent who teaches a grandchild to eat grapes, wear a colour, or welcome a first footer is passing on a story, a laugh, and a shared moment, not just a rule. The child may not believe the ritual brings luck, but they will remember who taught it, where they were, and how it felt to count down together. That memory, carried into adulthood, becomes a tradition in its own right, even if the belief behind it fades. Resolutions, by contrast, are often private and quickly forgotten, while rituals are social and repeated. That social repetition is why superstitions outlast many well-intentioned plans, and why they deserve to be treated as cultural heritage, not as embarrassments.

There is also a need for honesty about limits. A luck ritual should never replace practical preparation for the new year: saving money, looking after health, maintaining relationships, and planning work or study. A person who eats lentils but ignores debts, or wears yellow but neglects health, is using ritual as avoidance, not as celebration. The healthiest approach is to enjoy the ritual and also do the practical work, with the ritual serving as a joyful marker, not a substitute. When ritual and responsibility are combined — a lentil meal shared with family, plus a simple budget plan for January, for example — the new year begins with both hope and realism, which is a far stronger combination than either alone. Hope without action is wishful; action without hope is grim; together, they make a year worth starting.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What are the twelve grapes at New Year in Spain?**
In Spain, people eat twelve grapes at midnight, one with each clock stroke, with each grape representing a month of the coming year. Eating all twelve is thought to bring good luck for the whole year.

**Why are lentils eaten for luck at New Year?**
Lentils resemble small coins, so eating them is thought to symbolise wealth and prosperity. In Italy they are often served with pork sausage on New Year's Eve or New Year's Day.

**What is first footing in Scotland?**
First footing is the custom where the first person to enter a home after midnight brings luck, especially if they carry gifts such as coal, bread, salt, whisky, or a coin symbolising warmth, food, and prosperity.

**Why do people wear coloured underwear at New Year?**
In many Latin American countries, underwear colour is linked to wishes for the new year, such as red for love, yellow for wealth, and white for peace, though associations vary by country and family.

**Are New Year fireworks originally about luck?**
Yes, loud noise and bright light were traditionally thought to scare away evil spirits and bad luck at the turn of the year. Modern displays retain that drama, but professional shows are the safest way to enjoy them.

**Can I create my own New Year luck ritual?**
Yes, choose one ritual that fits your household and beliefs, practise it with intention, and repeat it yearly. A shared meal, a midnight toast with a spoken wish, or a visit to a neighbour can become a meaningful family tradition.
