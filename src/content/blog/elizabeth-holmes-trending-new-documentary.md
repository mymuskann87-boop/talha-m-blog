She is sitting in a Texas prison, years into an eleven-year sentence, with no company, no fortune and no freedom. And yet this week, Elizabeth Holmes is suddenly everywhere again — trending on social media, back in the headlines, her face once more attached to the most famous fraud story Silicon Valley has ever produced.

The reason is a film. A strange, intimate new documentary, shot in the last days before she walked into prison, has put the woman who fooled the world's smartest investors back in front of the world's cameras. Here is the full story — the rise, the lie, the fall, and the film that has revived it all.

## The documentary everyone is talking about

The film is called **"You Can See Everything"**, made by Nathan Fielder and Lance Oppenheim, and it opens on **Friday 16 October**. It runs nearly three hours, and its access was extraordinary: Holmes and her husband, Billy Evans, allowed a camera crew into their home during the **final 34 days before she reported to prison** — and the crew kept following the couple for about three years.

The film surprised audiences when it was shown at the Telluride Film Festival on 6 September, and a clip from it went viral this week, which is how a prisoner with no phone and no social media ended up trending across the internet. Festivals abroad are now lining up to show it too.

## The girl who wanted to be the next Steve Jobs

**Elizabeth Anne Holmes** was born on **3 February 1984** in Washington, D.C. Her father, **Christian Holmes IV**, worked as an executive in government agencies and energy companies; her mother, **Noel Holmes**, worked for a congressional committee. She has a brother, Christian.

Holmes was brilliant and driven from childhood. She went to **Stanford University** to study chemical engineering — and then, at just **19 years old**, she dropped out to start a company. She had a fear of needles, and out of that fear came a seductive idea: what if hundreds of medical tests could be run from just a **few drops of blood** from a finger prick, on one small machine?

The company was **Theranos** — a blend of the words "therapy" and "diagnosis".

## The $9 billion lie

The idea was beautiful. The problem was that it never truly worked.

That did not stop the rise. Holmes copied Steve Jobs' famous style — black turtleneck, deep voice, absolute certainty — and the world believed. Famous investors poured in hundreds of millions of dollars. Powerful men joined her board. At its peak, Theranos was valued at about **$9 billion**, and Holmes' personal paper fortune was put at around **$4.5 billion**. Forbes celebrated her as the youngest self-made female billionaire in America.

Then, in 2015, a Wall Street Journal investigation began pulling at the thread. The machines could not do what was claimed. Test results were unreliable. The company had misled investors, partners and patients. Theranos shut down in **2018**, and Forbes quietly revised Holmes' net worth to **zero**.

## Prison in Texas

In **2022**, a jury convicted Holmes of defrauding investors out of more than **$450 million**. She was ordered to pay **$452 million in restitution** together with her former business and romantic partner, Ramesh "Sunny" Balwani.

On **30 May 2023**, she reported to **Federal Prison Camp Bryan**, a minimum-security women's prison in Texas, about three hours from Dallas. Her original sentence was **11 years and three months**. In March this year, a judge shortened it by twelve months, to about **10 years and three months**. Officials have approved a move to a halfway house near Austin in **August 2027**, and her full release is projected for **March or April 2032**.

She did not go into prison alone in life. In 2019 she married **Billy Evans**, an heir to a hotel business. They have two children: a son, **William**, born in 2021, and a daughter, **Invicta**, born in 2023 — meaning both of her children were born while her legal world was collapsing around her. It is this family, in its final days of ordinary life, that the new documentary films.

## Why the world cannot let her go

Holmes' story has already produced a bestselling book, an award-winning TV drama ("The Dropout"), podcasts and countless articles. So why does a new film still stop the internet?

Partly, it is the access. No Theranos film before this one was allowed inside her home, with her husband and babies, in the last free days — the long goodbye before the prison gates. Partly, it is the question her story never stops asking: how did so many rich, powerful, experienced people believe a machine that did not work? And partly, if we are honest, it is her. The black turtleneck. The voice. The stare. A whole era of "fake it till you make it" is packed into one person, and the culture keeps returning to her the way you return to the scene of an accident.

This week, from a prison cell in Texas, Elizabeth Holmes is famous again. She will not see most of it. But the rest of the world is watching — which, in the end, was always the thing she wanted most, and the thing that destroyed her.
