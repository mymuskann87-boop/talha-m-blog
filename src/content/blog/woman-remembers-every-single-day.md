Pick a date. Any date from the last forty years or so — a random Tuesday in 1987, a wet Sunday in 1995, the day of a famous television finale in 1991.

Now imagine sitting across a table from a woman who can answer you instantly: which day of the week it was, what the weather did, and exactly what she herself was doing that day, down to what she ate for dinner.

That is the test scientists kept setting for Jill Price, an ordinary woman from Southern California with a completely extraordinary memory. And again and again, she passed it.

## The Letter That Started the Test

In the year 2000, a letter reached the desk of James McGaugh, a well-known memory researcher at the University of California, Irvine. The writer was a 34-year-old woman named Jill Price. She explained that she had an unbelievable ability to recall her past, and she was, in truth, asking for help — she wanted somebody to explain what was happening inside her head.

McGaugh and his colleagues, Elizabeth Parker and Larry Cahill, invited her in and began testing her carefully. In the first scientific report, published in the journal Neurocase in 2006, she was kept anonymous and called only "AJ," to protect her privacy. Later she chose to speak publicly under her real name, and in 2008 she co-wrote a book about her life, The Woman Who Can't Forget.

## Test One: Name a Date, Any Date

The basic test was simple to describe and very hard to fake.

The researchers would name a date from decades earlier. Price would respond with the day of the week it fell on, and then describe what she personally did that day. Reports of her testing give examples like these: Easter Day fell on 11 April in 1993 — she had spaghetti for dinner. Easter in 1995 fell on 16 April — it was rainy. She could state the date the space shuttle Challenger exploded (28 January 1986 — a Tuesday), and answer just as surely for quiet, ordinary days that meant nothing to anyone but her.

She did all of this automatically, without memory tricks, without counting on her fingers, and without any special effort. The answers simply arrived.

Her clear, detailed recall reaches back to her teenage years. She was born on 30 December 1965, and by her own account, starting from 5 February 1980, when she was fourteen, she remembers everything — and she notes, correctly, that 5 February 1980 was a Tuesday.

## Test Two: Checking Her Against Her Own Diaries

A sceptical scientist does not just accept amazing claims. So the team checked her answers against the diaries Price had kept over the years. When they compared the dates she described from memory with what she had written down at the time, her recall held up with astonishing accuracy.

That diary check is what makes her case so important. Many people feel they have wonderful memories. Price's memory could be measured, tested and confirmed against written records — and it passed. That is why she became the first documented case of her condition in the scientific literature.

## What Is Hyperthymesia, or HSAM?

The condition is called hyperthymesia, also described as hyperthymestic syndrome. Scientists now more often use the term HSAM, which stands for highly superior autobiographical memory.

Every word of that name matters. It is autobiographical memory — memory of her own life, of days she personally lived through. It is not general cleverness, and it is not a trick. Her brain was also scanned, and the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex — two areas deeply involved in memory — were reportedly normal. In other words, scientists did not find an obviously broken or extra part. Something subtler is going on in how her brain organises the past, and researchers are still working to understand exactly what it is. After Price's case was published in 2006, hundreds of people wrote to McGaugh's laboratory claiming the same gift. Most did not check out under testing, but several did, and the Columbia Undergraduate Science Journal notes that fewer than 50 individuals have been recognised with the condition. Price was simply the first one science ever documented.

## What Can She Not Remember?

Here is where the test results surprise most people, because her gift has very sharp edges.

Price does not have a photographic memory. She struggles to learn poetry or historical dates by heart, the way a student crams for an exam. National Geographic, reporting on the research, noted that she can never remember what the five keys on her own keyring are for. In laboratory testing, people with HSAM show only modest advantages in most other mental tasks — things like attention, verbal fluency and working memory are broadly ordinary.

To me, this is the most fascinating part of the whole case: her mind is not a camera, and it is not a supercomputer. It seems to be a diary that writes itself — total recall for the days of her own life, and perfectly normal forgetting for almost everything else.

## Test Three: Can a Super Memory Be Tricked?

In 2013, researcher Lawrence Patihis and colleagues at the University of California, Irvine — including McGaugh and the famous memory expert Elizabeth Loftus — ran a different kind of test. They compared 20 people with HSAM against 38 people with ordinary memories, and tried to plant false memories in both groups, using word lists with a missing word, slideshows of crimes followed by subtly wrong descriptions, and a questionnaire containing invented details about the 9/11 attacks.

The result startled everyone. The people with HSAM fell for the misinformation just as easily as the control group — and on some tests they were actually more likely to absorb the false details and repeat them with confidence and colour.

The lesson is deep: remembering is not playing back a recording. Every time any of us remembers, the memory can be reshaped a little. Even the strongest autobiographical memory on Earth is built by the same fallible, editable process as yours and mine.

## The Heavy Side: A Memory That Never Switches Off

It would be easy to envy Jill Price. The researchers who know her best would tell you not to.

The same system that keeps every birthday also keeps every bad day. A painful argument, a humiliation, a loss — for most of us, time slowly files the sharp edges off these things. For Price, they do not fade in the normal way. She has described her memory as a heavy burden that runs constantly and cannot be switched off, a stream of past days that arrives uninvited, whether she wants it or not. Later brain research on HSAM, reported in the Columbia journal, found differences in several brain regions in HSAM participants — including areas linked to habit and routine — though researchers are careful to say the causes of the condition are still not conclusively known.

Her case changed how scientists talk about forgetting. Forgetting, it turns out, is not a failure of the brain. It is one of the brain's kindnesses — a filter that lets the past soften so the present stays livable. Price's life shows what happens when that filter is missing.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Who is Jill Price?

Jill Price is an American woman from Southern California who was the first person ever documented by scientists with hyperthymesia, also called HSAM. She contacted researcher James McGaugh at the University of California, Irvine, in 2000, and her case was published in 2006.

### What exactly can Jill Price remember?

She can recall, in detail, nearly every day of her own life from about the age of fourteen — including the day of the week for any date, what she did, and events that happened around her on that day.

### Is hyperthymesia the same as a photographic memory?

No. Her gift covers her own lived days, not books, facts or study material. She struggles with rote learning like poetry and historical dates, and her other memory abilities test as broadly ordinary.

### How many people have HSAM?

Very few. After Price's case became public, hundreds of people contacted the researchers claiming the same ability, but most failed testing. Fewer than 50 individuals have been recognised with the condition.

### Can people with HSAM have false memories?

Yes. A 2013 study led by Lawrence Patihis found that people with HSAM were just as vulnerable to planted misinformation as people with ordinary memories — and sometimes more so.
