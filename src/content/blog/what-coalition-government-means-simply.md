Imagine a council with ten seats. Party A wins four seats, Party B wins three, Party C wins two, and Party D wins one. Nobody has the six seats needed for a majority. Nobody can govern alone. That is where a coalition government begins. Two or more parties, or independent representatives, must combine their votes to elect a government and support the decisions it takes. This post explains, in the simplest words, what a coalition is, how one is formed, what it is good at, what can go wrong, and what it means for ordinary voters.

## The One-Sentence Definition

The South African Government defines a coalition government as an arrangement in which two or more political parties, or independent representatives, combine their votes in a legislature or a council to elect a government and to support the decisions it takes. It becomes necessary when no single party has a majority of the seats. In local government, a governing coalition aims to elect a mayor and other members of the executive, and then to support their proposals on matters such as the budget and municipal policies.

The key number is more than half. A party with 51 out of 100 seats can govern alone. A party with 49 cannot, even if it is the largest party by far. In that case, the largest party must find partners whose seats, added to its own, pass the halfway mark. The partnership that results is the coalition. It is a maths problem before it is a friendship, and the maths never goes away.

## Why Coalitions Happen At All

Coalitions happen because of how votes turn into seats. In South Africa, elections use proportional representation, which means parties win seats roughly in proportion to the votes they receive. Such systems are less likely to give one party an outright majority, because the seats are shared out among several parties. When no party passes half, a coalition is necessary to support the executive. Otherwise, the president, premier or mayor is likely to be voted out of power by the legislature that elected them.

In systems where the executive is elected by the legislature and can be removed by it, a government without a majority is a government living on borrowed time. Every important vote becomes a test. A coalition solves that problem by gathering enough votes, in advance, to survive those tests. The partners agree, before the first big vote, that they will back each other on the decisions that matter most.

## A Council Of Ten Seats

Go back to the ten-seat council. Party A has four seats, Party B has three, Party C has two, and Party D has one. Party A and Party B together have seven seats, which is a majority. So do Party A and Party C and Party D together, with seven. Party B, Party C and Party D together have six, which is also a majority, even though Party A, the largest party, is left out. The arithmetic allows several different coalitions, and which one forms depends on negotiation, not on arithmetic alone.

The parties must negotiate and find enough support to form a governing arrangement. That does not mean they suddenly agree on everything. They may agree on a mayor, a budget, or certain priorities, while still disagreeing on other issues. The important point is that the numbers in the council matter. A mayor needs the support of a majority of councillors to be elected, and the council can remove executive leadership. A coalition is the deal that keeps those numbers on the government's side.

## How A Coalition Is Actually Formed

There is no fixed formula for forming a coalition. When an election produces a hung council — a council where no party won a majority — the parties in the council must explore options, find each other, and hopefully negotiate a partnership before the first meeting of the council, when office-bearers are elected. The same applies when a coalition collapses, for example after a motion of no confidence removes a mayor: a new partnership must be explored before the next meeting.

In practice, the talks cover the big questions first. Who becomes mayor, premier or president? Which parties get which jobs in the executive? What will the coalition agree on in the budget and in policy? Is there a written agreement, so voters can see what was promised? Residents can reasonably ask whether there is a clear coalition agreement and whether the parties have explained what they agreed to do. A coalition without a clear agreement is a coalition built on handshakes, and handshakes are easily forgotten.

## What Coalitions Are Good At

Coalitions have real advantages. The South African Government's own questions-and-answers on coalitions lists three. First, a coalition government of several parties represents different political ideas, cultures and ways of thinking, which helps bring communities together, because people see their representatives working together. Second, a coalition adopts compromise-based policies, which may last longer than policies adopted by one majority party that can be abolished as soon as that party loses power. Third, well-managed coalitions may make government more stable in the long run.

There is a deeper point behind the list. A coalition forces parties to talk before they act. A single-party majority can push a policy through without listening to anyone. A coalition must persuade its own partners first, which usually means the policy has been tested against more than one point of view before it reaches the public. That is slower, but it can also be wiser.

## What Can Go Wrong

Coalitions can also go badly wrong, and the same official source is honest about the risks. The composition of the government may not perfectly reflect the will of the voters: a large party could be left out of government if enough smaller parties combine against it, while the smallest party may end up inside government. That can feel unfair to voters who backed the largest party.

Coalitions may also be unstable. If the partnership is weak, and the parties fall into conflict soon after forming, the coalition will not last. Decisions are postponed, and the legislature or council becomes preoccupied with politics instead of governance and service delivery. For residents, that is not an abstract problem. Municipalities handle water, sanitation, electricity, refuse removal, planning and municipal finances. If political disagreements prevent a council from approving budgets or functioning properly, administration and service delivery suffer, and residents feel it in daily life.

## Can Coalitions Be Abolished

It is sometimes asked whether coalitions can simply be abolished. The answer, in South Africa's system, is no, not without changing the Constitution and the electoral system. The proportional representation system is not designed to produce an outright majority, so there will always be the possibility of coalition governments in local government. The law cannot force stable coalitions either, because the law cannot tell elected representatives how to vote or who to support, and it cannot decide the outcome of political negotiations.

What can be changed is the framework around coalitions: guidelines for how agreements are made, protection for the administration so that day-to-day services continue even when politicians disagree, and a change in political culture, so that negotiations aim at compromise-based policies for communities rather than at short-term gains in positions. The South African Government's dialogue on coalitions puts it plainly: coalitions are likely to remain a feature of the system, and the task is to make them work for residents, not to pretend they will go away.

## Why The Voter Still Matters

Voters are not directly involved in coalition negotiations. The deals are made between parties and independent representatives, because they are the ones who must agree to work together in the council or legislature. That can leave voters feeling excluded, and the feeling is understandable: people vote for a party, and then watch other parties decide the government behind closed doors.

But the voter's role does not end on election day. Once a coalition government is in place, it is fully accountable to voters and residents for all its decisions, and it is duty bound to involve the public in its decision-making. Voters can ask who is accountable for decisions, whether the council is functioning and approving budgets, whether basic services are being delivered, whether finances are managed responsibly, and whether residents are given chances to raise concerns through ward meetings and public participation. A coalition that cannot answer those questions is a coalition in trouble, whatever its maths say.

## Coalitions Are Not New

Coalitions are not a new invention. In South Africa, there have been coalitions since 1994. The first democratic national government after the 1994 elections was a broad-based coalition, a Government of National Unity. There have also been coalitions at provincial level and in municipalities ever since the first democratic local government elections in 2000. They became a major issue after the 2016 and 2021 local elections, when more municipalities, including the biggest cities, had hung councils. After the 2021 elections, about 70 councils had no single-party majority and needed coalitions, a number that keeps changing as by-elections alter council compositions.

The lesson of that history is simple. A coalition is not a failure of democracy. It is what democracy looks like when voters refuse to give any one party all the power. Whether it works depends less on the arithmetic and more on whether the partners can compromise, keep their agreements, and keep the services running while they argue about the rest.

Power shared must be watched more closely, not less.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is a coalition government in simple words?**

A coalition government forms when no single party wins more than half the seats, so two or more parties, or independent representatives, combine their votes to elect a government and support its decisions. In a council of ten seats, for example, a party with four seats must find partners with at least two more seats to reach a majority of six or more. At local level, the partners work together to elect a mayor and other executive members and to back decisions such as the budget and municipal policies. It is a deal built on numbers first, and on shared agreements second.

**How is a coalition government formed after an election?**

There is no fixed formula. When an election produces a hung council, where no party has a majority, the parties must explore options, find each other, and negotiate a partnership before the first council meeting, when office-bearers are elected. The talks cover who becomes mayor, premier or president, which parties get which executive jobs, and what the partners agree on in the budget and policy. If a coalition later collapses, for example after a motion of no confidence, a new partnership must be explored before the next meeting.

**Why do coalition governments sometimes collapse?**

Coalitions collapse when the partnership is weak and the parties fall into conflict soon after forming, so decisions get postponed and the council becomes preoccupied with politics instead of governance and service delivery. A large party can also be left out of government while a very small party gets in, which can feel unfair to voters, and partners may disagree on the budget or on key policies. The South African Government notes that weak partnerships lead to instability, delayed decisions, and councils focused on disputes rather than on running services such as water, electricity and refuse removal.

**Are coalition governments good or bad for voters?**

They can be both, and the difference lies in how they are managed. On the good side, coalitions represent different ideas and communities, adopt compromise-based policies that may last longer, and can make government more stable in the long run when well managed. On the bad side, they can be unstable, delay decisions, and leave voters feeling excluded, because voters do not take part in the negotiations themselves. For residents, the test is practical: does the council approve budgets, deliver basic services, manage finances responsibly, and give people chances to raise concerns.

**Can voters do anything about a coalition government?**

Yes. Voters are not directly involved in coalition negotiations, which happen between parties, but once a coalition is in place it is fully accountable to voters and residents for all its decisions and must involve the public in decision-making. Voters can ask whether there is a clear coalition agreement, who is accountable for decisions, whether the council is functioning, whether services are being delivered, and whether residents can raise concerns through ward meetings and public participation. At the next election, voters can also judge the coalition partners on what the coalition actually delivered.
