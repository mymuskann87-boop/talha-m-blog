Four million dollars.

The number hangs in the air of the Christie's salesroom in New York, and nobody blinks. It is the evening of 4 December 2014. On a table at the front of the room sits a disc of 23-carat gold, small enough to hide inside a closed fist. Three buyers are fighting for it down telephone lines, their voices carried into the room by Christie's staff. The price climbs in hard steps of $100,000 at a time.

Four million one hundred thousand.

The hammer falls. Sold.

Within minutes, the final figure — $4,757,000 once the auction house's premium is added — will flash across news wires all over the world. A record has been smashed. Yet the money is not the most shocking part of this sale. The seller is. The man who owns this medal is still alive, and he earned it for one of the greatest discoveries science has ever made: the structure of DNA, the molecule that carries the instructions for building every living thing, including you.

So why would a scientist sell the highest honour on Earth? Hold that question. First, watch the bids fly.

## The bidding war, blow by blow

The fight did not start at four million. Christie's opened the bidding at $1.5 million, and the price moved swiftly upward in a three-way battle between clients bidding by phone. At $3.8 million, one of the three dropped out, and the room grew quieter. Two telephone bidders remained. Neither would give in. Back and forth they went, $100,000 at a time, until the hammer settled it at $4.1 million.

Before the sale, Christie's had estimated the medal would fetch between $2.5 million and $3.5 million. The result blew past the top of that range. It was the highest price ever paid for a Nobel Prize medal, and James Watson had become the first living Nobel laureate in history to sell his own medal at auction.

The medal was not even the only piece of his history sold that day. His handwritten notes for the speech he gave at the 1962 Nobel banquet brought in $365,000, and a corrected 46-page manuscript connected to the prize ceremonies sold for $245,000 — an extra $610,000 in total, on top of the medal itself.

## Freeze the frame: whose medal is this?

Roll back the clock sixty-one years, to Cambridge in 1953. James Watson, a young American biologist born in Chicago in 1928, was working with the British scientist Francis Crick. Together they built the model that changed biology forever: the double helix. Picture a ladder twisted into a spiral, with two long strands winding around each other and chemical pairs forming the rungs. That twisting ladder is DNA, and understanding its shape explained how living things copy their instructions and pass them on.

The breakthrough leaned on vital evidence from King's College London, where Maurice Wilkins worked — and where Rosalind Franklin produced X-ray diffraction images of DNA, including the famous image known as Photo 51, which gave crucial evidence of the helix shape. Honesty matters here: Franklin died in 1958, aged just 37, and Nobel Prizes are not awarded to people who have died. So when the 1962 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine went to Watson, Crick and Wilkins, she could not be included. Many historians believe her contribution was undervalued for years, and today her name is rightly honoured alongside theirs.

Watson received that gold medal in 1962. He kept it for fifty-two years. By 2014, he was 86 years old — and he was putting it up for sale.

## The fall: seven cold years

To understand the auction, you have to sit through the years before it. In 2007, Watson gave an interview to the Sunday Times of London in which he linked race and intelligence, suggesting that the intelligence of African people was not the same as that of others. The claims had no solid scientific support, and the reaction around the world was fierce. He apologised for the remarks, but the damage was done.

He stepped down as chancellor of Cold Spring Harbor Laboratory, the famous research centre on Long Island that he had led for decades. Other doors closed too: board seats vanished, invitations dried up, and his income shrank until it rested mostly on his academic work. Talking to the Financial Times before the auction, he said he had become an "unperson" — a man the world had quietly decided to forget.

One event in 2013 showed him a possible way out. The family of Francis Crick, who had died in 2004, sold Crick's Nobel medal for about $2.27 million. If his late partner's medal could command a price like that, what might his own fetch while he was still alive to decide where the money went?

## His reasons, stated in the open

Watson never hid why he was selling. He said he wanted to re-enter public life after years of being shunned, and that the money would supplement the income he had lost. He planned to give part of the proceeds to the institutions that had shaped him: the University of Chicago, where he studied; Clare College at Cambridge, where he did the work that won the prize; and Cold Spring Harbor Laboratory itself.

And there was one smaller, very human wish on his list. He hoped to buy a painting by David Hockney.

Pride, punishment, money, art, and a wish to be welcomed back — all of it was riding on one small disc of gold as the telephones lit up in New York.

## Sold — and then the buyer steps forward

The winning bidder was anonymous. For five days, nobody knew who had paid $4.1 million for a Nobel medal. Then, on 9 December, the buyer revealed himself: Alisher Usmanov, a metals and telecoms tycoon described by Forbes as Russia's richest man, and a major shareholder in the Arsenal football club. The founder of USM Holdings, he had been named Russia's most generous charity benefactor by Bloomberg and Forbes in both 2013 and 2014. Looking back, that reputation was a hint of what was coming.

And then he said the thing nobody expected. A situation in which an outstanding scientist had to sell the medal recognising his achievements was, in his word, unacceptable. Watson, he said, was one of the greatest biologists in the history of mankind, and the award for the discovery of DNA's structure must belong to him. Usmanov announced he would give the medal back — and he wanted the money from the sale to support scientific research and the fight against cancer.

He kept his word. In June 2015, at a ceremony at the Russian Academy of Sciences in Moscow, the academy's president, Professor Vladimir Fortov, formally handed the medal back to Watson on Usmanov's behalf. Watson said he was deeply moved by the gesture, calling it the highest praise he could receive for his work on the structure of DNA.

So the seller kept his medal. He kept the money too, free to send it toward the laboratories and universities he had named. In my view, it is the strangest happy ending in the history of the Nobel Prizes: a prize that was sold at full price, in public, with a world record attached — and then simply came home.

But before you decide it all worked out perfectly, sit with the harder part for a moment. A man held science's greatest honour for fifty-two years, lost his standing in seven, and felt he had to put the medal on the auction block to be heard again. If your life's crowning award sat in a drawer while the world stopped calling, would you sell it too?

## Common questions

**Was James Watson really the first living laureate to sell a Nobel medal?**
Yes. Others had been sold before, but always after the winner's death, usually by their families. Watson was the first living Nobel laureate to put his own medal up for auction, and the price set a world record for any Nobel medal.

**How much did the medal sell for, exactly?**
The winning bid was $4.1 million. With the buyer's premium charged by Christie's added on top, the formal total came to $4,757,000. Watson received the hammer price, and the institutions he named received donations from the proceeds.

**Who bought the medal, and why return it?**
Alisher Usmanov, then described by Forbes as Russia's richest man. He said it was unacceptable for such a scientist to be forced to sell his medal, and that the award belonged with Watson. He returned it in the hope the money would support scientific research and cancer work.

**When did Watson actually get the medal back?**
Usmanov announced the return in December 2014, days after the sale. The medal was formally handed back at a ceremony at the Russian Academy of Sciences in Moscow in June 2015.

**Why was Rosalind Franklin not part of the 1962 prize?**
Her X-ray work, including Photo 51, provided key evidence for the double helix. But she died in 1958, and Nobel Prizes are not awarded after death, so she could not share the 1962 prize. Her contribution is widely recognised today.
