Note: This is a fictional horror story. It is not real, and any resemblance to real people or places is purely coincidental.

There is a room at the top of the old house on Miller's Hill that nobody uses. It is not locked. It has never needed to be locked. The door sticks in summer and swings freely in winter, and the floor inside is made of wide wooden boards, dark with age, worn smooth in the middle where feet have passed for a hundred years. The people who own the house now use the room for storage. Boxes. Old curtains. A broken chair. Nothing that needs to be visited often. Nothing that would make a person linger.

Thomas lingered. Thomas was the kind of man who fixed things, and the house on Miller's Hill had been left to him by an uncle he barely remembered, and fixing it felt like the right way to say thank you to a man he had never properly known. He started with the roof. He moved to the pipes. And on a grey afternoon in November, with rain tapping the windows and the light already fading, he climbed the stairs to the top room with a toolbox in his hand and a plan to replace the boards that creaked the loudest.

He knelt on the floor and pressed his palm flat against the wood. It was cold. Colder than the rest of the house, colder than the air outside, cold in the way that stone is cold, not wood. He told himself it was the wind under the house. He picked up his hammer. And then, from directly beneath his hand, so close that he felt it through the board as much as he heard it, a voice said, "Hello."

Thomas dropped the hammer. It clattered across the floor and came to rest against the wall. He stayed where he was, kneeling, hand still pressed to the board, breathing in a way that did not feel steady. The voice had been small. It had been polite. It had sounded like a child speaking through water, far away and yet somehow right beside him.

"Hello," it said again. And then, with a patience that made Thomas's skin crawl, it added, "We've been waiting for someone who presses down. Most people just walk across."

## Voices Under Floor Boards Speak

Thomas stood up so fast his knees cracked. He backed to the door, toolbox forgotten, and stood in the hallway with his back against the wall, listening to his own heart. The house was silent. The rain tapped the windows. Nothing else moved. He told himself, firmly and out loud, that old houses make sounds, that pipes knock, that wood contracts, that a tired mind can turn a creak into a word the way a tired eye can turn a shadow into a shape.

He went downstairs. He made tea. He sat at the kitchen table and did not go back up that day. But that night, lying in the bedroom below the top room, he heard it again. Not a creak. Not a knock. A murmur, soft and many-voiced, like a crowd speaking in another room with the door almost closed. It came from above him, from the floor of the room no one used, and it went on for hours, rising and falling, until Thomas fell asleep with a pillow over his head and dreamed of a room full of people standing shoulder to shoulder, looking up.

In the morning he did what sensible people do when they are frightened of their own house. He called a builder. The builder came, a broad man with sawdust on his boots, and Thomas led him up to the top room and pointed at the floor and said, "These boards creak. Can you look underneath?" The builder knelt, knocked on the boards with his knuckles, and frowned. He knocked again, harder. Then he stood up, dusted his hands, and said something Thomas did not expect.

"There's nothing under there," the builder said. "No space. No gap. These boards are laid straight onto solid earth. Must be the old way of building. Sound doesn't travel through that. Whatever you're hearing, it's not coming from below."

Thomas nodded. He paid the man. He watched him leave. And then he went back up to the top room alone, knelt on the floor, and pressed his ear to the coldest board, the one in the middle of the room, and he listened.

"Thank you," the voices said, all together, like a choir whispering. "Thank you for coming back. He told you there is nothing under here. He is right. There is nothing under here anymore. That is why we need you to lift the boards. So that there can be something again."

## The Floor That Asked to Be Opened

Thomas did not lift the boards that day. He did not lift them the next day either. But the voices did not stop. They spoke every night, and each night they spoke a little more clearly, and each night they told him a little more. They told him they had been under the floor for a very long time, longer than the house, longer than the hill, and that they had been put there by people who were afraid of them, and that being afraid was understandable, because they were, in their own words, "not finished". They told him that all they wanted was to be let out, just for a little while, just to stand in the air again, just to see the sky. They promised they would be grateful. They promised they would leave the house alone afterward. They promised, and promised, and promised, and each promise sounded exactly like the truth, which was the most frightening thing about them.

On the fourth night, Thomas asked them a question. He whispered it into the floorboards, feeling foolish and brave at the same time. "If I lift the boards," he said, "what will come out?"

There was a long silence. The kind of silence that follows a question no one wants to answer honestly. Then a single voice, smaller than the rest, said, "Us. And the dark we have been keeping down here with us. But mostly us."

Thomas pulled his hand back from the floor as if it had burned him. He went downstairs. He packed a bag. He drove to a friend's house in town and slept on a sofa, and for one night the voices were far away, and he slept properly for the first time in days. In the morning, feeling braver in daylight, he drove back to the house to collect the rest of his things, because he had decided, quite firmly, that he would sell the place and never think about it again.

The house was waiting. The top room was waiting. And on the floor, in the middle of the room, where the coldest board had been, there was now a gap. A single board had been lifted and set aside, neatly, as if by careful hands. Beneath it was darkness, complete and absolute, darker than any space under a floor has a right to be. And from that darkness, rising up like breath, came the voices, no longer whispering, no longer patient, singing.

## The Last Voice Beneath the Floor

Thomas ran. He ran down the stairs, out of the front door, across the garden, and he did not stop until he reached the road, where he stood shaking in the rain and watched the house on Miller's Hill stand silent against the grey sky. He sold it six weeks later, to a young couple who loved the garden and the tall windows and the quiet street. He did not tell them about the top room. He told himself that the voices had wanted him specifically, that they had waited for someone who presses down, and that a new family would never press down, never listen, never lift a board.

He was wrong. He knows he was wrong, because last month, late at night, his phone rang, and the number on the screen was one he did not recognise, and when he answered, he heard a sound he had not heard since November: a murmur, soft and many-voiced, like a crowd speaking in another room with the door almost closed. And then a single small voice, polite as ever, said, "Hello. We've been waiting for someone who answers. Most people just let it ring."

Thomas has not answered his phone since. He keeps it in a drawer now, switched off, and he tells himself that distance is protection, and that silence is safety. But he knows, in the way that people know things at three in the morning when they cannot sleep, that the voices are no longer under a floor. They are in the lines now, in the wires, in the spaces between one ring and the next, waiting for someone who presses down, someone who listens, someone who lifts the board that keeps them in.

And somewhere, in a house on Miller's Hill, in a room at the top of the stairs that nobody uses, the floor is warm for the first time in a hundred years. Because there is nothing underneath it anymore. And there does not need to be. They are already out.
