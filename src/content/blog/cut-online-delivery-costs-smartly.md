The rider's day was a map of small decisions. First stop: one phone cover, ordered alone, delivery fee larger than the cover itself. Second stop: three items from one shop, one fee between them. Third stop: a parcel sent express for no reason except impatience, at triple the normal fee. By evening, the rider had carried the same streets three times, and the customers had paid for every extra trip.

Online shopping means buying goods over the internet and having them delivered to your door. The delivery fee is the price of that journey. It feels small beside the item's price, which is exactly why it escapes notice — until the month's orders are added up and the fees stand there like a second shopping bill.

## Step one: bundle orders into one delivery

The simplest saving in online shopping is also the most powerful: order together instead of apart. One delivery carrying five items costs one fee. Five separate deliveries carrying one item each cost five fees. The items do not care whether they travel together. Your wallet does.

Keep a small running list — on paper or on your phone — of things you need. When the list has enough for one sensible order, place it. The habit costs a little patience and saves a fee almost every time. For example, three separate orders at 150 delivery each cost 450 in fees, while one combined order costs 150. The saving is not clever. It is just arithmetic that most people never do.

## Step two: do not let free-delivery thresholds trick you

Many shops offer free delivery above a certain order value. The threshold is a genuine saving when you were already going to buy that much. It is a trap when it makes you add items you never wanted.

Here is the test: look at the extra item and ask whether you would buy it tomorrow at full price, without the threshold. If the answer is no, the "free" delivery just cost you the price of something useless. The shop did not give you delivery. It sold you an extra item and called the delivery free.

In my view, a threshold is only a bargain when the basket was already full before you saw it — otherwise it is the shop spending your money for you.

## Step three: pay for speed only when it matters

Express delivery is a luxury with a luxury price. It makes sense for a birthday gift tomorrow, a medicine urgently needed, or a part that stops work until it arrives. It makes no sense for a phone cover, a notebook, or a kitchen gadget that can wait three days.

Before choosing express, ask one question: what changes if this arrives on Thursday instead of Tuesday? If nothing changes, choose the slower, cheaper option and keep the difference. Impatience is the most expensive delivery option on every website, and it is the only one you choose with a single careless click.

## Step four: collect from a nearby store or pickup point

Many sellers let you collect an order from a nearby store, locker, or pickup point at a lower fee than home delivery — sometimes at no fee at all. If the pickup point is on a route you already travel, the saving is pure profit.

Check the pickup option before every order, not after. It takes ten seconds to compare, and it turns a delivery fee into a short walk. The option suits people who pass shops daily far better than people who must make a special trip, so judge it by your own routine.

## Step five: compare delivery fees across sellers

The same item often sells on several websites, and the delivery fee can differ more than the item's price. Before ordering, check the total — item price plus delivery — on two or three sellers. The cheapest item with the most expensive delivery is often the most expensive order of all.

This comparison takes a minute and it applies to everything: books, chargers, clothes, kitchen goods. The total at checkout is the only price that matters. Everything before it is advertising.

## The honest limits of cheap delivery

Not every delivery fee can be cut, and some should not be. Heavy items, fragile goods, and faraway addresses genuinely cost more to move, and a fair fee for honest work is not waste. Remote areas may have only one delivery option, and in that case the saving comes from bundling and patience, not from shopping around.

Returns deserve a mention too. A cheap delivery that leads to three returns can cost more than one careful order with a fair fee. Read the item description, check the size, and order once, carefully. The cheapest delivery of all is the one you never need to send back.

## The monthly delivery audit

Monthly habits catch monthly fees, and delivery fees deserve their own audit. Once a month, open your order history on each shopping app and add up one column only: the delivery fees. Ignore the item prices for this exercise. The delivery column alone is the number that matters here.

Compare that number with your monthly shopping budget. If delivery fees eat more than a small slice of it, look for the pattern behind them. The audit usually reveals one of three stories. Friday-night impulse orders, placed tired and hungry, each carrying a full fee. One favourite seller whose delivery fee is always the highest, used out of habit rather than comparison. Or a month of single-item orders that could have waited two days and travelled together.

Set a monthly delivery budget after the first audit — a fixed amount that feels fair, written down where you will see it. When the budget runs out, later orders wait until next month or go to a pickup point. The budget is not a punishment. It is a fence that keeps a small fee from growing into a second shopping bill, and it works because it turns a feeling — "delivery feels cheap" — into a number you can argue with. Pair the budget with a waiting list: when you want something that is not urgent, write it on the list and wait two days before ordering. The waiting list also protects the delivery budget, because an order that waits is an order that can still be cancelled. Half the list will quietly lose its appeal, which is the list doing its job. What remains after two days is worth ordering, and by then it can often travel with another item on the same order. Waiting costs nothing, kills impulse orders, and makes bundling happen on its own — three savings from one patient habit, and the list itself becomes a small record of wants that faded, which is a useful thing to look back on at the end of every month.

## Common questions

### Why do delivery fees add up so fast?

Because each small order carries its own fee. Five separate orders at 150 delivery each cost 750 in fees alone, while one combined order costs a single fee. Bundling orders is the fastest way to cut the total.

### Is free delivery above a threshold always a good deal?

Only if you were already buying that much. If the threshold makes you add an item you never wanted, the "free" delivery actually cost you the price of that item. Buy the extra item only if you would buy it anyway.

### When is express delivery worth the extra cost?

When timing genuinely matters — a gift needed tomorrow, an urgent medicine, or a part that stops work. For ordinary items that can wait a few days, the slower cheaper option saves money with no real loss.

### Are pickup points really cheaper than home delivery?

Often, yes. Collecting from a nearby store or locker can cost less than home delivery, sometimes nothing at all. It works best when the pickup point is already on a route you travel daily.

### Should I always choose the cheapest item online?

No — compare the total, item price plus delivery, across sellers. The cheapest item with the most expensive delivery is often the most expensive order. The checkout total is the only price that matters.
