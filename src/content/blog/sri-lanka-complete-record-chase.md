Four hundred and thirty runs. That was the mountain standing in front of Sri Lanka in Rawalpindi — 430 runs to win a one-day match, against an England side that had just finished batting like the ground belonged to them. Most teams, looking at a target like that, quietly accept the day is over. Sri Lanka looked at it, took a deep breath, and started climbing.

What followed was not just a win. It was a chase so big that it now sits in the history books as the second-highest successful run chase in the whole history of one-day international cricket. And it was decided by a single six, off nearly the very last ball of the match.

## A Mountain Called 429

Every great chase begins with a score that looks impossible. England batted first in this ODI tri-series match in Rawalpindi, Pakistan, and they did not hold back. By the end of their innings, the scoreboard read 429 for 4 — a total so large that it usually ends a contest before the second innings even begins.

Think about what 429 means in a 50-over match. It means the batting side scored at well over eight runs an over, for the entire innings, without ever really slowing down. It means almost every bowler got punished. It means the fielding side spent hours chasing the ball to the boundary in the heat, and then had to walk out and somehow score even faster.

Teams have crumbled under far smaller targets. The pressure of a huge chase does strange things to batsmen. You cannot settle. You cannot take your time. Every quiet over feels like a small defeat, because the asking rate keeps growing behind you like a wave. One slow patch, two quick wickets, and the mountain suddenly looks twice as tall.

Sri Lanka knew all of this. And still, when their openers walked out, the belief was there. On a good batting surface in Rawalpindi, with a target that demanded courage from the very first ball, they had no choice but to play the bravest cricket of their lives.

## The Chase Begins: No Time to Breathe

A chase of 430 is not one long sprint. It is a series of waves, and a team has to ride every single one of them. Fall behind in the early overs, and the required rate climbs beyond reach. Lose wickets in a clump, and the whole innings collapses under the weight of the scoreboard.

Sri Lanka's approach had to be fearless but not foolish. The runs needed to keep flowing, over after over, while wickets stayed in hand. That balance — attack without panic — is the hardest thing in a big chase. Every batsman who walked in knew the same truth: there was no room for a slow start, no room for a cautious ten balls to "get set." The match would not wait for anyone.

As the innings grew, the tension grew with it. The crowd could feel that something unusual was happening. Chases like this almost always break at some point — a mistimed shot, a brilliant catch, a run-out — and the whole dream falls apart in a few deliveries. The question hanging over Rawalpindi was simple: how long could Sri Lanka keep the wave going?

## Three Heroes, One Impossible Innings

Big chases are never won by one man. They need a chain of heroes, each one carrying the innings a little further before handing it on. Sri Lanka found three of them on the day, and each played a different kind of innings.

### Pavan Rathnayake: The Rock of the Chase

Pavan Rathnayake scored 130 — the kind of innings that gives a team a spine. A century in any chase is special, but a century in a chase of 430 is the foundation everything else stands on. While he was at the crease, Sri Lanka always had a batsman who was set, scoring, and keeping the required rate honest. His 130 runs were not just runs. They were time bought for everyone else, and belief poured into the dressing room.

### Avishka Fernando: Aggression at the Top

Avishka Fernando made 97 — just three runs short of a hundred that he will think about for a long time. But in a chase like this, a fast 97 can be worth more than a slow century. Fernando's innings kept the pressure exactly where Sri Lanka needed it: on England. Every boundary he hit reminded the fielding side that this target, however huge, was being attacked with real intent.

### Dasun Shanaka: Power When It Mattered Most

Then came Dasun Shanaka, who smashed 97 of his own. Notice the word smashed. When a chase reaches its final stretch, what a team needs most is raw power — someone who can clear the ropes and turn a demanding equation into a manageable one in the space of a few overs. Shanaka provided exactly that. His hitting in the closing phase turned a chase that still looked doubtful into one that suddenly felt possible, and then likely, and then — with the crowd roaring — almost inevitable.

Three batsmen, three nineties-and-above contributions, three different styles. One patient anchor, one aggressive stroke-maker, one finisher with brute force. That is the complete recipe for a record chase, and Sri Lanka had every ingredient.

## The Final Over: One Ball Left in Rawalpindi

And still, after all of that, the match came down to the very end. Sri Lanka reached 430 for 8 in 49.5 overs. Read that again slowly. Forty-nine point five overs. Out of fifty. Eight wickets down. The winning runs arrived with exactly one ball left in the entire match.

One ball. If that final scoring shot had been a dot ball instead, the scores would have been level and the match would have ended in the most painful way imaginable for a team that had climbed so far. The margin between a famous victory and a heartbreaking near-miss was a single delivery.

The man who finished it was Janith Liyanage, and he did not finish it with a nudge into a gap or a scrambled single. He sealed the victory with a six — the boldest shot in cricket, hit at the moment of maximum pressure, when a mistake would have been remembered forever. Liyanage swung, the ball sailed, and Rawalpindi erupted. Sri Lanka had beaten England by two wickets, in a match England had controlled with 429 runs and somehow still lost.

In my view, a chase like this is cricket at its very best, because it proves that no scoreboard total, however giant, is safe when a team refuses to stop believing.

## Why This Chase Will Be Remembered for Years

Records in cricket are not broken every week, and this one carries real weight. Sri Lanka's 430 for 8 is the second-highest successful run chase in one-day international history. Only one team, in the entire history of the format, has ever chased down more. Generations of great batsmen and famous teams have tried to hunt down totals like this and failed. Sri Lanka did it in Rawalpindi, against England, with the pressure of a tri-series match on their shoulders.

There is also a lesson in this match for every young cricketer watching. England did almost everything right with the bat and still walked off as losers. That is the strange beauty of limited-overs cricket: the game is never finished until the final run is scored or the final wicket falls. A target of 430 felt like a wall. It turned out to be a door, and Sri Lanka walked straight through it.

Matches like this are why fans stay until the last ball, why crowds refuse to leave early, and why a chase — done properly, with courage and calm heads — can be even more thrilling than the biggest innings ever played.
