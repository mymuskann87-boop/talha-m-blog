Deep under the hot desert sand, far below the surface where no sunlight has ever reached, sits a barrel of oil that has not moved for millions of years. It began its life as tiny sea creatures in an ancient ocean. Time, pressure and heat slowly cooked them into the thick black liquid the whole modern world runs on. Cars drink it. Buses drink it. Planes, ships, factories and farms all drink it. And this week, that quiet barrel woke up to a frightening world — because above the sand, drones were flying, alarms were ringing, and its price shot up to $114, the highest level in more than five months.

Come, let us travel with this barrel. Its journey will explain, in the simplest way, why oil prices jump, why traders panic, and why a rickshaw driver in an ordinary town ends up paying for a fight that happened thousands of kilometres away.

## A Barrel Sleeps, Then the World Shouts

Our barrel does not travel alone. It is part of a giant underground lake of oil beneath the Arabian desert, in the region that holds some of the largest oil fields on Earth. Above it stand the famous nodding pumps, moving up and down all day like slow steel birds, pulling the oil out of the ground.

For months, the price of a barrel like this one had been moving in a fairly calm range. Brent crude — that is the name of the famous oil price the whole world watches, a bit like a scoreboard for oil — had been quiet enough that most people never thought about it. Petrol prices at home changed slowly. Life went on.

Then the headlines arrived, one after another, and every single one of them made traders nervous.

News reports said attacks by the Houthis were growing. Oil facilities in Saudi Arabia — the very fields and plants sitting above barrels like ours — were attacked. A drone attack hit King Fahd International Airport in Dammam, a major Saudi city, and according to the reports, one person was killed and eight people were injured, while flights had to be diverted away from the airport. In another reported incident, two people were killed by falling drone debris at an oil field operated by Aramco, the giant Saudi oil company. The United Kingdom announced it would deploy Typhoon fighter jets to Saudi Arabia as the attacks increased.

Each headline landed on the oil market like a stone thrown into a pond. And the ripples reached our barrel within minutes.

## Why Fear Raises the Price of Oil

Here is the secret most people never learn: the price of oil is not only about the oil that exists today. It is about the oil people *fear* might not arrive tomorrow.

Imagine a town where only one shop sells bread. If a rumour spreads that the flour truck may not come next week, the shopkeeper raises the price today — not because bread has run out, but because everyone suddenly wants to grab extra loaves before it does. The oil market works the same way, only a million times bigger and much, much faster.

Saudi Arabia is one of the world's biggest oil producers. When its airports are attacked and its oil fields are in danger, traders around the planet ask one scary question: what if the oil stops flowing? Nobody waits for the answer. They buy oil quickly, today, at almost any price, because their factories, airlines and shipping companies cannot survive without it. When thousands of buyers rush in at the same time, the price climbs — and climbs — and climbs.

That is exactly what happened this week. Brent crude climbed to its highest level since May. Oil jumped to $114 a barrel, its highest in more than five months. Our sleepy barrel, which had done nothing at all, was suddenly worth far more than it had been a week earlier — not because it changed, but because the world around it became afraid.

## The Men Who Watch the Number

Somewhere in London, New York, Singapore and Dubai, screens glow day and night with one number on them: the price of Brent crude. The people watching those screens are called traders, and this week they barely slept.

Every fresh report moved the number. An attack on an oil facility? The number jumped. Fighter jets being sent to protect the region? The number moved again, because jets can calm fears — or remind everyone how serious the danger is. A diverted flight, an injured worker, a fire at a field: each piece of news is like a hand pushing the price up or letting it slide down.

To these traders, our barrel is not really a barrel. It is a bet on the future. If they believe oil will be hard to get next month, they pay more for it today. If they believe peace is coming, they relax and the price falls. This is why oil can jump several dollars in a single day even when not one extra drop has been pumped or lost. The barrel stays in the ground. Only the fear travels.

## The Long Road to the Petrol Pump

Eventually, our barrel does get pumped out of the desert. It flows through pipelines to a port, rides inside a giant tanker across the sea, gets cleaned and cooked in a refinery, and turns into petrol, diesel and other fuels. Then trucks carry it to petrol stations in cities and small towns everywhere, including towns across Pakistan and the rest of Asia, whose countries buy most of their oil from abroad.

And here is where the story stops being about deserts and traders, and starts being about us.

When the world price of a barrel rises to $114, every country that imports oil pays more dollars for it. Governments and oil companies back home then face a painful choice: absorb the loss themselves, or pass some of it to the people. Very often, sooner or later, part of it reaches the pump. The price board outside the petrol station changes. And the last person in this long chain — the one who never saw a drone, never read a headline, never watched a trading screen — quietly pays the bill.

Picture him: a rickshaw driver who starts his day before sunrise. He fills his tank and notices the price has gone up again. His passengers still pay him the same fare. The difference comes out of his own pocket, out of the money meant for his children's school copies and the evening's vegetables. In my view, he is the most honest economist in this whole story, because he understands the $114 barrel better than any screen-watcher: for him, it is not a number, it is dinner.

## Why This Region Matters So Much

People often wonder why trouble in one desert region shakes prices for the whole planet. The answer has three simple parts.

First, a huge share of the world's oil comes from this region, so even a small threat there worries everyone. Second, much of the world's oil travels by ship through narrow sea routes near these coasts, and attacks make ship owners nervous about sending their tankers through. Third, oil is not like toys or clothes, where a buyer can simply wait a month. A country that runs out of fuel stops moving within days, so nobody dares to gamble. They pay the higher price, and they pay it fast.

That is why a drone over Dammam can raise the price of filling a motorcycle tank on the other side of the world. The barrel connects us all, whether we like it or not.

## What Happens Next to the Price

Nobody honestly knows, and anyone who claims to be fully sure is not telling the truth. Oil prices after a jump like this usually depend on what happens in the following days.

If the attacks stop and the oil keeps flowing normally, fear slowly cools down and prices often slide back, step by step. If more facilities are hit, or if a big field or port is seriously damaged, the price can climb higher and stay high for longer. Governments also watch closely, because expensive oil makes everything else expensive too — food travels by truck, factories run on fuel, and when transport costs rise, shop prices follow.

For ordinary people, the practical lesson is simple: when you hear that oil has jumped to a level like $114, expect fuel prices at home to feel pressure in the coming days and weeks, and expect everything carried by trucks and buses to feel it a little too. The barrel's journey always ends at our doorsteps.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why did oil jump to $114 a barrel?

Oil rose after reports of escalating Houthi attacks, including attacks on Saudi oil facilities, a drone attack on King Fahd International Airport in Dammam and deaths from drone debris at an Aramco-operated field. Traders feared oil supplies could be disturbed, so they bought quickly and pushed the price up to its highest in more than five months.

### What is Brent crude?

Brent crude is the most famous oil price benchmark in the world. When news reports say "oil climbed" or "oil jumped," they are usually talking about this price. It works like a scoreboard that buyers, sellers and governments all watch.

### How high is $114 compared with recent months?

It is the highest level in more than five months. Brent crude climbed to its highest since May, which is why this jump made headlines around the world.

### Will petrol prices in my country also go up?

Very likely they will feel pressure. Most countries in Asia import their oil, and they buy it at the world price. When that price rises and stays high, local fuel prices usually rise after some time, and transport and food costs can follow.

### Why does an attack far away affect my petrol pump?

Because oil is bought and sold in one giant world market. A threat to oil fields or shipping in a major producing region makes the whole world nervous at once, so the world price rises — and every petrol pump connected to that market feels it.

### Can oil prices come back down?

Yes. If attacks stop and oil keeps flowing normally, the fear that pushed the price up slowly fades, and prices often fall back step by step. The price follows the news, in both directions.
