The Albies 2026 were held on 9 October 2026 at the Natural History Museum in London, hosted by the Clooney Foundation for Justice. This was the fifth edition of the awards, which began in 2022. Unlike film or music awards, the Albies do not celebrate box-office success or chart sales. They honour people and organisations who defend justice, often at personal risk, and who rarely receive global attention. That difference explains why the winners list matters: most readers will not already know these names, and the point of the ceremony is to make them known and, in the foundation's words, to show them that the world is watching.

## What the Albies Are and Why They Exist

The Albies are named after Justice Albie Sachs, a South African anti-apartheid campaigner and former Constitutional Court judge who survived imprisonment, exile and an assassination attempt. George and Amal Clooney founded the Clooney Foundation for Justice and created the awards to give a public platform to justice defenders. The idea is practical, not only symbolic. International attention can protect a journalist, activist or organisation that faces threats at home, because attacks on a globally recognised figure carry a higher political cost.

The ceremony combines celebrity guests, donors and media coverage with the honorees' own stories. That mix draws criticism from some readers, who ask whether glamour distracts from serious work. The foundation's argument is that glamour is the tool: without famous hosts and guests, a Kyrgyz journalist or a Congolese organisation would receive far less coverage. Whether that trade-off is right is a judgement readers can make, but it is the stated purpose of the event, and it shapes how the winners list should be read. The honorees are not nominees competing in categories like best actor. They are selected defenders whose work the foundation wants to spotlight.

The 2026 ceremony was held in London, after launching in New York in 2022, and was hosted by Graham Norton, according to contemporaneous reports. Guests reported in coverage included Brad Pitt and Ines de Ramon, Anna Wintour, Emma Thompson, Laura Dern, Melinda Gates, Josh Hartnett and Tamsin Egerton, Emily Ratajkowski, Felicity Jones, Hannah Waddingham, Isla Fisher, Donatella Versace and Bianca Jagger. Those names explain the red-carpet coverage, but they are not the winners. The winners are the six honoree entries below, counting the two Kyrgyz journalists as one joint entry.

The following list is confirmed by multiple contemporaneous reports of the 9 October 2026 ceremony. Spelling of the Kyrgyz journalists' names varies slightly across outlets, which is noted where it occurs.

Both counts are correct depending on whether a joint honour counts as one entry or two people. This post uses five entries and six people to avoid confusion.

## Bolot Temirov and Makhabat Tazhibek Kyzy: Journalism Under Pressure

Investigative journalism in Central Asia carries risks that readers in safer media environments may not fully grasp. Journalists who report on corruption, power and public money can face surveillance, legal cases, detention, smear campaigns and physical threats. The Albies honour for Temirov and Tazhibek Kyzy therefore recognises not a single story, but sustained work under pressure. Their joint honour also matters because it recognises a team, not only a famous individual. Investigative reporting is usually collaborative: one person researches documents, another verifies sources, another publishes and defends the story.

For international readers, the useful question is what protection an award actually gives. It does not grant legal immunity or remove a court case. It does raise the cost of harassment, because foreign governments, media organisations and human rights groups are now watching. It can also help with funding, legal support and safe travel to conferences. At the same time, visibility can increase short-term attention from hostile actors. That tension is why the foundation pairs the award with ongoing legal and advocacy work, rather than treating the ceremony as the end of support.

Readers who want to follow this work should look for the journalists' own reporting and for independent coverage of press freedom in Kyrgyzstan, rather than relying only on ceremony photos. The award is a starting point for attention, not a substitute for reading the journalism itself.

## Vanessa Mwangala: Girls' Rights in Malawi

Vanessa Mwangala was honoured as a Malawian girls' rights advocate. Girls' rights work in Malawi, as in many countries, covers a connected set of issues: access to schooling, protection from early marriage and exploitation, health information, and the right to be heard in family and community decisions. Progress on one issue often depends on progress on others. A girl who stays in school longer is more likely to delay marriage, earn an income, and make informed health choices. A girl who is protected from exploitation is more likely to stay in school.

Advocacy in this area is often local and practical: working with families, schools, religious leaders and local officials, as well as campaigning for national law and policy. That work rarely produces a single dramatic moment suitable for a red-carpet clip. It produces gradual changes: a school that keeps girls enrolled, a community that questions a harmful practice, a local official who enforces an existing law. The Albies honour recognises that slow, community-level work as justice work, equal in importance to courtroom cases and international campaigns.

For readers outside Malawi, the lesson is to avoid treating girls' rights as a single issue or a single solution. Funding a school place helps, but so does safe transport, sanitary products, trained teachers, and protection from abuse. Mwangala's honour invites readers to support organisations that work on several of those links together, and to listen to Malawian advocates themselves about which change matters most in their community.

## Lynsey Addario: Photography as Evidence

Lynsey Addario is an American photojournalist known for documenting conflict and its human cost. Photojournalism in war zones is often described as bearing witness, but that phrase hides the practical work involved: gaining access, travelling under risk, verifying what a photograph shows, protecting the people photographed, and deciding what to publish without causing further harm. A photograph can become evidence, news, history and, for the people in it, a risk. Addario's work has covered that full range, from front-line fighting to the civilian consequences that continue after cameras leave.

The Albies honour for a photojournalist also raises a question about how the public consumes conflict images. A single powerful photograph can move donations and policy, but it can also reduce a complex war to one emotion. Good photojournalism resists that reduction by showing context over time: who is affected, what choices they face, and what happens after the first news cycle ends. Readers can honour that work by reading captions, credits and full photo essays, not only sharing one image without context.

Addario's career also illustrates the physical and psychological risks of the job. Photojournalists face injury, detention and trauma, and women in the field face additional risks and barriers. An award does not remove those risks, but it can help a photojournalist continue working, secure assignments, and access support. For young readers interested in this field, the practical lesson is that strong photography requires reporting skills, ethics and safety planning, not only a camera.

## Justice Plus: An Organisation, Not Only Individuals

Justice Plus, a Congolese organisation, was honoured for human rights work in the Democratic Republic of the Congo. Honouring an organisation, rather than only individuals, is an important choice. In conflict-affected regions, justice work is often done by local teams who document abuses, support victims, train lawyers and community workers, and push for accountability when national and international systems are slow. Those teams continue after any one leader leaves, is threatened, or is exhausted. Recognising the organisation protects the work itself, not only a public face.

The Democratic Republic of the Congo presents severe justice challenges: armed conflict, displacement, weak infrastructure, and abuses that are difficult to investigate and prosecute. Local organisations often work with limited funding, in dangerous areas, and with victims who fear retaliation. Their documentation can later support court cases, truth processes, or international investigations, even if justice is delayed for years. That long timeline is difficult for news coverage, which prefers immediate results, but it is central to how accountability actually happens.

For readers, the practical point is that supporting an organisation can be more durable than supporting a single campaign. Funding, legal partnerships and international attention that reach a local team help it train staff, protect witnesses and keep records safe. The Albies honour for Justice Plus should therefore direct attention to Congolese-led justice work, rather than only to international organisations working in Congo from outside.

## Brad Smith: Corporate Power and Human Rights Advocacy

Brad Smith, Vice Chair and President of Microsoft, was honoured for human rights advocacy. This is the most debated type of Albies honour, because it recognises a senior executive of a major technology company, not a front-line defender facing personal danger. Supporters argue that corporate leaders can influence policy, technology design and access to justice at a scale that individual activists cannot. A company that controls widely used platforms, cloud services and artificial intelligence tools makes decisions every day that affect privacy, free expression, surveillance and access to information.

Critics ask whether honouring a corporate leader risks blurring the line between advocacy and public relations. That concern is fair and should be stated openly. A technology company can support human rights in one area while facing criticism in another, for example over government contracts, data practices or content moderation. Readers should therefore treat Smith's honour as recognition of specific advocacy work cited by the foundation, not as a general endorsement of Microsoft's entire record. The useful question is concrete: which policies, legal interventions or access programmes did he support, and what measurable effect did they have?

Smith's honour also illustrates a wider shift in justice work. Courts and activists remain central, but technology companies now shape the conditions under which justice is sought: whether evidence can be preserved, whether journalists can communicate safely, whether misinformation spreads, and whether governments can access private data. Engaging corporate leaders is therefore a strategic choice by the foundation, even if it sits uneasily beside honours for journalists and local organisations.

## How Each Honour Connects to a Wider Justice Problem

Each 2026 honour maps to a wider problem that will not be solved by one ceremony. Press freedom in Kyrgyzstan connects to a global decline in independent journalism, where reporters face laws that label them, lawsuits that bankrupt them, and online harassment that follows them home. Girls' rights in Malawi connect to global gaps in schooling, health and legal protection, where a girl's future is often decided by family income, distance to school and community norms rather than by her ability. Conflict photography connects to how wars are remembered: without independent images, governments and armed groups control the narrative, and civilian suffering becomes invisible. Congolese justice work connects to impunity in conflict zones, where crimes are documented but rarely prosecuted because courts lack funding, witnesses fear retaliation, and international attention moves on. Corporate advocacy connects to a new justice frontier: private companies now control communications, data and artificial intelligence, so their policies can protect or undermine rights at scale. Seeing those connections helps readers move from admiring individual winners to understanding systems. A donation to a press-freedom fund, support for girls' schooling, careful sharing of conflict images, funding for local documentation teams, and pressure on technology companies are five different actions, each matched to a different honour, and each more useful than general applause.

## My Take:

I think the Albies are easier to dismiss than to understand, and that is a problem for the people they honour. It is easy to look at gold dresses, famous guests and a museum venue and decide the event is only celebrity theatre. That reaction is understandable, but it misses the mechanism the foundation is using. Attention is a resource. A journalist in Kyrgyzstan, a girls' rights advocate in Malawi, a Congolese justice team and a conflict photographer all need attention to stay safe and to keep working. A ceremony that borrows celebrity attention and redirects it toward those people is not automatically shallow. It can be strategic, provided the attention actually reaches the work after the photographs fade.

My judgement is that the 2026 list is strong precisely because it mixes different kinds of justice work. Journalism, girls' rights, photography, local organisation building and corporate advocacy do not look alike, and they should not be forced into one model. A journalist needs legal protection and a platform. A girls' rights advocate needs community trust and long-term funding. A photojournalist needs safety, ethics and time. A Congolese organisation needs staff, secure records and partnerships. A corporate leader needs pressure and incentives to make better policy choices. Honouring all five together says that justice is a system, not a single heroic act. That is a more honest picture than a list of only lawyers or only activists would give.

I am, however, uneasy about the corporate honour, and I think that unease should be stated rather than hidden. When a major technology executive receives the same award as journalists and local defenders, there is a risk that power is being celebrated alongside vulnerability. The foundation's answer would be that corporate power can be used for justice, and that engaging it is necessary. That answer is partly convincing, because technology decisions now affect justice directly. But it only works if the honour is tied to specific, checkable actions, and if criticism of the company continues after the ceremony. An award should not buy silence. Readers should keep asking what Microsoft does on privacy, surveillance, government contracts and AI safety, even while acknowledging any advocacy Smith has supported.

I also think the spelling variations for the Kyrgyz journalists matter more than they seem. A name is not a minor detail when a person faces risk. Variant transliterations can make it harder for readers to find their work, for supporters to verify their identity, and for journalists to cite them correctly. Outlets should standardise names carefully and explain variants, as this post does. Small accuracy failures undermine the very protection the award is meant to provide. If the public cannot reliably identify an honoree, the protective spotlight becomes blurred.

The girls' rights and Congolese organisation honours raise a different point: slow work is hard to celebrate, but it is often the work that lasts. News cycles reward dramatic moments, court verdicts and viral photographs. Community advocacy and local documentation reward patience. A girl who stays in school because a local advocate persuaded her family, or a victim whose testimony is preserved by a local team for a future trial, may never appear in a headline. Yet those outcomes change lives and, over time, change societies. The Albies deserve credit for putting that slow work on a stage, but the real test is whether donors and readers follow through with support after the stage lights go off.

For Lynsey Addario, I think the lesson for readers is about consumption. We share conflict photographs quickly and move on quickly. That habit can exploit the people photographed and flatten the story. If an award for photojournalism means anything, it should slow readers down: read the full essay, learn the names and places, understand what happened before and after the photograph, and consider the safety of the people shown. Photography is evidence, not decoration. Treating it as decoration is a form of disrespect to the photographer and to the subjects.

Finally, I think readers should judge the Albies by a simple follow-up test. Six months after the ceremony, are the honorees safer, better funded, and more widely read? Are their organisations stronger? Are their issues still covered? If the answer is yes, the celebrity mechanism worked. If the answer is no, and only the red-carpet photographs remain, then the criticism was right. This post gives readers the confirmed list so they can run that test themselves, by searching for each honoree's work in the months ahead, rather than accepting the ceremony as proof of impact. Justice work continues after awards night, and attention should continue with it.

Another angle worth considering is who is missing from the list, and what that absence tells us. The 2026 honorees include journalists, a girls' rights advocate, a photographer, an organisation and a corporate leader, but they do not include, for example, a judge, a whistleblower from inside a government, or a climate defender. That is not a criticism of the chosen winners; no single ceremony can cover every justice field. It is a reminder that an awards list is a selection, not a complete map of justice work. Readers who care about a missing field should not conclude that field is unimportant. They should look for other awards, funds and organisations that cover it, and ask why certain fields receive more celebrity attention than others. Judges and whistleblowers, for instance, often cannot accept public honours safely, which may explain their absence. Climate defenders may be honoured by environmental awards instead. Understanding selection helps readers use the Albies as one window into justice work, not as the whole building.

I also think the Albies raise a question about language. Terms like “honoree,” “defender” and “advocate” are used interchangeably in coverage, but they mean different things. A defender may face direct danger, an advocate may campaign from a safer position, and an honoree is simply a person selected by the foundation. Using those words precisely matters, because it prevents exaggerating one person's risk or minimising another's. Brad Smith, for example, is an advocate in a corporate sense, not a front-line defender facing detention. That distinction does not diminish his work, but it clarifies it. Similarly, a local organisation like Justice Plus is not a single advocate; it is a team, a structure and a record-keeping system. Precise language helps readers give each winner the right kind of respect, rather than the same generic praise for very different work.
