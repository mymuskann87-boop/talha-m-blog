## Why Placement Beats a New Router, at First

Wi-Fi is radio, and radio weakens with distance and obstacles, no matter how expensive the router is. A powerful router hidden in a corner, behind a television, inside a closed cabinet, or on the floor in a far room will perform worse than a modest router placed centrally, openly and at a sensible height. Before buying a new router, extender or mesh system, moving the existing router is free, takes minutes, and often produces the largest single improvement. Many "slow internet" complaints are actually weak-signal complaints: the broadband connection is fine, but the Wi-Fi signal reaching the device is weak, unstable or crowded.

Think of the router as a light bulb trying to illuminate a home. A bulb in a corner cupboard lights that corner brightly and leaves distant rooms dim, while a bulb in a central hallway lights everywhere more evenly. Wi-Fi behaves similarly, though walls, floors and metal objects block it more unevenly than light. The goal is not to maximise signal in the room containing the router, but to minimise the worst signal in the rooms where people actually work, study and stream. That shift in goal — from "strong near the router" to "adequate everywhere used" — changes placement decisions immediately.

Test before and after any move, so improvement is measured, not imagined. Use a speed-test app or website on the same device, in the same spots, at a similar time of day, and note download speed and stability, not just a single peak figure. Also note practical results: does video stop buffering, does a video call stop freezing, does a page load without retrying? A placement change that raises a speed test by a small amount but ends buffering in the bedroom is a success, while a change that raises a test near the router but leaves the bedroom unchanged is not. Measure where it matters.

## The Central, High, Open Rule

The most reliable starting position is central in the home, at about chest to head height, in open air, away from thick walls and large metal objects. Central placement reduces the maximum distance to any room, which is more important than perfect distance to one room. Height helps because furniture, people and low obstacles block signals less when the router is elevated, and because many devices are used at table or sofa height rather than floor level. Open air matters because a closed cabinet, especially metal or dense wood, absorbs and reflects signal, while an open shelf lets it spread.

Avoid the usual bad spots, even when they are convenient for cabling. A router on the floor collects dust, sits behind furniture, and sends much of its signal into the floor. A router behind a television competes with a large metal and electronic screen. A router in a far corner, chosen because the broadband socket is there, starves the opposite side of the home. A router next to a microwave, baby monitor, cordless phone base or large metal appliance faces interference and blocking. If the broadband socket forces a corner position, a longer Ethernet cable to a central spot, or a mesh node in the centre, is worth considering, because socket convenience should not decide whole-home coverage.

In multi-storey homes, placement becomes three-dimensional. A router on the ground floor in one corner will struggle to reach an upstairs far bedroom through two floors and walls. A central position on the middle floor, or a router on the ground floor with a wired access point or mesh node upstairs, usually works better than simply buying a stronger single router. Stairwells and open hallways help signals travel between floors, because they offer a less obstructed path, so a position near a stairwell often outperforms a position inside a closed room, even if the latter looks tidier.

## Walls, Floors, Water, Metal and Interference

Different materials block Wi-Fi very differently, and knowing them explains many dead zones. Thin plaster walls reduce signal modestly, brick and concrete reduce it significantly, and metal — including metal doors, filing cabinets, mirrors with metallic backing, aquariums and large appliances — can block or reflect it sharply. Water also absorbs Wi-Fi, which is why a large aquarium, a water tank, or a bathroom with tiled walls and pipes can create a shadow behind it. If a dead zone lies directly behind a bathroom, kitchen or metal cabinet, the obstacle, not the router's power, is the likely cause, and moving either the router or the device's usual position around that obstacle may help.

Electronic interference is a second, invisible obstacle. Microwaves, Bluetooth devices, baby monitors and neighbouring Wi-Fi networks all share crowded radio space, especially on the 2.4 GHz band. A router placed next to a microwave will suffer whenever the microwave runs, which explains the mysterious pattern of video buffering only at cooking time. Neighbouring networks in flats and terraced houses create congestion that placement alone cannot fully solve, though moving the router away from a shared wall and choosing a less crowded channel can help. Modern routers often choose channels automatically, but a manual check after a move can reveal whether a neighbouring network dominates the channel your router uses.

Device position matters as much as router position. A laptop with its lid nearly closed, a phone in a thick metal case, or a device behind a person's body will receive less signal than the same device in open air. A television pushed against a wall, with its Wi-Fi antenna at the back, may struggle while a phone beside it works fine. When diagnosing, test with the device in its normal use position, not held high in the air near the router, because that test flatters the result. Small changes — moving a desk a metre, opening a door, or removing a metal case — sometimes fix a problem that looked like a router failure.

## Bands, Channels and Router Settings That Help

Most modern routers offer two main bands: 2.4 GHz, which travels farther and through walls better but is slower and more crowded, and 5 GHz, which is faster over short distances but weaker through walls. Many routers combine them under one network name and steer devices automatically, which is convenient but can cause a device to cling to a weak 5 GHz signal when a stronger 2.4 GHz signal would work better, or vice versa. If a distant room struggles, check which band the device uses there, and consider separate network names for the two bands if your router allows it, so you can deliberately connect distant devices to 2.4 GHz and nearby devices to 5 GHz.

Channel selection is worth a brief look, especially in flats. Wi-Fi channels overlap, and a crowded channel behaves like a busy road: many networks sharing it slow each other, even if each signal is strong. A Wi-Fi analyser app can show which channels neighbours use, and choosing a less crowded channel, or letting the router auto-select after a restart, can improve stability. Avoid changing advanced settings you do not understand — transmit power, beamforming toggles, or QoS rules — without noting the original value, because a wrong change can worsen performance and be hard to reverse. Change one setting at a time and test.

Firmware updates, a restart schedule, and a sensible password also affect performance indirectly. Outdated firmware can contain bugs that cause drops, while a periodic restart clears memory leaks that accumulate over weeks. A weak or shared password allows neighbours or visitors' devices to consume bandwidth unknowingly, so a strong, private password and a separate guest network for visitors protect both speed and security. These steps cost nothing, take minutes, and prevent a common pattern: blaming placement for a problem caused by an overloaded, outdated or openly shared router.

## Extenders, Mesh and Wiring: When Placement Is Not Enough

If central placement still leaves a far room weak, the next step depends on home size and construction. A Wi-Fi extender repeats the signal, but it also halves effective speed in many setups and creates a second network name that devices may cling to, so it is best for a single small dead zone, not whole-home coverage. A mesh system, with two or three nodes that share one network name and hand devices between nodes, works better for larger homes, because devices roam more smoothly. Mesh nodes still need good placement: a node placed in the dead zone itself will have a weak signal to repeat, while a node placed halfway between router and dead zone, with a decent signal of its own, performs far better.

Wiring, where feasible, is the most reliable upgrade of all. An Ethernet cable from the router to a distant room, to a mesh node, or to a work computer removes Wi-Fi weakness entirely for that device and frees wireless capacity for phones and tablets. Powerline adapters, which carry network signals through household electrical wiring, are a compromise when running a cable is impossible, but their performance varies with wiring quality and circuit layout, so buy from a seller with a return option and test in your actual sockets. For a home office, a single wired connection often solves video-call freezing more reliably than any wireless upgrade.

Do not buy mesh or extenders before testing placement and settings, and do not buy based on advertised coverage area alone, because those figures assume open space, not brick walls and metal obstacles. Buy for your home's actual worst room, test during a return window, and keep the receipt. A mesh system placed poorly is an expensive version of the same problem, while a single well-placed router with one well-placed wired node often outperforms three nodes placed for tidiness rather than signal.

## Working From Home and Video Calls: Stability Over Speed

Video calls expose Wi-Fi weakness differently from streaming, because streaming can buffer ahead while a live call cannot. A call needs stable, low-delay delivery in both directions: your voice and video must reach others without freezing, and theirs must reach you without breaking up. A connection that shows a high download speed on a test can still fail calls if upload speed is low, latency is high, or the signal drops briefly every few minutes. For home workers, upload speed and stability therefore matter more than headline download speed, and a placement change should be judged by call quality — frozen faces, robotic audio, dropped calls — not only by a speed test number.

Practical steps for call stability start with position. Work from the room with the most reliable signal, not necessarily the room with the fastest peak test, and keep the device's antenna unobstructed: a laptop lid open, a phone out of a thick case, and a clear line toward the router where possible. Close bandwidth-heavy applications during important calls, such as cloud backups, large downloads and other household streaming, because they compete for the same connection. If calls still freeze, a wired Ethernet connection to the work computer, even temporarily via a long cable during call hours, often solves the problem immediately and reveals whether Wi-Fi, rather than the broadband service, was the cause. That diagnosis alone saves money, because it prevents buying a faster plan to fix a wireless problem.

Household scheduling is the final, often ignored factor. In a home where several people stream, game and call simultaneously, especially in the evening, the connection is shared, and no placement change creates extra total capacity. Agreeing on simple rules — pausing large downloads during a work call, using wired connections for the heaviest user, or staggering 4K streaming during meetings — improves everyone's experience without cost. A router with quality-of-service settings can prioritise calls, but household agreement is more reliable than a setting that is configured once and forgotten. Stable calls are a household outcome, not a single-device achievement, and placement, wiring and scheduling together produce them.

A simple diagnostic that saves many households money is to test speed with a cable directly into the router, then compare it with Wi-Fi in the problem room. If wired speed is good and Wi-Fi speed is poor, the problem is wireless placement, interference or device position, and a faster broadband plan will not fix it. If wired speed is also poor, the problem lies with the broadband service, the router, or the provider, and a support call with wired test results is far more persuasive than a complaint about Wi-Fi. That one comparison, done in ten minutes, directs every later decision correctly.

## My Take:

My take is that router placement is the most underrated free upgrade in the home, because it is invisible, boring, and therefore ignored in favour of buying something. People spend money on a faster broadband plan or a new router while the old router sits on the floor behind a television, and then wonder why the new plan feels no faster in the bedroom. The truth is unglamorous: radio needs open, central, elevated space, and no subscription can repeal that. Moving a router to a hallway shelf, opening a door, or running a longer cable is not a technical achievement, but it is the change that actually reaches the room where a child studies or a parent works, and that practical reach matters more than a specification.

I also think the "strong near the router" mindset misleads households. A speed test beside the router that shows a huge number feels reassuring, yet it tells you nothing about the far bedroom, the kitchen, or the home office where the connection is actually needed. I would advise every household to test in the worst spot, not the best spot, and to judge every change by that worst spot. That habit changes buying too: it stops people paying for peak speed they already have near the router, and starts them fixing the minimum speed where life happens. Good internet is not about a high maximum; it is about a reliable minimum everywhere it is used.

There is a fairness angle here as well. Families in larger, older, or brick-built homes, and in flats with many neighbouring networks, face harder Wi-Fi conditions than families in small, open-plan homes, yet they receive the same generic advice to "place the router centrally" without guidance on walls, water, metal and interference. Advice should acknowledge construction: a brick wall, a bathroom, a metal cabinet or a microwave is not a minor detail; it can be the entire explanation for a dead zone. Naming those obstacles helps people stop blaming their broadband provider or their device, and start moving a router, a desk, or a node around the real barrier.

Finally, I believe wiring deserves more respect than it gets in wireless marketing. We are told everything should be wireless, yet the most reliable home networks still use a wire for the important link — router to node, router to work computer, router to television — and wireless only for the last step to a phone or tablet. A single Ethernet cable, run tidily along a skirting board, can end years of video-call freezing and buffering, and it costs less than a mesh node. Wireless convenience is wonderful, but it should be built on a wired backbone where possible, not on hope that radio will pass through brick and metal. Placement first, wiring second, new hardware last: that order saves money and frustration in that sequence.

A further practical point is about testing honestly. Speed tests vary with time of day, server, device and background activity, so a single test proves little. Test at the same time, on the same device, in the same spot, with other streaming paused, and repeat twice. Note buffering and call freezing alongside numbers, because lived experience is the outcome that matters. A household that tests carefully will also notice patterns — buffering only when the microwave runs, drops only in one room, slowness only in the evening — and patterns point to causes. Without testing, every problem looks like "slow internet", and every solution looks like "buy something".

I would also add a note on shared living and neighbours. In flats, your Wi-Fi competes with dozens of networks, and courtesy helps everyone: use a strong password, avoid unnecessarily high transmit power that floods neighbours, and choose less crowded channels where possible. Your network is part of a shared radio environment, and a household that optimises only for itself while blasting maximum power can worsen congestion for everyone, including itself. A calmer, well-placed network with sensible settings is better for the building than a loud, poorly placed one, and that cooperative view is often missing from buying guides.

A final thought concerns renters and people who cannot move a broadband socket or drill for cables. For them, placement options are genuinely limited, and advice that assumes a homeowner's freedom can feel unhelpful. Renters can still use many of these ideas: a router on an open shelf rather than the floor, a longer cable run tidily along a skirting board with removable clips, a mesh node on a hallway table, and a wired connection for the work desk during call hours. Landlords, for their part, should consider central socket placement and permission for tidy cabling as a basic amenity, because reliable internet is now as essential as heating for work and study. Good placement should not require home ownership; it should be achievable with portable, reversible changes.

I would also add that internet providers should be clearer about the difference between broadband speed to the home and Wi-Fi speed inside the home. Many customers buy a faster plan expecting faster Wi-Fi in a far room, then feel misled when the far room barely changes, because the bottleneck was wireless, not broadband. Providers could help by explaining, at sale and in support, how to test wired speed versus Wi-Fi speed, and by offering placement and mesh guidance before upselling speed. That honesty would reduce complaints, returns and churn, and it would help customers spend on the fix that actually matches their problem, whether that is placement, wiring, mesh, or genuinely a faster plan.

I would also encourage households to keep a simple record of what they tried: router position, speed in the worst room, and whether buffering stopped. That record prevents repeating failed positions after a tidy-up moves the router, and it helps support calls, because a provider can see that placement, wiring and settings were already tested. Without a record, every problem feels new and every solution feels like guesswork. With a record, internet troubleshooting becomes a calm, evidence-based routine, and the household spends less time arguing about whether the internet "was better last week" and more time fixing the actual cause.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Where is the best place to put a Wi-Fi router?**
Centrally in the home, at chest to head height, in open air, away from thick walls, metal objects, aquariums and microwaves. The goal is adequate signal in rooms where people work and stream, not maximum signal beside the router.

**Does router height really affect Wi-Fi speed?**
Yes. An elevated, open position reduces blocking by furniture and people and spreads signal more evenly. A router on the floor behind furniture will perform worse than the same router on an open shelf.

**Why is Wi-Fi weak in one room only?**
A single weak room usually indicates an obstacle such as a brick wall, bathroom, kitchen, metal cabinet or aquarium between router and device, or interference on that side of the home. Moving the router, device position, or adding a halfway mesh node often helps.

**Should I use 2.4 GHz or 5 GHz Wi-Fi?**
Use 5 GHz nearby for speed and 2.4 GHz farther away for range through walls. If your router combines bands under one name and a distant device clings to a weak signal, separate network names can let you choose deliberately.

**Is a mesh system better than a Wi-Fi extender?**
Mesh usually works better for larger homes because nodes share one network and hand devices over smoothly, while extenders can halve speed and create a second network. Place any node halfway to the dead zone, not inside it.

**When should I use an Ethernet cable instead of Wi-Fi?**
Use a cable for a home office computer, a mesh node, or a television that buffers, because wiring removes Wi-Fi weakness entirely for that device and frees wireless capacity for phones and tablets.
