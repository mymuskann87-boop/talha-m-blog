The student's wallet was light, but the library card was heavy with possibility. It sat in a pocket next to a phone, a bus ticket, and a list of books the course required — a list whose total price was more than a month of food. The student looked at the list, then at the library building across the road, and walked toward the building. That walk, repeated by millions of students, is the oldest money-saving trick in education, and it still works.

Books are one of the largest costs of studying, and they arrive all at once at the start of a term, when money is tightest. New textbooks, reference books, and reading lists can cost more than the course fees in some cases. Yet much of what a student needs to learn is available free or cheaply, through libraries, legal free editions, sharing, and second-hand copies. Studying well does not require owning every book on the list. It requires access to the knowledge inside them, and access is often free.

## The library is the first stop, not the last resort

A public library lends books free of charge, and many college libraries lend course books too. Before buying anything, check whether the library holds it. Course textbooks are often kept in the library's reference section or in multiple copies for lending. Even when the exact edition is not available, an earlier edition usually covers the same core material, and the librarian can help you check what has changed.

Libraries offer more than books. They offer quiet study space, free internet in many places, newspapers, journals, and help from staff who know how to find things. A student who studies in the library also saves at home: no need for a quiet room, no extra electricity for long study hours, and fewer distractions than a shared bedroom.

The habit that matters is visiting early. At the start of term, the most wanted books are still on the shelves. Students who wait until the week before exams find empty shelves and long waiting lists. Go in the first week, borrow what you need, and note the return dates. A free book borrowed early beats an expensive book bought late.

## Classic books and open textbooks are legally free

A large part of human knowledge is free by law. Classic books whose copyright has expired — the works of old philosophers, scientists, and writers — are available legally free online from established digital libraries. Open textbooks, written by teachers and universities specifically to be free, cover many subjects from mathematics to history, and they are free to read, download, and print.

Use official free sources for these. Established digital libraries and university open-textbook projects are the safe places to look. Avoid shady sites that offer new textbooks for free, because those copies are usually pirated, and pirated copies can carry viruses, missing pages, or wrong content. Free does not mean stolen. The legal free sources are large enough that most students never need the shady ones.

For subjects where no open textbook exists, free video lessons and practice material from established educational sources can support the course. They work best as companions to the course book, not replacements for it. A video can explain a hard idea in ten minutes, and a practice set can test whether you truly understood it, but the course book remains the map of what the exam expects.

## Share, swap, and buy second-hand

No student needs to buy every book new. Share with classmates: if four students need the same reference book, one copy shared on a rota covers all four for most of the term. Swap books at the end of term: the book you finished is the book a junior student needs next, and their finished book may be the one you need now.

In my view, a swapped book carries more than its pages, because it passes knowledge from one student to the next without anyone paying full price twice.

Second-hand books are the next step. Shops, senior students, and online second-hand sellers offer used copies at a fraction of the new price. A used book with a few notes in the margin is not a worse book; sometimes the previous owner's notes make it a better one. When you finish, sell your own books on the same way, and the money comes back for the next term's list.

Buy new only when it truly matters: a workbook you must write in, a book with access codes the course requires, or a text that changes every year. For everything else, a shared, swapped, or second-hand copy teaches the same lesson.

## Plan the list before the term starts

The most expensive way to buy books is in a panic. The week before exams, with a list in hand and no time to search, students buy whatever the nearest shop has at whatever price it asks. Planning removes that panic.

When the reading list arrives, go through it with a pen. Mark each book as essential, useful, or optional. Essential books are the ones the course is built on. Useful books are worth borrowing. Optional books can wait until you know whether the course truly needs them. Then work through the free options first: library, legal free editions, sharing. Only the books left after that search deserve money, and they are usually far fewer than the original list.

Keep a small record of what you borrowed, from whom, and when it is due back. A lost borrowed book costs more than a bought one, because it damages trust as well as money. Return books on time, thank the lender, and the sharing circle stays open for the next term.

Before visiting, check the library catalogue online if your library offers one. A two-minute search from home tells you whether the book is on the shelf, on loan, or at another branch, and it saves a wasted trip. Note the shelf number before you go, and walk straight to it. Students who search first spend their library time reading, not wandering between shelves looking for a book that was never there.

## What free studying cannot replace

Honest limits matter here. Some courses require a specific new edition, because the syllabus, page numbers, or problem sets changed. In that case, check first before skipping the purchase. A wrong edition can cost marks, and marks are worth more than the saving.

Some subjects also need materials that cannot be borrowed: lab manuals, workbooks, or licensed software. Budget for those honestly, and save on everything else to make room for them. Free studying is about cutting the costs that can be cut, not pretending that all costs can disappear.

A final caution: never pay for a pirated copy or a "cheap" download from an unknown seller. Apart from the legal problem, pirated books are often incomplete, outdated, or infected with harmful files. The legal free sources, the library, and the second-hand shelf are safer, and they are enough for most students.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can I really study without buying all the course books?**

Yes, for most courses. Libraries lend course books free, classic works and open textbooks are legally free online, and sharing or buying second-hand covers much of the rest. Buy new only the books the course truly requires, such as workbooks or books with access codes.

**Are free books online legal?**

Many are. Classic books whose copyright has expired and open textbooks made by universities are legally free from established digital libraries. Avoid shady sites offering new textbooks free, because those copies are usually pirated and may be incomplete or harmful.

**Is an older edition good enough?**

Often, yes, but check first. For most subjects the core material changes little between editions. Ask your teacher whether the exam follows a specific edition's problem sets or page numbers before relying on an older copy.

**How do I share books fairly with classmates?**

Agree on a simple rota: who has the book which week, and when it passes on. Treat borrowed books carefully, return them on time, and keep a note of who lent what. A fair sharing circle lasts the whole course and saves everyone money.

**What should I do with books after the term ends?**

Sell them second-hand or swap them with junior students who need them next. The money you get back funds the next term's list, and the books keep teaching instead of gathering dust on a shelf.
