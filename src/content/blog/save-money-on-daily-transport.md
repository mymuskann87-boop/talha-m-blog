Seven in the morning. The notebook opens on the kitchen table, and the day's journeys get priced like items in a shop. The school run: one short ride. The trip to work: a longer one. The evening errand: a third. By dinner, the notebook tells a story most people never read about their own lives — that transport, bought in small daily pieces, is one of the biggest bills in the month, and that it is bought without ever being added up.

Nobody thinks of a single ride as expensive. It is a small amount, paid quickly, forgotten instantly. But a month of small rides is a large amount, paid slowly, and forgotten completely. The notebook fixes that. One day of honest prices is enough to see where the money goes, and once it is seen, it can be changed.

## The walk that costs nothing and pays twice

Start with the shortest trips, because they are the easiest wins. Any regular trip under fifteen or twenty minutes on foot is a candidate for walking, and the price of walking is exactly zero. The corner shop, the nearby school, the neighbour's house, the bus stop one stop early — these are all trips people pay for out of habit, not need.

In my view, short walks are money and mood at the same time, because the walker arrives awake, unhurried, and slightly happier than the person who sat in traffic for the same distance. A person who walks two short trips a day saves the fare twice a day, and over a month that is a visible sum, earned by doing something the body wanted anyway. The honest catch is weather and time. Walking in heavy rain or when already late is not smart. The rule is simple: walk the short trips when the day allows it, and pay for the ride when it does not. A habit with exceptions still saves far more than no habit at all.

## The monthly pass versus the daily ticket

For the regular route — the one taken five or six days a week — the maths deserves five quiet minutes. A daily ticket looks cheap. A monthly pass looks expensive. But multiply the daily ticket by the number of working days, and compare it honestly with the pass price. On a route taken almost daily, the pass usually wins, sometimes by a wide margin. On a route taken twice a week, the daily ticket usually wins, and the pass would be a waste.

The key word is "your" route. Someone else's answer means nothing. Count your own trips for two normal weeks, multiply, and let the numbers decide. And remember the pass has a second value: it removes the daily decision. No fumbling for change, no queue at the counter, no day when the fare quietly rises without you noticing. The pass turns transport from a daily purchase into a monthly fact, and monthly facts are easier to budget around.

Public transport itself deserves a plain definition here, because it is the backbone of cheap travel: it is transport shared by many people on fixed routes — buses, trains, and similar services — which is exactly why it costs so little per person. One vehicle, many passengers, the cost split dozens of ways. A private ride can never compete with that arithmetic on a busy route.

## Sharing the ride, splitting the fuel

For routes where public transport is weak, the next cheapest seat is someone else's car — shared. Colleagues who live near each other, neighbours whose children go to the same school, friends heading to the same market: one car, two or three or four people, the fuel split between them. Everyone pays less than driving alone, and the car spends less time on the road, which is a small kindness to the whole street.

Sharing works only with a little organisation. Agree the pickup times. Agree how the fuel money is split — weekly is simplest, in a small shared pot or a quick transfer. And agree what happens when someone is sick or late, so one late morning does not end the arrangement. The best car shares are boringly reliable: same time, same people, same split, week after week. Boring is good. Boring saves money.

## The bicycle, and the one-trip errand rule

A bicycle is the middle child of transport: faster than walking, far cheaper than any motor. On short daily trips — the school run, the market, the nearby office — a bicycle pays for itself over time, because each ride replaces a fare or a fuel cost. It needs a safe place to park and a little care, and the rider needs the basics of safety: a helmet, lights for dark evenings, and roads that are actually rideable. Where the roads are dangerous, the bicycle is not a saving; it is a risk, and no saving is worth that.

Then there is the smallest habit of all, the one-trip rule. Instead of five small trips scattered across the week — one for the pharmacy, one for the market, one for the bank — batch them. One outing, one route, several errands in order. Every errand trip avoided is a fare or a fuel cost that never happened, and the day feels organised instead of chopped up. The notebook loves this habit. The week with batched errands is visibly cheaper than the week without them.

Keep the notebook going for a full month, not just a day. A single day can mislead — a rainy day, a holiday, a sick child — but a month shows the true pattern: which routes repeat, which fares add up, and which occasional splurge is actually rare. At month's end, add the transport column up and compare it with the food column. Many people are surprised to find transport rivals food, and that surprise is the moment the saving habit truly starts, because the number is theirs, not a stranger's advice.

## The honest limit: time is also money

Here is the part a saving guide must not hide. A cheaper journey that takes two extra hours a day is not a saving for everyone. A person who reaches work exhausted after three changes of bus has paid with their energy, and energy is a real currency too. So the rule is balance: take the cheaper option when it costs a little extra time, and pay for the faster option when the time matters more — a job interview, a sick child, a day that is already full. Transport saving is about the average week, not about winning every single trip. The person who walks the short trips, holds a pass for the daily route, and pays for a fast ride twice a month has the best of both, and the notebook will prove it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is a monthly pass always cheaper than daily tickets?**

No. It is cheaper only if you travel the route often enough. Count your trips for two normal weeks, multiply the daily fare, and compare it with the pass price. The answer belongs to your route, not to anyone else's.

**What if public transport does not reach my area?**

Then sharing rides is usually the next best option — neighbours, colleagues, or a regular car share. Batching errands into one trip also helps a lot, because every avoided trip is money saved whatever vehicle you use.

**Is a bicycle really cheaper than a bus?**

On short daily trips, yes, over time, because the bicycle has no fare and no fuel. But count the real costs honestly: the bicycle itself, basic maintenance, a lock, and a helmet. And only ride where the roads are safe enough.

**How do I start a car share without awkwardness?**

Start with one person and one regular trip, such as the morning work run. Agree the fuel split in advance — weekly is simplest — and keep it friendly and exact. Money arguments end car shares faster than traffic does.

**Does walking short trips really add up?**

Yes. Two short paid trips a day, replaced by walking, saves two fares a day, and across a month that is a sum you can see. The saving is small per trip and large per month, which is the pattern of almost all transport saving.
