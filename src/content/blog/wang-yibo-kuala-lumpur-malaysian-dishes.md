## Dish One: Claypot Bak Kut Teh in Pudu

Bak kut teh is one of Malaysia's most beloved dishes, and its name tells you much of its story. It is a soup of pork ribs simmered slowly with herbs and spices, traditionally eaten with rice, and often served with tea, pickled vegetables, or fried dough sticks for dipping. The Malaysian version is known for its rich, dark broth, made by simmering pork ribs with a blend of Chinese medicinal herbs and spices. It is warm, slightly sweet, deeply savoury, and comforting in a way that few soups manage. It is also recognised as part of Malaysia's national food heritage, which means it is not just a meal. It is a cultural marker, a dish that tells a story about migration, community, and home cooking.

The claypot detail matters. Serving bak kut teh in a claypot keeps it bubbling hot at the table, and the heat deepens the flavour as the meal goes on. It also changes the social feeling of the meal. A claypot sits in the middle of the table, everyone reaches in, everyone shares, and the meal becomes communal rather than individual. For a racing team, that is a perfect choice. A team that eats together, sharing from the same pot, builds trust in a way that separate plates do not. Photographs showing Wang relaxed at that table therefore tell fans something real about team life, not just about food. He was not eating alone for a photograph. He was eating with his people, after a day of work.

Pudu matters too. Pudu is a well-known Kuala Lumpur neighbourhood with a long food history, busy markets, and local eateries that serve residents as much as tourists. Choosing Pudu, rather than a hotel restaurant or a private dining room, suggests a desire for a real local meal, not a staged celebrity dinner. Fans noticed that, and they liked it. A star who eats where locals eat, sits at a normal table, and enjoys a normal dish feels closer than a star who only appears in luxury settings. That closeness is part of why the photographs spread so quickly. They did not show glamour. They showed appetite, relaxation, and belonging, three things fans love to see in a hardworking idol.

For readers who have never tried bak kut teh, here is what to expect if you order it yourself. The broth is the star, so taste it first, before adding sauces. The ribs should be tender enough to fall from the bone, but still have texture. Rice is not a side dish here. It is part of the dish, soaking up broth and balancing the richness. If the restaurant offers different herbal strengths, a first-timer should start with a standard version, not the strongest herbal mix. And if you are sharing a claypot, let it bubble for a few minutes at the table. Patience is part of the flavour, and rushing a claypot meal misses half the experience.

## Dish Two: Durian at Jalan Alor

Durian is the opposite of a safe choice, and that is why it became the headline of the night. Durian is a large, spiky fruit, famous for its strong smell and its rich, creamy flesh. People either love it instantly or need time to understand it. There is very little middle ground, which is why durian stories always travel fast. A celebrity trying durian is not just eating fruit. They are taking a test, in public, with fans watching to see whether they love it, tolerate it, or quietly put it down. Wang Yibo sampled durian at Jalan Alor, and the fact that fans shared the moment so widely suggests they enjoyed watching a familiar star meet Malaysia's most famous fruit.

Jalan Alor is the right stage for that test. It is a famous food street in Bukit Bintang, Kuala Lumpur's busy shopping and entertainment district, lined with stalls and restaurants that stay lively late into the night. Visitors go there to graze, to try many small things, to watch street life, and to eat outdoors in the warm air. It is loud, colourful, crowded, and delicious, and it is very different from a quiet restaurant meal. Moving from a claypot dinner in Pudu to a durian stop at Jalan Alor in the same evening shows a classic Kuala Lumpur food journey: a proper sit-down meal first, then a street-food adventure afterwards, when the city is at its most alive.

Durian itself deserves a fair description, because many readers outside Southeast Asia have only heard jokes about its smell. The smell is strong, yes, and it can fill a room. But the flesh, when ripe, is creamy, sweet, and complex, with flavours that fans compare to custard, almond, and tropical fruit, all at once. Different varieties taste different, and Musang King is one of Malaysia's most famous varieties, prized for its rich flavour and creamy texture. This article does not claim that Wang Yibo tried a specific variety, because the reporting used here says only that he sampled durian, without naming a variety. Fans who want to follow his path should try a small portion first, at a reputable stall, and let the seller recommend a variety for beginners.

There is also a practical side to durian that first-timers should know. Durian is filling, so a small portion is enough for a first taste. It is best eaten fresh, not rushed, and it pairs well with a drink afterwards. Some people find the smell stronger than the taste, and that is normal. If you try durian at Jalan Alor, go with friends, share one portion, and treat it as an experience rather than a challenge. The goal is not to prove bravery. The goal is to understand why millions of people love a fruit that the rest of the world finds so strange. Wang Yibo's stop, brief as it was, introduced that question to many new fans, and that is a gift to Malaysian food culture.
