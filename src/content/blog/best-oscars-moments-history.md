The Academy Awards, known as the Oscars, have been held since 1929, and their history is also a history of cinema itself. Over nearly a century, the ceremony has produced moments of joy, surprise, controversy, and cultural change that viewers remember long after the winners' lists fade. Some moments matter because they recognised a groundbreaking film. Others matter because a speech gave voice to a wider movement, because an unexpected winner challenged expectations, or because a live mistake revealed how fragile a grand ceremony can be. This guide looks at the kinds of Oscars moments that have lasted, explains why they matter, and helps new viewers understand the ceremony's place in film culture.

## What the Oscars Are and Why They Matter

The Oscars are presented by the Academy of Motion Picture Arts and Sciences, a professional organisation whose members vote for films and performances they consider outstanding in a given year. Awards cover acting, directing, writing, music, cinematography, editing, design, and the overall Best Picture, among other categories. Winning an Oscar does not automatically make a film the best-loved film of its year, and many excellent films never win, but an Oscar win brings prestige, attention, and often a new audience, especially for smaller or international films that might otherwise reach fewer viewers.

The ceremony matters culturally because it is one of the few occasions when the film industry speaks to a global audience about itself. Speeches, tributes, performances, and even fashion become part of public conversation, and the ceremony's choices are debated as statements about what cinema values at a given moment. When the Academy recognises a film from another country, a first-time director, or a story about a marginalised community, that recognition can influence which stories get funded and seen in following years. When it overlooks such films, that omission also becomes part of the conversation, and critics and audiences respond accordingly.

For viewers, the Oscars are best understood as a mixture of celebration, industry event, and television show. The ceremony aims to honour craft, to entertain a large audience, and to present the film industry at its most glamorous, and those aims sometimes conflict. A long, formal ceremony may honour craft thoroughly but feel slow to television viewers. A fast, entertainment-focused ceremony may feel lively but may rush through technical awards that filmmakers value deeply. Understanding that tension helps viewers enjoy the ceremony without expecting it to be a perfect ranking of artistic merit.

## Speeches That Became History

Acceptance speeches are the Oscars' most personal moments, because they turn a formal award into a human story. The most remembered speeches usually share qualities: they are sincere, specific, and connected to something larger than the winner's career. A winner who thanks a parent, a teacher, or a community that supported them gives the audience a glimpse of the long path behind a brief moment of fame. A winner who uses the stage to speak about representation, workers' rights, war, or the environment gives the ceremony a relevance beyond cinema, and those speeches are often quoted for years afterwards.

Some speeches are remembered for brevity and humour. A short, witty speech that acknowledges the absurdity of a grand ceremony while still showing gratitude can become iconic precisely because it breaks the expected pattern of long thank-you lists. Other speeches are remembered for emotion, when a winner who has waited decades, overcome illness, or returned after a long absence finally reaches the stage. Those moments resonate because they remind viewers that behind every statuette is a working life with setbacks, persistence, and support from others.

Speeches also reveal changing attitudes. Early Oscars speeches were often brief and formal, reflecting a more reserved industry culture. Later speeches became more personal, political, and inclusive, reflecting broader social change and a greater willingness among artists to speak openly. Viewers watching old speeches today can trace that change, noticing how language, topics, and who gets to speak have evolved. That evolution is part of why speech compilations remain popular: they are not only about film, but about how public speech itself has changed over nearly a century.

## Surprise Wins and Upsets

Surprise wins are the Oscars' most discussed moments, because they challenge predictions and reveal how voting works. Before each ceremony, critics, audiences, and betting markets form a consensus about likely winners, based on earlier awards, critical praise, and box office. When a less favoured film or performer wins, the surprise generates headlines, debate, and sometimes delight or disappointment that lasts for years. Upsets remind viewers that the Oscars are voted by thousands of Academy members with different tastes, not decided by a single panel or by popularity alone.

Some upsets are remembered because they recognised a film that later became a classic, while a more favoured film faded. In hindsight, such wins look prescient, as if the Academy saw lasting value that contemporary hype missed. Other upsets are remembered because they remain controversial, with viewers arguing that a different film deserved the award. Both kinds of memory are valuable, because they show that artistic judgement is not fixed. A film's reputation changes over time, as new audiences discover it, as cultural attitudes shift, and as later films build on its ideas. An Oscars upset therefore becomes a starting point for re-evaluation, not a final verdict.

It is also worth noting that surprise wins often involve Best Picture, the ceremony's final and most prestigious award, because that category carries the greatest symbolic weight. A surprise in a technical or short-film category may matter greatly to filmmakers, but it rarely dominates public discussion. A surprise Best Picture win, by contrast, becomes shorthand for a year in film history, and it is debated in articles, classrooms, and fan discussions for decades. That disproportionate attention is a reminder that the Oscars, like all awards, are experienced through their most visible moments, even though their full value lies in recognising many crafts.

## Milestones for Representation and International Cinema

Some of the most important Oscars moments are milestones that expanded who could be recognised. For much of its early history, the Academy's winners reflected a narrow range of American studio filmmaking, and performers, directors, and stories from other backgrounds were often overlooked. Over time, milestones challenged that pattern: first wins for performers from underrepresented groups, recognition for films in languages other than English, and Best Picture wins for international productions. Each milestone mattered not only to the winner, but to audiences who saw themselves, their language, or their country represented on a global stage for the first time.

International milestones deserve particular attention. When a non-English-language film wins major awards, it signals that cinematic excellence is not confined to one language or industry, and it often brings that film, and its country's cinema, to viewers who might not otherwise seek it out. Similarly, when a film from a smaller industry wins, it can influence funding, distribution, and festival attention for other films from that region. Those effects extend far beyond a single ceremony night, and they explain why international Oscars moments are celebrated with particular intensity in the winner's home country.

Representation milestones also invite critical reflection. A single win does not erase a history of exclusion, and later ceremonies are often judged by whether progress continues or stalls. Viewers and critics therefore treat milestones as both celebrations and benchmarks: they honour the achievement, while asking what still needs to change in voting, membership, and the kinds of stories that reach major studios. That dual response, celebration plus scrutiny, is healthy for an awards system that claims to represent an entire art form, and it keeps the Oscars relevant as a subject of public debate rather than merely a night of glamour.

## Performances and Tributes on Stage

Musical performances and tribute segments are another source of lasting Oscars moments. When a nominated song is performed live, the performance can elevate the song, introduce it to a wider audience, or become more memorable than the award itself. Performances that reinterpret a song, combine artists from different traditions, or stage a song with striking visuals often generate discussion and replay online. Conversely, a performance that feels disconnected from the film can fall flat, reminding viewers that the ceremony must balance film celebration with live entertainment.

Tribute segments, including the In Memoriam section honouring film figures who died in the previous year, carry a different emotional weight. They remind viewers of cinema's human history, connecting current stars to earlier generations of actors, directors, writers, and craftspeople. The choice of who is included, what images are shown, and what music accompanies them is often scrutinised, because omissions can feel like oversights. At their best, tributes are moving and inclusive, honouring well-known stars alongside editors, composers, and other craftspeople whose work shaped films without public fame. At their worst, they feel rushed, which itself becomes a talking point about how the industry values its own history.

Presenters also create memorable moments, through humour, chemistry, or unexpected pairings. A well-chosen presenting duo can set a warm, witty tone for an award, while a joke that misfires can become notorious. Hosts, in particular, shape the ceremony's mood, balancing celebration with satire, and their opening monologues are often remembered as summaries of a year in film and in culture. The best hosting moments acknowledge the industry's flaws with affection rather than cruelty, and they include the audience in the joke rather than excluding parts of it.

## Mistakes, Controversies and Live Television

Because the Oscars are broadcast live, mistakes become part of history in a way pre-recorded events do not. The most famous example is the Best Picture envelope error, in which the wrong film was announced as the winner before the mistake was corrected on stage. That moment is remembered not only for its drama, but for how those involved handled it with grace under extraordinary pressure, and for what it revealed about live television: even the most rehearsed ceremony can go wrong, and the response to error matters as much as the error itself. Viewers still discuss that night as a lesson in composure, accountability, and the human side of a polished event.

Other controversies have shaped the Oscars more gradually, through debate rather than a single incident. Campaigning practices, voting rules, the balance between popular blockbusters and smaller films, and the representation of Academy membership have all been subjects of sustained criticism and reform. The Academy has changed its membership, voting procedures, and eligibility rules over time in response to such debates, although each change generates new discussion about whether it goes far enough or too far. For viewers, these controversies are a reminder that awards are institutions, shaped by rules and interests, not pure reflections of artistic truth.

Controversial moments involving winners, presenters, or incidents on stage also become part of Oscars history, and they are often discussed in the context of wider cultural debates about behaviour, accountability, and media coverage. This guide does not single out such incidents for sensational retelling, because their details are widely documented elsewhere and because the most useful lesson for new viewers is general: live ceremonies amplify both celebration and controversy, and public reaction often says as much about the moment's cultural context as about the incident itself. Approaching such moments with that perspective helps viewers understand why some Oscars nights are remembered for debate rather than for winners.

## How to Watch the Oscars as a New Viewer

For a new viewer, the Oscars are most enjoyable when approached as a celebration to sample rather than a test to pass. You do not need to have seen every nominated film to enjoy the ceremony. Watching a few nominated films beforehand, especially in major categories such as Best Picture, acting, and directing, gives context, but even without that preparation, speeches, performances, and montages offer an engaging introduction to a year in cinema. Many viewers watch with friends, follow live commentary, or catch highlights afterwards, and each approach has merits: live viewing offers shared surprise, while highlights offer a concise, curated experience.

It helps to know the major categories before watching. Best Picture honours the overall film, including production. Acting awards honour individual performances in leading and supporting roles. Directing honours the director's vision and craft. Writing awards distinguish original screenplays from adapted screenplays. Technical awards honour cinematography, editing, sound, visual effects, production design, costume design, and makeup, among others. Music awards honour original scores and songs. Understanding those distinctions makes the ceremony less confusing, because a film can win in technical categories without winning Best Picture, and a performer can win for a supporting role that is central to a film's impact despite limited screen time.

Finally, treat Oscars results as a starting point for discovery, not as a final ranking. If a winning film does not appeal to you, that does not mean your taste is wrong. If a losing film becomes your favourite, that is a valid response, shared by many viewers and critics over time. The most rewarding way to use the Oscars is to note winners and nominees that intrigue you, watch a few you missed, and form your own view of a year in cinema. Over years, that habit builds a personal film education that is broader and more enjoyable than any single ceremony's results.

## My Take:

My own view is that the Oscars are most valuable not as a ranking, but as a record. A list of winners and nominees, read across decades, tells a story about what the film industry valued, what it overlooked, and how those values changed. That record is imperfect, and it contains omissions that later generations rightly criticise, but it is still a useful map. When I look back at a year through its Oscars, I do not only see which film won Best Picture. I see which performances were honoured, which countries were recognised, which crafts were celebrated, and which stories were considered important enough for a global stage. That broader picture is, in my opinion, the ceremony's real contribution to film history.

I also believe that speeches matter more than most viewers admit. A statuette is a symbol, but a speech is a statement, and statements endure in a way symbols alone do not. The speeches I remember are not the longest or the most polished; they are the ones where a winner spoke specifically and sincerely, about a person, a community, or a cause that shaped their work. Those speeches remind me that films are made by people with histories, families, and beliefs, not by studios alone. They also remind me that a global stage can be used for more than gratitude, and that artists who use it thoughtfully can influence public conversation far beyond cinema. I therefore pay close attention to speeches, even when I have not seen the winning film.

Another opinion I hold is about upsets. I enjoy upsets, even when I disagree with them, because they prove that voting is genuinely uncertain and that consensus is not destiny. A ceremony where every predicted winner wins would be predictable and dull, however accurate its predictions. An upset, by contrast, forces viewers to reconsider a film, to watch something they dismissed, or to argue about merit with friends. That argument is part of the pleasure of awards season. It turns passive viewing into active discussion, and it keeps film culture lively. I would rather have a surprising, debatable Oscars than a perfectly predictable one, because debate is a sign that people care about cinema.

I am also somewhat sceptical about the Oscars' ability to represent all of cinema. The Academy's membership, voting system, and eligibility rules shape its choices, and those choices have historically favoured certain kinds of films, particularly English-language studio productions and dramas with clear awards narratives. That bias does not invalidate individual wins, many of which are richly deserved, but it does mean viewers should not treat the Oscars as a complete canon. Some of the greatest films ever made never won major Oscars, and some winners have faded while their fellow nominees endured. A viewer who only watches Oscar winners will miss much of cinema's range, including genre films, international films, and experimental work that the Academy has been slower to honour.

I have a further view about international milestones. I regard them as the most encouraging development in recent Oscars history, because they challenge the assumption that major awards must go to American or English-language films. When an international film wins Best Picture or major acting and directing awards, it does more than honour one production. It signals to distributors, funders, and audiences that stories from other languages and cultures can succeed globally, and it encourages viewers to explore cinemas they might otherwise ignore. That expansion of attention is, in my view, the Oscars' most important cultural function today, more important than any single winner in a given year.

I also think the ceremony itself deserves appreciation as a piece of live television craft. Producing a long, global broadcast that balances awards, performances, tributes, and humour, while handling surprises and mistakes live, is an enormous undertaking. The ceremony's flaws, such as length, pacing, or uneven jokes, are real, but they should be judged against that difficulty. When the ceremony works, it creates a shared cultural moment that few other television events still achieve: millions of people watching the same speeches, performances, and surprises at the same time, and discussing them together afterwards. In an age of fragmented viewing, that shared experience has value in itself.

I would add that viewers should approach Oscars controversies with proportion. A single incident, however widely discussed, should not define an entire ceremony or an entire year in cinema, and a single win or loss should not define a filmmaker's career. The most thoughtful response to controversy is to ask what it reveals about institutions, expectations, and cultural context, rather than to reduce it to a viral clip. That approach does not excuse poor behaviour or flawed systems, but it keeps attention on the larger questions those moments raise, such as representation, accountability, and how live media shapes public judgement.

I would also add that technical awards deserve more viewer attention than they usually receive. Categories such as cinematography, editing, sound, and production design shape how a film feels, yet they are often rushed or treated as minor compared with acting and Best Picture. When I watch the Oscars now, I try to notice those crafts, because understanding them changes how I watch films throughout the year. A beautifully lit scene, a precisely edited sequence, or a soundscape that builds tension without music all represent years of skill, and honouring them properly is part of what the ceremony should do, even if television audiences prefer shorter segments.

Finally, my recommendation for new viewers is to treat the Oscars as an invitation. Watch the ceremony, or its highlights, with curiosity rather than with a checklist of predictions. Note the films and performers that intrigue you, watch a few you missed, and form your own view over time. Keep a personal list of winners and nominees you loved, regardless of whether they won, and revisit it as your taste develops. The Oscars will continue to surprise, disappoint, and inspire in roughly equal measure, and that mixture is precisely why they remain worth watching. A perfect awards system would be less interesting than this imperfect, human, and historically rich ceremony, and I would not trade its surprises for predictability.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What are the Oscars in simple terms?
The Oscars are awards presented by the Academy of Motion Picture Arts and Sciences to honour outstanding films and performances each year. Categories include Best Picture, acting, directing, writing, music and technical crafts, voted on by Academy members.

### When did the Academy Awards begin?
The Academy Awards began in 1929, making them one of the oldest film awards in the world. Their history spans nearly a century of cinema, from early studio films to modern international productions.

### What is the most famous Oscars mistake?
The most famous mistake was a Best Picture envelope error in which the wrong film was initially announced as winner before the error was corrected live on stage. It is remembered for its drama and for how those involved handled the correction.

### Why do Oscars winners sometimes surprise people?
Surprises happen because thousands of Academy members vote with different tastes, and because predictions based on earlier awards or popularity do not always match the final vote. A surprise win often sparks debate and re-evaluation of the winning film.

### Have non-English-language films won major Oscars?
Yes, non-English-language films have won major Oscars, including Best Picture in recent years. Such milestones are significant because they broaden global attention to international cinema and challenge assumptions about which films can succeed.

### How should a new viewer watch the Oscars?
Watch a few nominated films beforehand if possible, learn the major categories, and treat results as discovery prompts rather than final rankings. Live viewing offers shared surprise, while highlights offer a concise introduction to a year in cinema.
