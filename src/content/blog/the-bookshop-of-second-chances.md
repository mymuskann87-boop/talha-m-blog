Felix had owned the bookshop in the arcade for eleven years, and he knew every shelf the way a sailor knows a coastline. The shop was narrow and deep, with wooden shelves that reached the ceiling, a ladder that rolled on a brass rail, and a bell over the door that rang whenever anyone came in from the rain. Felix was forty-one, wore jumpers with leather patches at the elbows, and believed, firmly, that a second-hand book was better than a new one, because a second-hand book had already been loved by someone, and love, in his view, improved paper the way it improved people.

On a Tuesday in October, which was the quietest day of the week, Felix found the first note. It was written in pencil, in the margin of a battered paperback about the sea, beside a passage about a lighthouse keeper who never left his island. The note said, in small, neat handwriting: "I have never seen the sea. Have you?"

Felix stared at it for a long time. He had sold thousands of books, and he had found many things inside them: train tickets, photographs, shopping lists, a pressed flower, once a twenty-pound note. He had never found a question before. He put the book back on the shelf, in its proper place, and told himself he would forget about it by closing time.

He did not forget about it. He thought about it while he made tea. He thought about it while he shelved returns. He thought about it all the way home, walking through the arcade with the rain coming down outside, wondering who had written it, and why, and whether the person who wrote it had ever found an answer.

## The Second Note In The Margin

The second note appeared nine days later, in a different book, on a different shelf. It was a collection of poems about winter, and beside a poem about snow falling on an empty road, someone had written, in the same small, neat pencil: "I walk this road every morning. It is never empty when I think of it this way."

Felix sat down on the little stool behind the counter and read the line three times. The handwriting was the same. The pencil was the same. The habit was the same: a question, or a thought, left in the margin for no one in particular, as if the writer were talking to the book itself, and the book were listening.

Felix did something he had never done in eleven years of selling books. He answered. He took a pencil from the jar by the till, the sharp one he used for pricing, and wrote, very carefully, in the margin below the note: "Then the road is lucky. Some roads are empty even when they are full."

He closed the book. He put it back on the shelf. He felt foolish, and hopeful, and seventeen years old, all at once, and he told himself, firmly, that he was a grown man with a shop to run, and that grown men did not write notes to strangers in the margins of poetry books.

He checked the shelf first thing the next morning. The book was gone.

## The Woman Who Bought Poetry

The woman came back on a Friday, in the rain, with wet hair and a green coat that was too big for her. She went straight to the poetry shelf, as if she knew exactly where she was going, and she stood there for a long time, reading spines, touching covers, the way people touch books they are deciding whether to trust.

Felix watched her from the counter, pretending to sort receipts. She was perhaps thirty-five, with dark hair tied back, and glasses that kept slipping down her nose, and a way of reading a first page and smiling at it, privately, as if the book had said something only she could hear.

She brought three books to the counter. The poetry book was not among them. Felix wrapped them in brown paper, as he always did, and she paid, and she smiled, and she said, "You have a wonderful shop. It feels like the books choose you here, not the other way around."

"Thank you," Felix said, and then, before he could stop himself, "Do you ever write in the margins?"

The woman looked at him. Her smile did not leave her face, but it changed, the way a smile changes when it becomes a secret. "Only when a book asks me a question first," she said. "Goodbye, Mr. Bookshop."

"My name is Felix," Felix said, to the bell over the door, because she was already gone, out into the rain, with her three books in brown paper, and the arcade empty behind her.

## The Ticket Stub And The Confession

The third note was not in a margin. It was a train ticket, tucked into a novel about two people who miss each other by one train, every day, for a year. On the back of the ticket, in pencil, was written: "I missed my train once, on purpose, because the person next to me was telling a story I did not want to end. I never saw them again. I still think about the ending."

Felix read it twice. He put the ticket in his pocket. He carried it all day, in the pocket of his jumper, next to his heart, which was behaving in a way it had not behaved in years, which was to say, foolishly, and hopefully, and all at once.

That evening, after closing, Felix wrote a note of his own, on a card, and left it inside the novel, on the shelf, where the ticket had been. The card said: "If you ever miss a train on purpose again, I hope the story is worth it. And I hope you tell me the ending. — F."

He did not sleep well that night. He dreamed of trains, and margins, and green coats, and a woman who smiled at books as if they were old friends. In the morning, he went to the shop early, unlocked the door, and walked straight to the shelf. The novel was still there. The card was gone. In its place, tucked between the pages, was a new ticket stub, blank on both sides, except for one word, written in pencil, in small, neat handwriting: "Tuesday."

## Tuesday, In The Arcade

Felix did not know what Tuesday meant, until Tuesday came, and the woman in the green coat came with it, at ten in the morning, when the shop was empty and the rain had stopped and the arcade was full of light. She stood in the doorway, with the bell ringing above her, and she said, "My name is Priya. I am the person who writes in your books. I am sorry. I know it is rude to write in books, even second-hand ones. I have been doing it for years, and no one has ever answered before."

Felix came out from behind the counter. His hands were shaking, very slightly, and he put them in his pockets, where the first ticket stub still was, folded small, like a secret.

"Don't be sorry," he said. "I have been answering for weeks, and no one has ever written back before. I mean, until you did. With a ticket. And a word. Tuesday."

Priya laughed. It was a real laugh, warm and sudden, and it filled the shop the way sunlight fills a room when a curtain is pulled back. "I didn't know what else to write," she said. "Tuesday seemed like the bravest word I had."

They talked for an hour, standing between the shelves, about books, and margins, and trains, and the sea, which Priya had still never seen, and the road in the poem, which she walked every morning on her way to the school where she taught, and which had never felt empty since she had read the poem about it. Felix told her about the shop, and the ladder, and the bell, and the eleven years, and how he had believed that second-hand books were better because they had been loved before, and how he had never thought that the loving might continue, in pencil, in his own shop.

## The Ladder And The Brass Rail

One Tuesday, when the shop was quiet and the rain was falling on the arcade roof, Felix showed Priya the rolling ladder. It ran on a brass rail along the tallest shelf, and it squeaked, and it wobbled, and Felix was very proud of it, in the way that people are proud of things that are old and imperfect and entirely their own.

Priya climbed it, carefully, holding the rail, and reached the top shelf, where the oldest books lived, the ones no one asked for, with cracked spines and faded gold letters. She pulled one down, a book of letters written by sailors' wives, and opened it, and inside, on the flyleaf, in faded ink, was written: "To my dearest, come home safe. I will be waiting. — M."

Priya read it aloud, softly, and Felix, standing below, holding the ladder steady, felt his throat tighten, because the book was a hundred years old, and the woman who wrote in it was long gone, and yet her words were still there, still waiting, still true. "Some books," Priya said, climbing down, "are letters that took a hundred years to arrive."

Felix put the book back, gently, on the top shelf, where it belonged, and he thought about all the books in his shop, and all the people who had written in them, and how a bookshop was not really a shop at all, but a place where letters waited for the right reader, sometimes for a very long time.

## The Book With No Notes

Priya began to come to the shop every Tuesday, and then every Tuesday and Friday, and then most days, at ten in the morning, with coffee in two cups, one for her and one for Felix, and they would talk, and shelve books, and read first pages aloud, and argue, gently, about endings. Felix learned that Priya taught history to teenagers, that she had moved to the city three years ago, that she had once been engaged, and that the engagement had ended quietly, the way some things end, not with a fight, but with a slow realisation that two people were reading different books.

Priya learned that Felix had inherited the shop from his uncle, that he had once wanted to be a writer, that he had written one novel, and put it in a drawer, and never shown it to anyone, because he was afraid that the ending was wrong, and that he had been shelving other people's endings ever since, to avoid facing his own.

One Tuesday, Priya brought him a book. It was new, with clean pages, no notes, no tickets, no pressed flowers. She put it on the counter, and she said, "This one has no notes in it. It is waiting for someone to write the first one. I thought you might like to be the first."

Felix opened it. On the first page, in Priya's handwriting, was written: "For Felix, who answers. Write the ending you are afraid of. I will read it, and I will tell you the truth, kindly. — P."

Felix did not speak for a long moment. He looked at the book, and at Priya, and at the shop, full of second-hand stories, and he understood, with a clarity that felt like sunlight through the arcade roof, that his life had been a bookshop full of other people's margins, and that it was time, at last, to write in his own.

## The Tuesdays Of Reading Aloud

As Felix wrote, Priya read. Every Tuesday, he gave her the pages he had written the week before, and she read them aloud, sitting on the little stool behind the counter, while Felix shelved books and pretended not to listen, and listened to every word. She was honest, and kind, and exact, the way a good reader is. She told him when a sentence was too long, when a character would not say such a thing, when the ending of a chapter felt like a door closing too soon.

Felix had never shown his writing to anyone. For eleven years, the novel had lived in a drawer, and the drawer had lived in the back room, and the back room had lived behind the shop, and Felix had told himself that the novel was finished, when what he meant was that he was afraid to finish it. Priya's Tuesdays changed that. A page read aloud, in a shop full of books, by a woman in a green coat, was no longer a secret. It was a story, being told, and stories want to be finished.

One Tuesday, Priya read the last page Felix had written, and she closed the folder, and she looked at him, and she said, "You are not afraid of the ending, Felix. You are afraid of what comes after the ending. Write it anyway. I will be here, on Tuesday, to read it."

## The Ending Felix Was Afraid Of

Felix wrote the ending in three weeks, at night, after closing, at the counter, with the bell over the door silent and the shelves listening. It was not a new novel. It was the ending of the old one, the one in the drawer, the one he had been afraid of for eleven years. The ending was simple, and true, and a little sad, and a little hopeful, the way true endings are. A man who sells other people's stories decides, at last, to live one of his own.

He gave it to Priya on a Tuesday, in brown paper, tied with string, the way he wrapped books for customers. She read it in the shop, sitting on the little stool behind the counter, while Felix pretended to sort receipts, and the bell rang, and customers came and went, and the rain began outside, soft and steady.

When she finished, Priya looked up, and her eyes were wet, and she was smiling, and she said, "The ending is not wrong, Felix. The ending is exactly right. It is the ending of a man who was brave enough to finish."

Felix took her hand, across the counter, among the receipts and the pencils and the pricing gun, and he said, "Will you go and see the sea with me? I have never taken a holiday in eleven years. I would like to take one now. With you. If you would like that."

Priya held his hand, and she did not let go, and she said, "I would like that very much. And Felix? I have never seen the sea either. We can be frightened of the ending together, and go anyway."

## The Sea, At Last, Together

They went to the sea on a Saturday, in a borrowed car, with a picnic, and a blanket, and three books, one of which was the novel about the lighthouse keeper, which Felix had kept, with the first note still in its margin: "I have never seen the sea. Have you?"

They stood on the beach, in the wind, with the waves coming in, grey and green and endless, and Priya took Felix's hand, and she said, "I have seen it now. And you?"

"I have seen it now," Felix said, "and it is better because you are seeing it too."

They walked along the shore, collecting shells, and reading the lighthouse passage aloud, and laughing at the wind, and Felix thought about the shop, and the shelves, and the margins, and how a question written in pencil, in a second-hand book, had travelled through eleven years of quiet, and found its answer, not in a book, but on a beach, in the hand of a woman in a green coat, who wrote in margins and meant every word.

On the way home, Priya took a pencil from her pocket, the same pencil, Felix was sure, and wrote, in the margin of the lighthouse book, below the first note, below the question that had started everything: "Yes. We have seen it. Together. And it was worth the wait."

## The Shop That Answers Back

The bookshop in the arcade is still there. The bell still rings. The ladder still rolls on its brass rail. Felix still wraps books in brown paper. But now there are two cups of coffee on the counter every morning, and a green coat on the hook by the door, and a new sign in the window, written in Priya's neat handwriting, which says: "Notes in the margins are welcome here. Kind ones only. The books will answer, if you are patient."

And on the shelf where the lighthouse book lives, in its proper place, there is a small card, tucked between the pages, which Felix wrote, on the day Priya first said Tuesday, and which he has never taken out. It says: "If you ever miss a train on purpose again, I hope the story is worth it. And I hope you tell me the ending. — F."

Below it, in pencil, in small, neat handwriting, is the answer, which Priya wrote, on the beach, in the wind, with the sea coming in, grey and green and endless. It says: "The story was worth it. The ending is this: we went to the sea, together, and we are going back, every year, for as long as the books last."

Some love stories begin with a grand gesture. Some begin with a question, in pencil, in the margin of a book about the sea, written by a woman who had never seen it, and answered, weeks later, by a man who had been waiting, without knowing it, for someone to write back.
