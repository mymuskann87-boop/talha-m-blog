The rain started at 6:14 in the evening, all at once, the way rain does when it has been holding itself back all day. One moment the street was dry and golden with evening light. The next, the sky opened, and water came down in silver sheets, and everyone on Market Street ran for cover at the same time, laughing and shouting and holding bags and newspapers over their heads.

Zara did not run. She stood under the narrow awning of the closed bookshop with her arms wrapped around herself, watching the rain turn the street into a river of light. She had no umbrella. She had left it at the office that morning, because the forecast had promised a clear evening, and the forecast had lied, the way forecasts sometimes do about the most important things.

"Zara?"

She froze. She knew that voice the way you know a song from long ago — not the words, not the tune exactly, but the feeling of it, all at once, in your chest. She turned slowly.

Kabir stood three steps away, holding a large black umbrella, rain dripping from his shoulders, staring at her as if she were someone he had dreamed about and then found standing in real life. He looked older than the boy she remembered, and exactly the same, both at once. Same dark eyes. Same crooked smile, starting on the left side of his mouth. Same way of standing with his weight on one foot, like he was ready to run or ready to stay, and could not decide which.

"Kabir," she said, and her voice sounded strange to her own ears, thin and far away. "What are you doing here?"

"I live two streets away now," he said. "I was just... walking home. And then the rain. And then you."

For a moment, neither of them moved. Five years stood between them like a wall of glass. Five years since the evening they had sat in this same street, in another rain, and said the words that ended everything. She remembered that evening too clearly. He had been offered a job in another city. She had been offered a place at a university here. They had both been twenty-three and proud and certain that love should never ask anyone to choose. So they had chosen their futures instead of each other, and they had called it being grown up, and they had both cried all the way home.

"You're getting soaked," Kabir said now, holding out the umbrella. "Share?"

Zara looked at the umbrella. It was large, but not that large. Sharing it would mean walking close. Shoulder to shoulder. The way they used to walk, years ago, when one umbrella had always been enough for both of them.

She stepped under it.

## A Rain Love Story on a Crowded Street

They walked slowly down Market Street, pressed close beneath the black umbrella, while the rain drummed above them and the city blurred into gold and silver around them. Zara was very aware of his shoulder touching hers. She was very aware of the smell of rain on his coat. She was very aware that her heart, which had behaved itself perfectly well for five years, was now beating like a drum in a parade.

"So," Kabir said, after a while, in the careful voice of someone walking on thin ice, "are you still painting?"

Zara nodded. "I have a little studio now. Near the river. It's small, but it's mine."

"I knew you would," he said, and there was something warm and proud in his voice that made her throat tighten. "I used to tell everyone that one day your paintings would be in a gallery. Do you remember?"

"I remember everything," Zara said quietly, before she could stop herself.

They walked in silence for a while after that. The rain eased, then poured again, then eased. A bus splashed past, throwing up a fan of water. Kabir tilted the umbrella without thinking, shielding her side first, the way he always used to do, and Zara felt something inside her crack open, like a window that had been painted shut for years.

"Why did you never write?" she asked suddenly. The question had been sitting in her chest for five years, and the rain and the umbrella and the closeness had loosened it. "After you left, I waited. I told myself I wouldn't, but I did. Every day, for months."

Kabir stopped walking. He stood in the rain, the umbrella dripping, and looked at her with eyes full of a sadness she recognised, because she had carried the same sadness herself.

"I did write," he said. "Three letters. Long ones. I tore them all up."

"Why?"

"Because every letter ended the same way," he said. "With me asking you to come to me, or me asking to come back to you. And I thought... I thought if I loved you properly, I should not ask you to give up your dreams. So I said nothing instead. I thought silence was the kind thing. It wasn't. It was just the cowardly thing."

Zara stared at him, rain running down her face, mixing with tears she did not bother to hide, because in the rain no one can tell anyway.

## The Rain Love Story They Never Finished

"I thought you didn't care," she whispered. "I thought you left and forgot me in a week."

"I never forgot you for a single day," Kabir said. "I came back to this city two years ago. I took a job here. I walk down Market Street sometimes, hoping... I don't know what I was hoping. Maybe exactly this. Rain. And you. And one umbrella."

He laughed, a shaky, wet laugh. "I know how that sounds. Like something from a film. But it's true."

Zara laughed too, through her tears, and the laugh surprised them both, bright and real in the middle of the rain. "We were so young," she said. "We thought choosing our dreams meant losing each other. We never thought we could choose both."

"Do you think," Kabir said slowly, "that we could try again? Not the old way. Not with pride and big decisions and all-or-nothing. Just... slowly. Coffee first. Then a walk. Then whatever comes after that, when it comes."

Zara looked at him — at the rain on his hair, at the crooked smile, at the man she had loved at twenty-three and, if she was honest, had never stopped loving — and she felt something settle in her chest, something that had been restless for five years finally coming to rest.

"Coffee first," she said. "Tomorrow. Same street. And Kabir?"

"Yes?"

"Bring the umbrella," she said, smiling. "Just in case."

He laughed, and took her hand under the umbrella, and held it the whole way home, his fingers warm around hers despite the cold rain. When they reached her door, he did not kiss her. He only pressed her hand to his heart for a moment, and looked at her the way you look at something precious you thought you had lost forever and have just, miraculously, found again.

The rain kept falling, soft and silver, washing the street clean behind them. Some love stories end in the rain. This one, after five long years, was only just beginning again.

## The Morning After the Rain

The next morning dawned clear and washed clean, the way the world always looks after a night of rain. Zara woke early, her heart already awake before the rest of her, and stood by her studio window with a cup of tea, watching the light come back to the street. She kept thinking about Kabir's hand in hers under the umbrella, and about coffee, and about the word "tomorrow," which had sounded so simple when she said it and so enormous now that tomorrow had actually arrived.

Kabir was already waiting when she reached Market Street, standing outside the little café on the corner with two cups in his hands and a nervous smile that made him look nineteen again. "I didn't know how you take it now," he admitted, holding out one of the cups. "So I guessed. A little milk, no sugar. Am I right?"

"You are right," Zara said, amazed and touched all at once. "You remembered after five years."

"I remember everything about you," Kabir said quietly. "That was always the problem. And the blessing."

They sat by the café window and talked for two hours, the way people talk when they are making up for lost time and also, gently, making new time. He told her about the job that had brought him back to the city, about the small flat he had rented two streets away, about the evenings he had spent walking down Market Street hoping for exactly the kind of accident that had finally happened. She told him about her studio, about the paintings stacked against the walls, about the gallery owner who had promised her a small show in the autumn. He listened the way he used to listen, with his whole attention, as if her words were the most important thing in the room.

When they finally stood up to leave, Kabir walked her to the door, and paused, and said, "Zara, I don't want this to be a story we tell about one rainy night. I want it to be the beginning of something we choose, day by day, on purpose."

"Then let's choose it," Zara said, and took his hand, and felt the whole bright, rain-washed morning open up in front of them like a road after a storm.
