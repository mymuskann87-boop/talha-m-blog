Almost every home on Earth knows the red glow of Netflix starting up on the TV. But this week, inside the company itself, the mood is very different. About **one thousand people** — writers, engineers, managers, staff who built the shows the world binges — are losing their jobs in the biggest round of cuts the company has made in four years.

The news did not come with a press conference. It came, as these things so often do now, in an email.

## What exactly happened?

On Wednesday 14 October, the Wall Street Journal reported that Netflix is cutting about **6 percent of its workforce**, which works out to **roughly 1,000 jobs**. The report, carried by Reuters, was based on an email sent to employees.

Netflix had about **16,000 full-time employees** at the end of last year, making it one of the giants of the entertainment world. The company itself had not made a big public comment on the cuts at the time the news broke — days earlier, it had declined to comment on an earlier report by Puck News that a cut of about 5 percent was being planned.

For the workers involved, the numbers are not just numbers. A 6 percent cut means whole teams shrinking, projects paused, and a thousand families suddenly recalculating their lives.

## Why is the streaming king cutting people?

This is the question everyone is asking, because from the outside Netflix looks unstoppable. The honest answer is that the streaming war has changed, and it has become much more expensive to win.

**The rivals are bigger now.** Netflix no longer competes only with other streaming apps. Old media companies have combined forces and built their own platforms, and they fight hard for every viewer.

**YouTube is eating the television.** More and more of the world's watching time — and the advertising money that follows it — is going to YouTube, especially on living-room TV screens. Every hour a family spends there is an hour, and a rupee of advertising, that Netflix does not get.

**Growth is harder than it was.** In the early years, Netflix could add millions of new subscribers almost by standing still. Those days are over in most rich countries, where nearly every household that wants streaming already has it. When easy growth ends, big companies usually do the same thing: they tighten costs.

That is why Netflix has also been pushing into new directions — **advertising plans, live programming and gaming** — trying to earn money from more than subscriptions alone. Reshaping a company like that often means letting some teams go while building others.

## The last time this happened

Older readers may remember **2022**, the year the streaming dream cracked. Netflix lost subscribers for the first time in a decade, its share price fell hard, and the company cut hundreds of jobs in several painful rounds. Those cuts became a symbol of the moment the tech and media world realised the party could not grow forever.

Today's round is being described as the **biggest since then** — which is exactly why it is making headlines. It does not mean Netflix is dying; the company remains the leader of streaming. But it does mean the leader is feeling the pressure, in public, at the very top of the industry. When the king starts cutting, everyone below him gets nervous.

## What does this mean for viewers?

For the ordinary viewer, the short answer is: not much changes today. The app still opens, the shows still play, and no price announcement came with this news.

The longer answer is about what kind of shows get made. A company in cost-cutting mode usually becomes more careful: fewer risky, expensive experiments, more sequels and formats it knows will work, and a harder look at anything that does not pull a big audience quickly. Some beloved niche shows may find the road tougher. At the same time, the push into live events, sport-style programming and games — the things Netflix believes will keep families subscribed — is likely to grow.

## One email, a thousand lives

Business news often reads like weather: percentages, quarters, markets. But step back, and this story is simpler and sadder. A thousand people opened an email and learned that their chapter at the world's most famous streaming company was over — not because they failed, but because the war for your evening hours has become so fierce that even the winner is cutting back.

Netflix will survive this week easily. The question the layoffs really ask is about everyone else: if the king of streaming feels this much pressure, what does the future look like for the rest of the entertainment world?
