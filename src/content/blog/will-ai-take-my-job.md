Will AI take my job? It is the question most people ask about artificial intelligence, and it is usually asked with worry. The honest answer, from the research, is less simple than either the fear or the reassurance. AI will change tasks faster than it removes whole jobs. Some jobs will shrink, some will grow, and almost all jobs will change. This post explains, in simple words, what the major studies actually say: the World Economic Forum's forecasts, the IMF's exposure figures, Stanford's evidence on young workers, and Microsoft's list of the most and least exposed occupations. It ends with what you can actually do about it.

## The Short Answer: Tasks, Not Whole Jobs

The most important distinction in all the research is between tasks and jobs. A job is a bundle of tasks. A customer service job, for example, includes answering questions, solving problems, calming angry customers, updating records, and following up. AI may be able to do one or two of those tasks well, and others badly. The job does not disappear. It changes, because the tasks inside it change.

A guide by Bushe, published in September 2026, puts it plainly: AI is changing tasks faster than whole jobs. That sentence is the key to the whole subject. If you ask whether AI will take your job, you are asking the wrong question. The right question is: which of my tasks can AI do, which can it not do, and what will my job look like when the first group is automated? The answer to that question is different for every occupation, and the research gives a surprisingly clear picture of where the line falls.

## The World Economic Forum Numbers

The World Economic Forum's Future of Jobs Report, published in January 2025, is the most widely cited forecast. It says that 170 million new jobs will be created and 92 million jobs displaced by 2030, a net increase of 78 million jobs, which is 7 percent growth in total employment. It also says that 22 percent of today's jobs will be disrupted by 2030, through jobs being created, destroyed, or changed, and that 39 percent of the skills needed in a job will change between 2025 and 2030.

Those numbers are often quoted selectively. The 92 million displaced jobs make headlines. The 170 million created jobs get less attention. The net figure, 78 million more jobs, is the headline the Forum itself emphasises. But the net figure hides the disruption: even if the total number of jobs grows, the people in the displaced jobs must find new ones, learn new skills, and often move to different occupations. The disruption is real, even when the total is positive. The Forum also found that 41 percent of employers plan workforce reductions as AI automates tasks, while 77 percent plan to upskill their workers. Both things are happening at once: cuts and training, in the same companies.

## Who Is Most Exposed: The IMF View

The International Monetary Fund, in research cited by Bushe, estimates that about 40 percent of global employment is exposed to AI, rising to about 60 percent in advanced economies. Exposure does not mean replacement. It means the job is affected by AI, either because AI can do some of its tasks, which is a risk, or because AI can help the worker do the job better, which is an opportunity. The IMF's point is that exposure is not destiny. A job can be highly exposed and still grow, if the worker uses AI to do more, better work.

The exposure figure is higher in advanced economies because more jobs there involve the kind of cognitive work — reading, writing, analysing, coding — that AI does well. In poorer economies, more jobs involve physical work, which AI and robots do less well, so exposure is lower. That does not mean poorer countries are safe. It means the disruption arrives in a different form, through global competition and through the jobs that are created elsewhere.

## The Stanford Evidence On Young Workers

The most worrying evidence comes from Stanford University, in research published in August 2026. It found that employment for 22- to 25-year-olds in the jobs most exposed to AI was about 19 percent below the level of their peers in less-exposed jobs, after adjusting for other factors. The effect was concentrated among young workers in the most exposed occupations, and it appeared quickly, within a few years of AI tools becoming widely available.

The reason is straightforward. Young workers are usually hired for the entry-level tasks that AI does well: drafting, summarising, coding simple programs, answering routine questions. When AI can do those tasks, employers need fewer entry-level workers, and the first rung of the career ladder becomes harder to reach. The Stanford finding does not prove that AI has destroyed those jobs forever. It proves that the entry point into those careers is narrowing, and that young workers are feeling the change first. That is a serious problem, because a career that cannot be entered cannot be climbed.

## Microsoft's List: Most And Least Exposed

Microsoft published research in 2025 that listed the occupations with the highest and lowest overlap with what AI can do. The most exposed occupations included interpreters and translators, historians, passenger attendants, sales representatives, writers and authors, customer service representatives, computer numerical control tool programmers, telephone operators, ticket agents and travel clerks, and broadcast announcers and radio DJs. These are jobs where the core tasks — language, information, communication — overlap closely with what language models do.

The least exposed occupations included dredge operators, bridge and lock tenders, water treatment plant operators, foundry mould and coremakers, rail-track laying and maintenance equipment operators, pile driver operators, floor sanders and finishers, orderlies, motorboat operators, and logging equipment operators. These are jobs that involve physical work in unpredictable environments, operating heavy equipment, and hands-on care. AI cannot do them, and robots cannot yet do them cheaply or reliably.

The list is not a prediction that the most exposed jobs will vanish. It is a measure of overlap: how much of the job's work looks like what AI already does. Writers, for example, are highly exposed, but writing is also a job where human judgement, originality, and trust matter, and where AI is more often used as a tool than as a replacement. The list tells you where to pay attention, not where to panic.

## Lawyers, Accountants And Programmers

Three professional groups illustrate the pattern well. Lawyers use AI to search documents, draft contracts, and summarise cases, which speeds up the routine work, but the judgement, the advocacy, and the responsibility to the client remain human. Accountants use AI to process transactions and prepare reports, but the advice, the audit judgement, and the trust of the client remain human. Programmers use AI to write and check code, which makes them faster, but the design of systems, the understanding of what the user needs, and the responsibility for the result remain human.

In each case, AI changes the tasks, not the profession. The junior work changes most, because it is the most routine. The senior work changes least, because it needs judgement. The pattern is consistent across the research: AI takes the routine first, and the human work that remains is the work that needs judgement, trust, and responsibility. The question for any professional is whether they are doing routine work that AI can do, or judgement work that it cannot.

## What To Do: Skills, Not Panic

The research points to a practical response, and it is not panic. The World Economic Forum found that 63 percent of employers cite the skills gap as the main barrier to change, and 77 percent plan to upskill their workers. The skills that matter most are the ones AI does not do well: judgement, creativity, communication, problem-solving, and the ability to work with AI itself. Learning to use AI tools, rather than competing with them, is the single most useful step a worker can take.

The practical steps are simple to state and hard to do. Learn the AI tools used in your field, and use them daily, so that you are the person who knows how to get the best from them. Build the skills AI cannot copy: understanding clients, making judgements under uncertainty, and taking responsibility for results. Keep learning, because 39 percent of the skills in your job will change by 2030, according to the Forum. And pay attention to the entry-level problem: if you are starting out, look for roles where you work alongside AI and learn the judgement parts of the job, not only the routine parts.

The future of work is not a choice between humans and AI. It is a choice between humans who use AI well and humans who do not. The research is clear on that, even when it disagrees on the numbers. The jobs will change. The workers who change with them will be the ones who thrive.

## The Pattern Across All The Research

Read together, the studies tell one consistent story. The World Economic Forum expects more jobs created than destroyed by 2030, the IMF finds about 40 percent of jobs exposed worldwide, Stanford finds young workers in exposed jobs falling behind, and Microsoft's overlap list shows language and information work most exposed and physical work least exposed. None of the four says whole professions will simply vanish.

The pattern is about tasks, not job titles. Routine work changes first, junior work changes most, and the work that remains is the work that needs judgement, trust, and responsibility. Lawyers, accountants, and programmers all show the same shape: AI speeds the routine, humans keep the decisions. The workers who learn to use AI tools, and who build the judgement parts of their jobs, are the ones the research expects to do well.
