On Salisbury Plain in southern England stands a circle of huge stones, some upright, some topped with horizontal lintels, others fallen and broken. It is one of the most recognised ancient monuments in the world, and one of the most misunderstood. Visitors often arrive expecting a single answer to a single mystery, as if Stonehenge were a puzzle with one hidden solution. In reality, Stonehenge was built, rebuilt, and reused over more than a thousand years, by people whose tools, beliefs, and daily lives were very different from ours, and its purpose probably changed over time rather than staying fixed.

The monument we see today is only the final stone phase of a much longer story. The site began as a circular earthwork with a ditch and bank, and later gained timber posts, smaller bluestones, and finally the great sarsen stones that dominate the view today. Understanding Stonehenge therefore means thinking in stages, not in a single building event, and accepting that some questions have strong evidence behind them while others remain open. Archaeologists can say a great deal about when stones were moved and where they came from, but they must be more careful about why people invested so much labour in this place.

This guide separates what is well supported from what is speculation. It covers the building stages, the extraordinary transport of stones from Wales and from local sources, the engineering that allowed people without wheels, iron, or cranes to raise multi-tonne stones, the alignment with solstices, the burials and artefacts found nearby, and the leading theories about purpose, including ceremony, healing, astronomy, and community gathering. It also addresses popular myths, including claims about aliens, Merlin, and single-purpose explanations that the evidence does not support.

## A Monument Built in Stages Over 1,500 Years

Stonehenge did not appear in one project. The earliest phase, around 3000 BCE, was a circular ditch and bank about 110 metres across, with a ring of 56 pits known as Aubrey Holes, named after the antiquarian John Aubrey who first noted them in the seventeenth century. At this stage there were probably timber posts, and the site already served as a burial ground, with cremated human remains placed in the ditch and in the Aubrey Holes. This early Stonehenge was an earthwork monument, impressive in scale but very different from the stone circle visitors see now.

Centuries later, around 2600 to 2500 BCE, smaller bluestones were brought from the Preseli Hills in Wales, a journey of more than 200 kilometres, and arranged in the centre of the site. Bluestones are a group of smaller stones, weighing up to a few tonnes each, distinct from the larger sarsens. Their transport remains debated, with theories involving overland haulage, river transport, and even glacial movement for some stones, although human transport is now favoured for the main bluestone phase. The effort involved suggests the stones themselves mattered, not only the finished shape, because people chose to bring specific stones from a distant place rather than using only local rock.

The great sarsen phase followed, around 2500 to 2400 BCE, when massive local sandstone blocks, some weighing more than 20 tonnes, were shaped, transported from the Marlborough Downs about 30 kilometres away, and erected in a circle with lintels joined by mortise-and-tenon joints, a woodworking technique adapted to stone. This is the phase that gives Stonehenge its iconic post-and-lintel silhouette. Later, bluestones were rearranged inside the sarsen circle, and the monument continued to be used and modified into the Bronze Age, before eventually falling into ruin as stones were removed, buried, or broken for building material in later centuries.

Thinking in stages matters because it prevents a common mistake: attributing every feature to a single group or a single moment. Different generations added, moved, and reinterpreted the monument, much as a cathedral is altered over centuries. A theory that explains only the final sarsen circle may miss the earlier burial use, and a theory that explains only the burials may miss the later astronomical alignments. The full story is a sequence of uses, not one use frozen in time.

## Moving Stones Without Wheels or Cranes

The transport of Stonehenge's stones is often presented as impossible, which it was not, although it was extraordinarily labour-intensive. Experiments and reconstructions show that a large stone can be moved on wooden rollers or on a wooden sledge over prepared ground, pulled by teams of people using ropes. For the sarsens, a route from the Marlborough Downs would have required preparing a trackway, possibly with timber rails, and coordinating dozens or hundreds of pullers, with others levering, steering, and placing rollers ahead of the stone. It would have been slow, dangerous, and highly organised, but it did not require machinery beyond timber, rope, leather, and stone tools.

The bluestones present a harder problem because of distance. The Preseli Hills are far from Salisbury Plain, and the route may have combined overland dragging with river and coastal transport by raft or boat. Some researchers have proposed that bluestones were moved in stages over many years, perhaps as part of a migration or as a deliberate act of bringing a sacred landscape to a new home. Others have suggested that glaciers carried some bluestones part of the way during the Ice Age, although the main arrangement at Stonehenge is still attributed to human action. The debate continues, and new geological fingerprinting, which matches stone chemistry to specific outcrops, is refining the picture year by year.

Raising the stones was also a feat of practical engineering. A common reconstruction involves digging a pit, tipping the stone into it using a timber crib and levers, then packing the pit with rubble to hold the stone upright. Lifting a lintel onto two uprights could be done by building a timber platform or an earthen ramp and levering the lintel up step by step, with wooden cribbing supporting it as it rose. Shaping the stones involved hammering with harder stone mauls, a slow process that produced the smooth, jointed surfaces still visible today. The mortise-and-tenon joints, where a knob on the upright fits into a hole in the lintel, and tongue-and-groove joints between lintels, show a level of stoneworking skill that is easy to underestimate from a distance.

None of this required supernatural help, and none of it was quick. Estimates of labour vary, but all agree that Stonehenge represented a major communal investment over generations. That investment itself is evidence: people do not move 20-tonne stones for a casual reason. Whatever Stonehenge meant, it mattered enough to coordinate labour, food, shelter, and leadership across a wide region, which tells us something about the society that built it, even if its beliefs remain partly hidden.

## The Solstice Alignment and Astronomy

One of Stonehenge's most famous features is its alignment with the solstices. On the summer solstice, the Sun rises roughly over the Heel Stone, an outlying stone northeast of the circle, when viewed from the centre. On the winter solstice, the Sun sets in the opposite direction, through the gap between the tallest trilithons, the pairs of uprights joined by a lintel. These alignments are broadly accepted, although the precision and the intended meaning are debated. The monument's main axis points toward both solstice events, which is unlikely to be coincidence given the care taken in laying out the circle.

It is important not to overstate what this proves. An alignment with the solstices does not prove that Stonehenge was primarily an observatory in the modern sense, with people making precise astronomical measurements. Neolithic farmers already knew the seasons from farming, daylight, and the Sun's changing rising points, and a monument that marked the turning points of the year could have served a ceremonial or calendrical purpose without being a scientific instrument. The solstices are the longest and shortest days, natural moments for gathering, feasting, and ritual, and marking them in stone would have given those moments a permanent, communal focus.

Other astronomical claims about Stonehenge, including predictions of eclipses and complex lunar cycles, have been proposed and debated for decades. Some researchers argue that the Aubrey Holes could have been used as a calculator for eclipse cycles, while others caution that the evidence is too incomplete to support such a specific function. The safest summary is that Stonehenge has clear solar alignments, plausible lunar connections, and a general relationship with the sky, but that detailed astronomical theories should be treated as hypotheses rather than settled facts. The sky mattered to its builders, but we should not project modern observatory functions onto a Neolithic monument without strong evidence.

Seasonal gatherings are supported by other evidence from the surrounding landscape. Animal bones found near Stonehenge suggest large feasts, particularly around winter, with pigs slaughtered at an age indicating winter killing. People appear to have travelled from across Britain to gather, bringing animals and perhaps stone, tools, and stories. A monument aligned to the winter solstice would have been a natural centre for such a gathering, a place where the return of longer days could be marked together after the darkest time of year.

## Burials, Healing, and the People Around Stonehenge

Stonehenge was also a cemetery. Cremated remains from the early phase represent one of the largest known Neolithic cremation cemeteries in Britain, with estimates of up to 150 to 240 individuals over time, although exact numbers are debated because remains were moved and mixed. Later burials include the famous Amesbury Archer, a man buried nearby around 2300 BCE with copper and gold objects, and the Boscombe Bowmen, a group burial that suggests people travelled long distances to the area. Isotope analysis of teeth and bones, which can indicate where a person grew up based on the chemistry of local water and food, shows that some people buried near Stonehenge came from far away, including possibly continental Europe.

A healing theory has also been proposed, based on the bluestones. Some bluestones show signs of having been chipped, and folklore in Wales associated the Preseli stones with healing properties. Researchers have suggested that people may have visited Stonehenge seeking cures, bringing sick relatives or taking fragments as talismans. This theory is intriguing but not proven: chipping could have other causes, including later souvenir hunting or tool production, and the presence of burials with injuries or illnesses does not by itself prove a healing centre. It remains one plausible use among several, rather than a single explanation.

The wider landscape is essential to understanding Stonehenge. It does not stand alone; it is part of a complex of monuments, including Durrington Walls, a large settlement where builders and visitors may have lived, Woodhenge, a timber circle, and numerous burial mounds and avenues. Durrington Walls in particular has revealed houses, pottery, and animal bones indicating large seasonal feasts, and a timber circle that mirrors Stonehenge in layout but in wood rather than stone. Some archaeologists interpret the two as complementary: Durrington Walls as a place of the living, with timber that decays, and Stonehenge as a place of the dead, with stone that endures. This interpretation is elegant and influential, but like other theories, it is a model to test, not a proven fact.

Together, burials, feasting, and the surrounding monuments paint a picture of Stonehenge as a ceremonial landscape, not an isolated curiosity. People gathered, ate, buried their dead, and marked the seasons there over many generations. That picture is richer than any single-purpose story, and it explains why the site continued to attract attention long after its main building phases ended.

## Myths, Misunderstandings, and What We Should Not Claim

Stonehenge attracts myths, and sorting them out is part of understanding it. The Merlin legend, in which the wizard transported the stones from Ireland by magic, dates to medieval writings by Geoffrey of Monmouth and reflects a time when the monument's true age and builders were unknown. It is a fascinating story about how later societies explained a monument they did not build, but it is not history. Similarly, claims that Stonehenge was built by Romans, Druids, or aliens ignore the dating evidence: the main phases predate the Romans by more than two thousand years, and the Druids, as a later Iron Age priesthood, postdate the main building by over a thousand years, although later Druidic interest in the site is a real part of its modern history.

Alien theories fail for a different reason: they are unnecessary. Every engineering step at Stonehenge has plausible reconstructions using Neolithic technology, and attributing the work to extraterrestrials explains nothing while ignoring the evidence of human organisation, tool marks, and staged construction. The same applies to claims of lost advanced civilisations with modern machinery: no such machinery has been found, and the stoneworking marks are consistent with stone hammers and slow, patient labour, not with power tools. A good rule for ancient mysteries is to prefer the explanation that fits the artefacts, even if it is less sensational.

Another misunderstanding is the idea that Stonehenge is a single, perfect circle that has survived intact. In fact, many stones have fallen, been buried, or been removed, and the monument was restored in the twentieth century, with some stones re-erected and concreted into place for stability and safety. Restoration was based on archaeological evidence, but visitors should know that the present arrangement is partly a modern conservation outcome, not a pristine survival. That does not diminish its importance; it means that what we see is a cared-for ruin, interpreted with the best available evidence, rather than a time capsule frozen since the Bronze Age.

Finally, there is a tendency to ask for one purpose, as if Stonehenge must have been either a temple, an observatory, a calendar, or a cemetery. The evidence suggests it was several of these at different times, and perhaps several at once. A place can be a burial ground, a seasonal gathering point, a marker of the solstices, and a symbol of community identity simultaneously, without contradiction. Accepting multiple, changing purposes is not a failure to answer the question; it is the answer that the evidence supports.

## Visiting Stonehenge Today and Protecting It

Visiting Stonehenge today is very different from visiting it a century ago, because conservation, access, and interpretation have all changed. The monument is managed as a World Heritage Site, with a visitor centre, a shuttle or walking route to the stones, and a path that keeps most visitors at a distance to protect the stones and the surrounding archaeology from erosion and damage. Special access visits allow smaller groups to walk among the stones outside normal hours, under supervision, which offers a closer experience without the wear that unrestricted access would cause. Anyone planning a visit should check current opening times, booking requirements, and solstice arrangements, because access rules change for large gatherings.

Conservation challenges are ongoing. Weathering, lichen growth, visitor pressure, and nearby roads and farming all affect the site, and archaeologists must balance public access with preservation. The nearby A303 road has been the subject of long debate, with proposals for a tunnel to reduce traffic impact on the landscape, illustrating how modern infrastructure decisions affect ancient monuments. The wider landscape, including burial mounds, avenues, and settlements, is also protected, because Stonehenge's meaning depends on its setting, not only on the stones themselves. A visit that includes the visitor centre exhibition, which explains the stages, the people, and the landscape, is far richer than a quick photograph of the circle alone.

For those who cannot visit in person, digital resources, reconstructions, and museum collections offer valuable alternatives, including models of how the monument may have looked at different stages, and objects found nearby, such as tools, pottery, and burial goods. These resources help correct the impression that Stonehenge is a single, static monument, by showing its changes over time. Whether visited in person or studied online, the most rewarding approach is to ask staged questions: what was here first, what was added later, who lived nearby, and what did this place mean to each generation, rather than seeking a single secret.

## My Take:

My honest view is that Stonehenge is most impressive precisely because it was built by ordinary people with extraordinary organisation, not because it was built by magic or by visitors from elsewhere. The idea that Neolithic farmers, without writing, iron, or wheels, could coordinate the transport of stones over 200 kilometres and raise them with joints cut in stone is, to me, more astonishing than any alien story. It tells us that prehistoric people were not primitive in the sense of being simple-minded; they were skilled, patient, and capable of long-term planning, and we should respect that intelligence rather than explaining it away.

I also think the staged history of Stonehenge is the most important lesson for visitors. Many people arrive expecting a single moment of construction, as if a king ordered it built in a year. The reality of a monument evolving over 1,500 years, with different generations adding earthworks, bluestones, and sarsens, is harder to grasp but far more human. It suggests that Stonehenge was not a project but a tradition, a place that each generation inherited, reinterpreted, and renewed. That idea changes how you walk around the stones: you are not looking at one building, but at layers of devotion, like rings in a tree.

On purpose, my opinion is that we should resist the urge to pick a winner. The burial evidence is strong, the solstice alignment is strong, and the feasting evidence is strong, and none of those cancels the others. I think Stonehenge was a ceremonial landscape where death, seasons, and community came together, and that trying to reduce it to a single label does violence to that richness. If a guide tells you it was definitely a healing centre or definitely an eclipse computer, I would treat that as a hypothesis to enjoy, not as a settled fact to repeat.

I am also sympathetic to the healing theory, but with caution. The idea that people travelled seeking cures is moving and plausible, and the chipped bluestones are suggestive, but suggestion is not proof. I would like to see more evidence before calling Stonehenge a hospital of the ancient world, because that label can easily become a new myth, replacing the Merlin story with a medical one. The responsible position is to say that healing may have been one use among others, and that further research may clarify it, without pretending we already know.

Finally, I believe Stonehenge matters today not only as archaeology but as a reminder about time. We live in a world of fast projects and short attention spans, yet this monument was tended over millennia by people who would never see it finished. That long view is humbling and, I think, hopeful. It shows that humans can sustain a shared project across generations when they believe it matters, and that a place can hold meaning long after its builders are gone. When you stand before the stones, you are not only looking at the past; you are looking at what long-term commitment can create, and that is a lesson that still applies.

I also think Stonehenge raises an important question about who owns the past. The monument is a national heritage site, a World Heritage Site, a place of spiritual significance for modern Druids and Pagans, a tourist attraction, and an archaeological resource, and those interests sometimes conflict, especially at solstices, when large crowds gather. Managing those interests fairly, with respect for both scientific research and contemporary spiritual practice, is difficult, but it is part of what makes Stonehenge a living place rather than a dead ruin. I think heritage management should listen to all these voices, while keeping archaeological evidence at the centre of historical claims, so that belief and evidence are both respected without being confused.

Another angle is the value of experimental archaeology, in which researchers test ancient techniques by building replicas, moving stones, and using stone tools. These experiments do not prove exactly how Stonehenge was built, but they show what was possible, how long tasks took, how many people were needed, and where the difficulties lay. I find that practical testing more convincing than purely theoretical arguments, because it grounds speculation in muscle, timber, and stone. It also gives modern people a tangible sense of Neolithic labour, which is easy to underestimate when looking at finished stones from a path.

I also want to reflect on the surrounding landscape, which is often overlooked in favour of the circle itself. Durrington Walls, Woodhenge, the Avenue, and the burial mounds form a ceremonial landscape that is larger and more complex than Stonehenge alone, and understanding that landscape changes the story from a single monument to a whole region of gathering, living, and burying. I think future research will continue to reveal more about that landscape, through geophysical survey, excavation, and environmental analysis, and that each discovery will refine, rather than overturn, the staged picture we already have. Patience is therefore a virtue in archaeology, as it was in building.

Finally, I believe Stonehenge's enduring appeal lies in its silence. Unlike a written record, it does not tell us what its builders believed in words, so each generation must interpret it anew, with the evidence available at the time. That silence can be frustrating, but it is also an invitation to humility: we must admit what we do not know, even about a monument we have studied for centuries. In a world that often demands instant answers, Stonehenge reminds us that some questions are worth living with, studying slowly, and revisiting as new evidence emerges, rather than settling prematurely for a sensational story.

One last thought: the best way to honour Stonehenge's builders is to keep asking evidence-based questions rather than settling for a single story. New surveys, isotope studies, and dating methods will continue to refine what we know, and each refinement should be welcomed, even when it complicates a favourite theory. A monument that survived for five thousand years deserves that patient, curious attention, and visitors who leave with more questions than they arrived with have understood it better than those who leave with a single slogan.

Keep that lesson in mind whenever a new claim or trend appears: check the evidence, consider who benefits from the claim, and choose the option that is safe, sustainable, and suited to your own situation rather than the loudest option online.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Who built Stonehenge?
Neolithic and early Bronze Age people in Britain built Stonehenge in stages over more than a thousand years, starting around 3000 BCE. It was not built by Romans, Druids, or a single ruler, and there is no credible evidence for alien involvement.

### How were the huge stones moved to Stonehenge?
Sarsen stones were likely dragged on wooden sledges or rollers from about 30 kilometres away, while smaller bluestones came from Wales, possibly by a combination of overland and water transport. Raising them involved pits, levers, timber cribbing, and ramps, using stone tools and large coordinated teams.

### What was Stonehenge used for?
Evidence supports multiple uses over time, including burial, seasonal gathering and feasting, and marking the solstices. Healing has been proposed as another possible use, but it is not proven. It was probably not a single-purpose building.

### Is Stonehenge aligned with the Sun?
Yes, broadly. Its main axis aligns with summer solstice sunrise and winter solstice sunset. This suggests the solstices mattered to its builders, although it does not prove Stonehenge was a modern-style observatory.

### Where did Stonehenge's bluestones come from?
Geological matching links most bluestones to the Preseli Hills in Wales, more than 200 kilometres away. The exact transport route is still debated, but human transport is the favoured explanation for the main phase.

### Can visitors walk among the stones today?
General visitor access is usually to a path around the stones, with special Stone Circle Access visits allowing closer entry at certain times, subject to booking and conservation rules. Visitors should check the official English Heritage information before travelling.
