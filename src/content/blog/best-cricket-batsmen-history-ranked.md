Ranking the best cricket batsmen in history is one of the sport's great debates, and it is a debate that will never be fully settled. Cricket has been played at the highest level for well over a century, across different eras, different conditions, and different formats. A batsman who dominated on uncovered pitches in the 1930s cannot be compared directly with a batsman who plays on covered pitches, with better protective equipment, against faster bowling and more sophisticated fielding. Yet fans love the debate, because it forces us to decide what greatness means: is it the highest average, the most runs, the most centuries, the greatest innings under pressure, or the player who changed how the game is played? This ranking weighs all of those things, and it explains its reasoning openly, so readers can agree, disagree, and argue with evidence rather than just with feeling.

The first challenge is the sheer variety of cricket. Test cricket, played over five days, is the format that most rankings prioritise, because it tests technique, temperament, and endurance more thoroughly than any other. One-day international cricket, introduced in the 1970s, and Twenty20 cricket, introduced in the 2000s, reward different skills: faster scoring, innovation, and the ability to perform under immediate pressure. A complete ranking should acknowledge all formats, but it should also be honest that Test cricket remains the benchmark for most historians. The players at the top of this list excelled in Test cricket, and several also dominated limited-overs cricket, which strengthens their cases.

The second challenge is the quality of opposition and conditions. A batsman who averaged 50 against strong bowling attacks on difficult pitches has a stronger claim than one who averaged 55 against weaker attacks on flat pitches, though averages alone do not capture that difference. Historians therefore look at context: who the batsman faced, where he played, whether he succeeded home and away, and whether he performed when his team needed him most. Averages are a starting point, not a verdict, and this ranking uses them alongside innings of special significance, longevity, and influence on the game.

## Don Bradman: The Outlier Who Defines Greatness

Don Bradman of Australia stands at the top of almost every serious ranking, and for good reason. His Test batting average of 99.94, across 52 matches between 1928 and 1948, is so far ahead of any other player that it remains one of sport's most extraordinary statistics. The next-highest Test averages, for players with substantial careers, are in the low 60s, which means Bradman was not merely the best of his era but better by a margin that no other sport can match. He scored 29 Test centuries, including 12 double centuries, and his highest score was 334. Had he scored just four runs in his final innings, his average would have reached 100, a detail that has become part of cricket folklore.

Bradman's dominance was not limited to statistics. He scored quickly, often at a rate that put pressure on bowlers and changed the course of matches. He was famously targeted by England's Bodyline tactics in the 1932-33 series, when fast bowlers aimed at the body with a packed leg-side field, a strategy designed specifically to stop him. That England felt compelled to adopt such controversial tactics is itself evidence of his dominance. Bradman also succeeded in different conditions, in Australia and England, and against the best bowlers of his era. Critics sometimes note that he played fewer Tests than modern players and did not face some bowling attacks, but those caveats do not erase a gap of nearly 40 runs per innings between Bradman and his nearest rivals. On any reasonable measure, he is first, and the debate is really about who is second.

Bradman's influence extended beyond his playing career. He became an administrator and a revered figure in Australian cricket, and his name became synonymous with batting excellence. Young players are still compared to Bradman, usually unfavourably, and his average is used as a benchmark for sporting dominance in general. No ranking of cricket batsmen can be taken seriously if it does not place Bradman first, and this one does so without hesitation.

## Sachin Tendulkar: Longevity, Volume, and a Nation's Hopes

Sachin Tendulkar of India is second on this list, and his case rests on a combination of volume, longevity, and the weight of expectation he carried. Tendulkar played 200 Test matches and 463 one-day internationals between 1989 and 2013, scoring 15,921 Test runs and 18,426 one-day runs, both records, and 100 international centuries, a feat no other player has matched. He made his debut as a 16-year-old against a formidable Pakistan attack and was still scoring centuries more than two decades later. That longevity, across different eras, different bowling attacks, and the transition from a less professional era to the modern game, is extraordinary.

Tendulkar's significance goes beyond numbers. For much of his career, he carried the hopes of more than a billion Indian fans, and his performances were followed with an intensity that few athletes have experienced. When he batted, India often seemed to pause, and when he was dismissed, a sense of deflation spread through the country. That pressure would have broken many players, but Tendulkar handled it with remarkable consistency and dignity. He also adapted his game over time, from an aggressive young batsman to a more measured, technically complete player, and he succeeded in all conditions, home and away, against all opponents.

Some critics argue that Tendulkar's average, 53.78 in Tests, is lower than those of some other greats, and that he did not always dominate in the biggest matches as others did. Those criticisms have some merit, but they overlook the sheer scale of his achievement and the context in which it was achieved. Scoring 100 international centuries, playing for 24 years at the highest level, and maintaining excellence while carrying a nation's expectations is a unique achievement. Tendulkar is not merely a great batsman; he is a cultural figure whose impact on Indian cricket and on the global game is immense. Second place is a fitting recognition of that legacy.

## Brian Lara, Viv Richards, and the West Indian Greats

Brian Lara of Trinidad and the West Indies is third on this list, and his case rests on peak performance and record-breaking innings. Lara holds the record for the highest individual score in Test cricket, 400 not out against England in 2004, and the highest score in first-class cricket, 501 not out. He scored 11,953 Test runs at an average of 52.88, with 34 centuries, and he often scored heavily when his team was struggling, carrying a West Indies side that was declining from its former dominance. His innings were characterised by elegance, timing, and an ability to score all around the wicket, and his 400 not out remains one of cricket's most celebrated achievements.

Vivian Richards, also of the West Indies, is a strong contender for the top five, and some fans would place him higher. Richards played between 1974 and 1991, scoring 8,540 Test runs at an average of 50.23, with 24 centuries, and he did so without a helmet for much of his career, facing some of the fastest bowlers in history with an aggressive, swaggering style that intimidated opponents. Richards rarely wore a helmet even against express pace, a choice that reflected both his confidence and the era's culture, and his ability to dominate attacks, often scoring quickly and putting bowlers under immediate pressure, made him one of the most feared batsmen of all time. He also excelled in one-day cricket, where his aggressive style was ahead of its time.

Other West Indian greats deserve mention. Garry Sobers, who is often ranked as the greatest all-rounder in history, was also a superb batsman, scoring 8,032 Test runs at an average of 57.78, including 365 not out, which was then the world record. Sobers's versatility, he could bat anywhere in the order, bowl different styles, and field brilliantly, makes him unique, though his batting alone would place him among the greats. The West Indies' dominance in the 1970s and 1980s was built on fast bowling, but it was also built on batsmen like Richards, Sobers, and Lara, who gave the team the runs to support its bowlers. Their legacy is central to any history of batting.

## The Modern Masters: Kohli, Smith, Root, and Williamson

The modern era has produced several batsmen who belong in any all-time discussion, though their careers are still unfolding or have only recently ended. Virat Kohli of India is among them, with a remarkable record across formats, including 82 international centuries as of recent seasons, and a period of dominance in the late 2010s when he seemed to score centuries at will. Kohli's intensity, fitness, and chasing ability in one-day cricket, where he has an exceptional record in run chases, set him apart, and his Test record, while slightly less dominant than his limited-overs record, still includes many superb innings in difficult conditions.

Steve Smith of Australia is another modern great, whose unorthodox technique and extraordinary concentration have produced a Test average that, at its peak, approached Bradman's territory, though it has settled in the 50s in recent years. Smith's ability to score heavily in series against strong opposition, particularly in England, and his quirky but effective method, have made him one of the most fascinating batsmen to watch. Joe Root of England, the leading run-scorer among active players for much of recent years, combines elegance with longevity, and his record as England's highest Test run-scorer is a significant achievement, though his conversion rate from fifties to centuries has sometimes been questioned.

Kane Williamson of New Zealand, though less prolific in volume than some rivals, is admired for his technique, temperament, and leadership, and his average in the mid-50s in Tests reflects a player of the highest class. These modern players benefit from better fitness, analysis, and preparation than earlier generations, but they also face more cricket, more travel, and more scrutiny. Their places in history will become clearer as their careers conclude, but they have already done enough to be discussed alongside the greats, and future rankings may place them higher.

## The Elegant Greats: Sobers, Gavaskar, Ponting, and Others

Beyond the top names, several other batsmen deserve recognition for their distinctive contributions. Sunil Gavaskar of India was the first player to reach 10,000 Test runs, scoring 10,122 runs at an average of 51.12, and he did so as an opener facing the great West Indian fast bowlers without a helmet for much of his career. His technique against pace, his patience, and his ability to score in difficult conditions made him a pioneer for Indian cricket and a model for opening batsmen everywhere. Gavaskar's achievement is even more impressive given that he played in an era when Indian pitches and support were less developed than today.

Ricky Ponting of Australia, who scored 13,378 Test runs at an average of 51.85, with 41 centuries, combined aggression with longevity and captained Australia during a dominant period. His pull shot was one of the most feared in the game, and his ability to score quickly while maintaining consistency made him a central figure in Australia's success. Jacques Kallis of South Africa, with 13,289 Test runs at 55.12, was both a great batsman and a fine bowler, and his all-round record is unmatched in modern cricket, though his batting alone places him among the elite.

Other names that belong in the conversation include Kumar Sangakkara of Sri Lanka, whose elegant batting and wicketkeeping produced 12,400 Test runs at 57.40; Rahul Dravid of India, known as The Wall for his defensive solidity and his 13,288 Test runs; and Greg Chappell, Allan Border, and Steve Waugh of Australia, each of whom contributed to their country's dominance in different eras. The list could continue, and fans will argue passionately about omissions, which is part of the pleasure. A ranking is a starting point for discussion, not an end to it.

## How Conditions, Formats, and Eras Shape Rankings

Any ranking must grapple with the fact that cricket has changed enormously. Early Test cricket was played on uncovered pitches that deteriorated, with less protective equipment and slower outfields. Modern cricket is played on covered pitches, with helmets, better bats, and faster outfields, but also against more varied bowling, including reverse swing, mystery spin, and express pace, and with far more cricket played each year. A batsman from the 1930s who averaged 50 was achieving something different from a modern batsman who averages 50, though both achievements are impressive.

Formats also matter. A player who dominates Test cricket but struggles in limited-overs cricket may still be a great batsman, but a player who dominates all formats has a broader claim. Tendulkar, Kohli, and Richards excelled across formats, while Bradman played only Test cricket, since limited-overs internationals did not exist in his era. That should not count against Bradman, but it does mean that cross-format comparisons require care. Similarly, a player who dominates at home but struggles away has a weaker claim than one who succeeds everywhere, and this ranking gives weight to away performances.

The best approach is to judge each player against his own era and then compare eras with humility. Bradman's dominance over his contemporaries was greater than anyone else's, which is why he is first despite playing fewer matches. Tendulkar's longevity and volume across eras is unmatched. Lara's peak innings were extraordinary. Richards's intimidation and style changed the game. Each of these qualities matters, and a ranking that only counts runs or averages misses the richness of cricket history. Fans should use rankings as a guide to exploring the game's past, watching old footage, and reading about innings they did not see live.

## My Take:

My take is that Bradman first is not really a debate, and that the interesting debate starts at second place, where reasonable people can disagree passionately and still be right in different ways. I place Tendulkar second because of the unique combination of volume, longevity, and pressure, but I fully understand fans who place Lara second for his peak brilliance, or Richards second for his dominance and style, or Sobers second for his all-round genius. Those are not wrong answers; they are different answers to a question that has no single correct response, because greatness itself is not a single thing. What I would resist is a ranking based only on averages, because averages hide context, and context is where cricket's real stories live.

I also think modern players are sometimes underrated by nostalgic fans and sometimes overrated by fans who only watch the present. The truth is that cricket's standard has risen in many ways, fitness, fielding, analysis, and bowling variety are all better than ever, but the mental and technical challenges of batting have not become easier. A modern batsman faces more cricket, more travel, and more scrutiny, and sustaining excellence across formats is a different kind of achievement from dominating one format in one era. I would therefore encourage fans to appreciate both eras without romanticising one or dismissing the other. Watch footage of Richards facing fast bowling without a helmet, and you will understand his courage. Watch Tendulkar's innings under the weight of a nation's hopes, and you will understand his temperament. Watch Lara's 400, and you will understand his genius. Each is great in a way the others were not.

Finally, I believe the best way to enjoy a ranking like this is to use it as an invitation to discover cricket history, not as a final verdict. Pick a player you know little about, Gavaskar, Sobers, or Sangakkara, for example, and read about their greatest innings. You will find stories of courage, skill, and drama that enrich your understanding of the modern game. Cricket is a sport that honours its past, and its greatest batsmen are not just names on a list but characters in a long, unfolding story. Bradman will always be first, but the players who follow him are what make the story worth telling, and arguing about their order is one of the game's great pleasures.
A ranking focused only on men's cricket tells half the story, and any serious discussion of batting greatness should acknowledge the women's game. Players such as Belinda Clark, Mithali Raj, Meg Lanning, Ellyse Perry, and Suzie Bates have produced records and innings of the highest quality, often with far less support, media coverage, and financial reward than their male counterparts. Clark's 229 not out in a one-day international, Raj's longevity and run-scoring, and Lanning's dominance as captain and batter all deserve recognition in any history of batting. The women's game has grown rapidly in professionalism and visibility, and its greatest batters should be celebrated on their own terms, not merely as footnotes to the men's game. Future all-time lists that ignore women's cricket will look incomplete, and fans who explore the women's game will find batting of great skill and drama.

Twenty20 cricket has also changed batting in ways that affect historical comparisons. The shortest format rewards innovation, power hitting, and unorthodox shots, and it has influenced Test batting too, with players now more willing to attack, to play reverse sweeps and scoops, and to score quickly in situations where previous generations would have defended. That evolution is exciting, but it also means that modern Test averages are achieved in a different tactical environment from those of earlier eras. Some traditionalists worry that T20 has eroded defensive technique, while others argue that it has made batsmen more complete. My view is that both effects are real: modern batsmen have a wider range of shots, but they may be less practised in long defensive innings. Judging a modern player against a player from the 1970s therefore requires acknowledging that the game itself has changed, not only the players.

Statistical adjustment is another tool that historians use, and it is worth understanding its limits. Analysts sometimes adjust averages for era, opposition strength, and conditions, producing rankings that differ from raw averages. Those adjustments can be illuminating, for example by showing that a player who averaged 50 against strong attacks was more valuable than one who averaged 55 against weaker ones, but they also involve assumptions that can be debated. No adjustment can fully capture pressure, leadership, or the impact of a single innings on a series. Fans should therefore treat adjusted rankings as interesting arguments, not as definitive answers. The joy of ranking batsmen lies partly in the argument itself, in the memories, the footage, and the stories that statistics alone cannot convey, and that argument will continue as long as cricket is played.
One final thought for readers who want to go deeper: the best way to settle your own ranking is to watch the innings, not just read the numbers. Seek out footage of Bradman's cover drives, Richards hooking fast bowling without a helmet, Lara's 400, Tendulkar's straight drives, and Kohli's chases, and notice how different each player's genius looks. Statistics can tell you how many runs were scored, but only footage, and the commentary and crowd reactions that accompany it, can tell you how those runs felt to those who watched them live. Read match reports from the time, listen to players describing what it was like to bowl to these batsmen, and you will understand why rankings provoke such passion. Your own list will probably differ from this one, and that is exactly as it should be, because cricket's history belongs to its fans as much as to its record books, and every fan's list is a personal history of the game they love.
There is one more pleasure in this debate that is worth naming: it connects generations of fans. A grandfather who saw Sobers, a parent who saw Tendulkar, and a child watching a modern star can argue across those eras with genuine passion, each defending the player who defined cricket for them. That conversation, across ages and countries, is part of what makes cricket a shared culture rather than merely a sport. Rankings give that conversation a starting point, but the conversation itself, with its stories, disagreements, and discoveries, is the real reward, and it will continue as long as new batsmen emerge to challenge the legends who came before them.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Who is the best cricket batsman in history?

Don Bradman is widely regarded as the best, with a Test average of 99.94 that remains far ahead of any other player. The debate is mainly about who ranks second.

### How many centuries did Sachin Tendulkar score?

Sachin Tendulkar scored 100 international centuries, comprising 51 in Tests and 49 in one-day internationals, a record no other player has matched.

### What is Brian Lara's highest Test score?

Brian Lara scored 400 not out against England in 2004, the highest individual score in Test cricket history. He also holds the first-class record of 501 not out.

### Why is Viv Richards considered so great?

Viv Richards combined aggressive batting with remarkable success against fast bowling, often without a helmet, and he dominated attacks in a way that intimidated opponents. His style and impact went beyond his statistics.

### Do modern batsmen compare with older greats?

Modern batsmen face different challenges, including more cricket, better bowling variety, and greater scrutiny, while benefiting from better fitness and equipment. Comparisons across eras require context, not just averages.

### What is a good Test batting average?

A Test average above 50 is generally considered excellent, above 55 is elite, and above 60 is historically rare. Bradman's 99.94 is an extreme outlier.
