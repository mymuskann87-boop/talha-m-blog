On Friday, October 9, 2026, a powerful earthquake struck Panama. Buildings and roads collapsed in some areas, people evacuated tall buildings in the capital, and a tsunami warning was issued for coasts across the region.

Let's go through it slowly and respectfully — what happened, where the damage was worst, and what officials said in the first reports.

## The short version

The US Geological Survey first measured the earthquake at 8.0, then revised it to 7.6 and later to 7.7. Its epicentre was about 10 km from the small town of Pitaloza Arriba in Herrera province, at a depth of about 10 km. A second quake of about 6.6 followed, and more than 80 aftershocks were reported. Buildings and roads collapsed near the centre of the country, but no casualties were immediately reported, according to AP reports. A tsunami warning was issued for several countries, and the threat later passed.

## Why did the size of the earthquake keep changing?

You may have seen three different numbers for this one quake: 8.0, 7.6 and 7.7. That has a simple explanation.

The US Geological Survey first measured it at 8.0, then revised it to 7.6, and later to 7.7. In the first hours, scientists work with early information, and as more readings come in, the estimate is adjusted. A revision is a normal part of how these measurements improve.

Either way, this was a powerful earthquake. Its epicentre — the point on the surface closest to where the quake began — was about 10 km from the small town of Pitaloza Arriba in Herrera province, at a depth of about 10 km, which is relatively shallow. Shallow earthquakes are often felt more strongly at the surface.

## Where was the damage worst?

Damage was concentrated in the Veraguas, Herrera and Los Santos provinces, near the centre of the country. Reports said buildings and roads collapsed in that area, and local TV showed a cathedral in Penonomé that had collapsed.

A collapsed cathedral is a painful image for any community — buildings like that are much loved, and part of a town's identity. Collapsed roads matter too, because they can make it harder for help to reach people.

At the same time, the first reports carried one piece of relief: no casualties were immediately reported, according to AP reports. That means no deaths had been confirmed in those early reports — not that the final picture was already known.

Further away, no damage was reported in the capital, Panama City, about 200 km away. People there still felt the quake, though, and evacuated tall buildings as a safety step.

## A second quake and more than 80 aftershocks

The first earthquake was not the end of the shaking. A second quake of about 6.6 followed, and more than 80 aftershocks were reported.

Aftershocks are smaller earthquakes that follow a main quake in the same area. Some are barely felt, but a quake of about 6.6 is strong in its own right. For people near the epicentre, each aftershock brings fresh worry about already damaged buildings.

This is why officials ask people to stay careful after the first shaking stops. A building weakened by the main quake can be brought down by an aftershock, so staying away from damaged buildings can save lives.

## The tsunami warning — and the threat that passed

After the earthquake, a tsunami warning was issued for the coasts of Panama, Colombia, Costa Rica, Ecuador, Peru, Nicaragua, Guatemala, El Salvador and Mexico. That's a long list of countries, and a warning like that naturally causes fear in coastal communities.

A warning like this is a safety step, telling people near the sea to follow official guidance while experts check whether dangerous waves are coming.

In this case, the threat later passed — welcome news for every country named. That doesn't mean the warning was a mistake: it's far better to warn early and stand down later than to stay silent and be wrong.

## What did Panama's president say, and why do quakes happen there?

President José Raúl Mulino called it a major disaster and asked people to stay calm. That matters in a crisis: calm communities share information more clearly, keep roads open for emergency help, and look after neighbours.

Panama lies along the Pacific "Ring of Fire" — despite the name, this isn't about flames. It's a region around the Pacific Ocean where the earth's crust is especially active, and powerful earthquakes are a known risk.

Living in such a region means preparation matters — clear evacuation habits and quick official warnings help protect people when the ground moves. For communities in Veraguas, Herrera and Los Santos, the coming days will be about recovery. Our thoughts are with everyone affected.

In my view, the most important lesson of the Panama quake is the value of a warning that is issued quickly and lifted honestly. A tsunami warning is a frightening thing to receive, and it asks thousands of people to move, to wait, and to trust experts they cannot see. When the threat passes, as it did here, the temptation is to call the warning a false alarm, or to treat the next warning with less care. That temptation should be resisted. A warning that proves unnecessary is not a failure. It is the system working as it should, giving people time to reach safety while the facts are still being gathered. The post's account of the changing magnitude shows why early figures move: measurements improve as more data arrives, and honesty about that change builds more trust than a single, confident number issued too soon.

Readers should compare two kinds of damage in an earthquake. One kind is the damage of the shaking itself, to buildings, roads, and lives, which cannot be undone once the ground moves. The other is the damage of confusion, of people who do not know where to go, what to trust, or when it is safe to return. Panama's experience, as reported, shows both. Buildings and roads collapsed in some areas, and that loss is real. Yet the evacuation of tall buildings, the tracking of aftershocks, and the clear lifting of the tsunami threat limited the second kind of damage. I think the aftershocks deserve special attention. More than 80 of them, plus a second quake, test nerves long after the first shock, and a public that is told to expect them is far better prepared than one told only that the worst is over.

What should readers understand differently? An earthquake is not a single moment. It is a sequence, and safety lies in respecting the whole sequence: the main shock, the aftershocks, and the official word on when danger has passed. If you live in, or visit, a quake region, learn the safe places, the evacuation routes, and the sources to trust before the ground moves. Panama was warned, shaken, and then spared the worst of the sea. My call is to treat that outcome as a success of preparation and honesty, and to carry the same respect for warnings into the next event, whenever it comes.
