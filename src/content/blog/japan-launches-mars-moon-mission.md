## Entry One: The Pad, Minutes Before Liftoff

Picture a quiet island coast in Japan, in the dark hours before dawn. The sea is calm. The sky is black. And standing on the launch pad at the Tanegashima Space Center, lit up like a small tower of light, sits a spacecraft carrier that is about to do something no mission has ever done.

The local time is coming up on 4:41 in the morning, 20 October 2026. Half a world away in the United States, clocks are showing 3:41 in the afternoon of 19 October. Two countries, two different times on the clock, one single shared moment.

Inside the control rooms, people watch numbers. Outside, the air feels heavy with waiting. A launch is a strange kind of silence. Everything that can be checked has been checked. Everything that can be practised has been practised. Now there is only the counting.

Then the engines speak, the ground shakes, and a bright new traveller climbs into the sky.

Its name is MMX, short for Martian Moons eXploration. And it is not going to Mars itself. It is going somewhere smaller, stranger, and in some ways more mysterious: the moons of Mars.

## Entry Two: Two Tiny Moons With a Big Secret

Mars has two moons, and honestly, they look like a mistake. They are not round and smooth like our Moon. They are small, dark, and lumpy, more like giant potatoes tumbling through space than proper moons.

The bigger one is called Phobos. The smaller one is Deimos. For a long time, scientists have argued about a simple question: where did these two odd little worlds come from?

There are two main ideas. In the first idea, the moons are captured asteroids, wandering space rocks that drifted too close to Mars long ago and got trapped by its gravity. In the second idea, they were born from Mars itself, formed from the dust and debris thrown up when something huge crashed into the young planet.

Both ideas sound believable. Neither one is proven. And the argument cannot be settled by looking through telescopes from far away.

It can only be settled by going there, touching the ground, and bringing a piece of it home.

That is exactly what MMX has set out to do. The mission, led by the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency, known as JAXA, with NASA taking part as well, plans to study Phobos and Deimos in detail, work out how they formed, and collect samples of surface material to carry all the way back to Earth.

## Entry Three: A Long, Quiet Road Through Space

A trip to Mars is not like a trip to the next city. There is no rushing it. Once the spacecraft leaves Earth, it settles into a long cruise through the dark, and the calendar becomes its biggest enemy.

The plan gives MMX an arrival at Mars in August 2027. That means months of travel lie between the fiery launch morning in Japan and the moment the spacecraft finally sees the red planet growing large in front of it.

During those months, the mission becomes a test of patience. Engineers on Earth will watch over their travelling machine the whole way, checking its health, guiding its path, and preparing for the delicate work waiting at the other end. Every signal takes time to cross the huge distance. Every answer from the spacecraft arrives late. Space travel teaches everyone involved the same lesson: good things here move slowly, and they must be handled gently.

## Entry Four: Two Space Agencies, One Team

MMX is a Japanese-led mission, but it is not a solo journey. NASA is part of the adventure too, and the American contribution is a practical one.

The United States is providing scientific instruments, the kind of tools a spacecraft needs to examine strange new ground. It is also providing sample collection technology, which matters enormously, because picking up pieces of an alien moon and keeping them safe for the long ride home is one of the hardest parts of the whole plan. On top of that comes communications and navigation support, the invisible lifeline that lets the spacecraft talk to Earth and stay on course across hundreds of millions of kilometres of empty space.

The teamwork does not end with hardware. NASA-funded researchers will work side by side with international specialists to analyse the data the mission sends back. Different countries, different universities, different accents, one shared puzzle: what are the moons of Mars really made of, and what story do they carry?

## Entry Five: Why a Handful of Moon Dust Matters

It is fair to ask why anyone would spend years of effort and cross the solar system just to fetch some dust and small rocks from a lumpy little moon.

The answer is that this dust is a message from the distant past.

If Phobos and Deimos are captured asteroids, their material holds clues about the raw ingredients that floated around the young solar system. If they were born from a crash into Mars, their material is a piece of Mars history itself, a page from the planet's earliest chapters that no rover standing on the Martian surface could ever reach.

Either way, the samples win. Scientists will be able to hold material from the Mars system in their laboratories on Earth, study it with the best instruments humans have, and test ideas that today exist only as arguments.

Nicky Fox, the head of NASA's Science Mission Directorate, has said that the samples could lead to fundamental discoveries about the Martian system for decades to come. Decades. Sit with that thought a second. A mission launched on an October morning in Japan may still be giving scientists new answers when today's schoolchildren are grown up and running laboratories of their own.

And there is one more reason, looking even further ahead. As NASA also prepares for future crewed missions to Mars, every lesson learned near the red planet counts. Its two small moons are part of that neighbourhood. Understanding them is part of understanding where human explorers may one day travel.

Personally, I find something deeply moving in the thought that a tiny piece of a faraway moon will one day rest in a laboratory on Earth, quietly answering questions that people have asked for generations.

## Entry Six: What Waits at the Red Planet

So the log of this mission now stands at an exciting place. The launch chapter is written. The long cruise chapter is being written right now, day by quiet day, as the spacecraft crosses the dark between worlds.

Ahead of it waits August 2027, and a rendezvous with Mars. Ahead of that wait the two moons themselves, Phobos with its slow, close orbit, Deimos small and distant. Ahead of that, the most delicate work of all: studying the moons closely, choosing where to touch down, gathering the precious surface material, and starting the long journey home.

No part of the road ahead is easy. But that is what makes this mission worth following. For the first time, a spacecraft has been sent on this exact errand: visit the moons of Mars, learn where they came from, and bring a piece of one back to Earth.

The countdown is over. The travel has begun. And somewhere out there, between our blue world and the red one, a small machine is flying through the night, carrying the hopes of two space agencies and the curiosity of everyone who has ever looked up at Mars and wondered about the two strange little moons beside it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the MMX mission?

MMX stands for Martian Moons eXploration. It is a mission led by the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency (JAXA), with NASA participating, created to study the two moons of Mars and bring samples of surface material back to Earth.

### Which moons will MMX study?

The mission will study Phobos and Deimos, the two small, lumpy moons of Mars. One of its main goals is to determine how these moons formed, whether they are captured asteroids or debris left over from an ancient impact on Mars.

### When did MMX launch, and from where?

The mission launched from the Tanegashima Space Center in Japan. Liftoff was scheduled for 3:41 p.m. U.S. Eastern Daylight Time on 19 October 2026, which was 4:41 a.m. on 20 October in Japan.

### When will the spacecraft reach Mars?

The spacecraft is scheduled to arrive at Mars in August 2027, after a long cruise through space following its launch from Earth.

### What is NASA contributing to the mission?

The U.S. contribution includes scientific instruments, sample collection technology, and communications and navigation support. NASA-funded researchers will also work alongside international specialists to analyse the data returned by the mission.
