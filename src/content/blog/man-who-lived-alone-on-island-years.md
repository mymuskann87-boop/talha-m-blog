The man who lived alone on an island for years, and whose story became Robinson Crusoe, was Alexander Selkirk, a Scottish privateer marooned for four years and four months, from 1704 to 1709, on an uninhabited island in the Juan Fernández group in the South Pacific. He was not shipwrecked by accident in the way Crusoe was. He asked to be put ashore, after a dispute with his captain about whether their ship was seaworthy, and then, when the ship was about to leave, he begged to be taken back — and was refused. He lived alone, without a Friday, without a companion, and without a rescue for more than four years, until a British privateer found him in February 1709. His story is not a novel. It is a record of what solitude, hunger, injury, and ingenuity actually do to a person.

## A Scottish Sailor Who Argued About a Leaking Ship

Alexander Selkirk, also spelled Selcraig, was born in 1676 in Largo, Fife, Scotland, the seventh son of John Selcraig, a shoemaker and tanner. He was, by contemporary accounts, an unruly youth, and he joined buccaneering and privateering voyages to the South Pacific during the War of the Spanish Succession. Privateering was state-licensed piracy: a private ship, authorised by a government, attacked enemy merchant ships and shared the prize. It was legal, profitable, and violent, and it attracted men who were brave, argumentative, and unwilling to accept orders they thought were foolish.

In May 1703, Selkirk joined an expedition under William Dampier, sailing as sailing master on the Cinque Ports, a galley of 96 tons and 16 guns, captained by Thomas Stradling. The ship sailed around Cape Horn and north along the Chilean coast, reaching the Juan Fernández Islands, west of Valparaíso, in September 1704 to resupply. There, Selkirk argued that the ship's caulking was insufficient, that it would leak so badly it would sink, and that it should be repaired before sailing on. Stradling disagreed. The dispute became personal, and Selkirk, in anger and confidence, asked to be put ashore.

That request is crucial, because it changes the moral of the story. Selkirk was not a victim of a captain's cruelty in the simple sense. He chose the island, believing a passing ship would rescue him within weeks or a couple of months, because the Juan Fernández group had long been used as a pirate and privateering base, with ships calling regularly. He was, in that belief, wrong about time, but right about the ship: Cinque Ports later foundered near Malpelo Island, about 400 kilometres from the coast of what is now Colombia, and its surviving crew were taken to a Spanish prison. Selkirk's judgement about the ship was correct. His judgement about rescue was catastrophically optimistic, and he paid for that optimism with four years alone.

## Put Ashore With Almost Nothing

When Selkirk was put ashore in September or October 1704 — accounts differ on the exact month, with the island tablet giving 1704 and the rescue in February 1709 — he was given only a few ordinary necessaries. The lists in early accounts, including Woodes Rogers's Cruising Voyage Round the World (1712) and Edward Cooke's Voyage in the South Sea (1712), include clothes, bedding, a musket, gunpowder and shot, a knife, a kettle, a Bible, and some other small items, but no large store of food, no tools for building a proper house, and no companion. Before Cinque Ports left, Selkirk begged to be readmitted. Stradling refused. That refusal, in full view of the crew, is the moment his story turns from an argument into a marooning.

His first weeks, by his own later account as reported by Richard Steele in The Englishman on 3 December 1713, were the worst. He had food for only a short time, and the island, now called Isla Robinson Crusoe (formerly Isla Más a Tierra), is crescent-shaped, mountainous in the centre, with a long thin peninsula at the western end. It had fresh water, vegetation, fish, lobsters, sea lions in abundance, and feral goats left by earlier voyages, but it had no people, no houses, and no help. A sailor used to a crew, to orders, to conversation, and to a ship's routine, suddenly had to provide every calorie, every shelter, and every decision himself, while watching the sea for a ship that did not come.

He reportedly spent his first months in misery, weeping, and struggling to sleep, haunted by the sound of the sea and by his own decision. That psychological detail, preserved in early accounts, is more valuable than any list of survival tricks, because it shows that the first survival problem was not food. It was despair. A man who cannot sleep, cannot think clearly, cannot hunt well, and cannot plan. Selkirk's recovery began, by his account, when he forced himself into routine: hunting, building, reading his Bible, and watching from a lookout point that later became known as Selkirk's Lookout, where a tablet was erected in 1868 by Commodore Powell and officers of HMS Topaze.

## How He Ate, Slept, and Stayed Alive

Selkirk survived by hunting feral goats, which he at first shot with his musket, and later, when gunpowder ran out, chased on foot until he could catch them by hand. He learned to run, climb, and cut with a knife, and, by Rogers's account, became so agile that he could outrun goats over rocky ground. He ate goat meat, fish, lobsters, turnips and other plants that grew on the island, and he drank fresh water from streams. He built two huts from pimento wood, one for sleeping and one for cooking, and he made fire by rubbing wood, after his initial supplies ran out.

He also made clothes from goat skins, using a nail as a needle and goat sinew as thread, after his original clothes wore out. That detail — a nail as a needle — is a useful measure of ingenuity: survival was not about having the right kit, but about repurposing the wrong kit until it worked. He tamed cats and kids (young goats) for company, and he kept a small garden of sorts, but he never had a companion human. The cats, in particular, reportedly protected him from rats at night, which had attacked him while he slept. That is a small, practical alliance: a man alone, rats at night, cats as guards, and sleep as a resource to be defended.

Injury was a constant risk, and one injury nearly killed him. While chasing a goat, he fell down a precipice and was knocked unconscious, lying there for about 24 hours before, by his account, he regained consciousness and crawled back to his hut. A fall that would have been survivable with a crew to find him became life-threatening alone, because no one could fetch water, dress a wound, or watch for infection. He survived that fall, but it taught him the central rule of solo survival: avoid injury at all costs, because there is no second person to compensate for your mistake. That rule, more than any hunting skill, explains why he lived: he learned to be cautious, even when hunger urged him to take risks.

## Four Years Without a Human Voice

Selkirk's solitude lasted four years and four months, from 1704 to 31 January 1709, when he was found, and 12 February 1709, when he was taken off by Captain Woodes Rogers, commander of the Duke privateer, with Dampier as pilot. During that time, he saw ships, but they were Spanish, and he hid from them, fearing capture, imprisonment, or death as an enemy privateer in Spanish waters. That hiding is an important correction to the romantic image of a castaway waving at every sail. For Selkirk, a ship was not automatically rescue. It could be an enemy, and an enemy ship meant a Spanish prison, the fate his former shipmates had suffered. He therefore watched, judged, and hid, for years, while rescue, in the form of a British ship, did not come.

That long, watchful solitude changed his speech and behaviour. When Rogers's men found him, they reported he was dressed in goat skins, looked wild, and had almost forgotten how to speak English fluently, speaking slowly and with difficulty at first. He had, by some accounts, taught himself to sing psalms and to read his Bible aloud to keep his language alive, but without conversation, language atrophies. That detail is a direct lesson in what solitude does to the brain: language is a social skill, and without social use, it fades, even in an educated adult. Selkirk's recovery of speech after rescue was quick, but the initial difficulty shows that four years alone is not only a physical ordeal. It is a cognitive one.

He also, by Rogers's account, had become expert at his island life in a way that made rescue bittersweet. He could hunt, build, and live without money, orders, or society, and he was, in that narrow sense, free. Yet he chose rescue, and he chose to return to a world of crews, captains, and wars, because freedom without company is not freedom most humans want for long. He joined Rogers's crew as mate, later commanded a prize ship, the Increase, and returned to the Thames on 14 October 1711, and to Largo in 1712. He went to sea again in 1717, and died on 12 December 1721 as master's mate of HMS Weymouth, aged 45 by some accounts, 47 by the tablet's erroneous inscription. His island years did not make him rich or famous in his lifetime, except as a story told in London coffee houses.

## Selkirk, Crusoe, and the Difference That Matters

Daniel Defoe published The Life and Adventures of Robinson Crusoe in 1719, shortly after a second edition of Rogers's Voyage appeared in 1718. Defoe's novel is clearly indebted in its main outlines to Selkirk's story, but most of its incidents are independent, as the 1911 Encyclopaedia Britannica notes. Crusoe is shipwrecked, not deliberately marooned. Crusoe's island is tropical, off South America in the novel's geography, not the temperate, mountainous Juan Fernández island. Crusoe survives 28 years in the novel (27 years in some summaries), not four years and four months. Crusoe finds Friday, a companion, while Selkirk never had one. Crusoe salvages tools, guns, and supplies from his wreck, while Selkirk had only what he carried ashore.

Those differences matter because readers often transfer Crusoe's comforts to Selkirk. Crusoe builds a fort, farms, makes pottery, and talks to Friday. Selkirk built two huts, hunted goats, wore skins, and talked to cats. Crusoe's story is about civilisation rebuilt alone. Selkirk's story is about civilisation stripped away, and about a man who kept enough of it — language, faith, routine, caution — to survive until rescue, without rebuilding a colony. Defoe's genius was to turn a sailor's ordeal into a novel about self-reliance. Selkirk's reality was less systematic and more precarious: a fall, a rat, a Spanish ship, or a failed hunt could have ended it at any point.

The island itself was renamed to honour the connection: in 1962, the Chilean government officially renamed the archipelago the Robinson Crusoe Islands, with Más a Tierra becoming Isla Robinson Crusoe and Más Afuera becoming Isla Alejandro Selkirk. That renaming, done to stimulate tourism, is ironic: the island that made Selkirk famous is now named for a fictional character, while the real man's name is given to a neighbouring, more remote island. Tourists visit Selkirk's Lookout, where the 1868 tablet reads, in part, that he lived “in complete solitude for four years and four months.” That phrase — complete solitude — is the accurate summary, and it is what separates Selkirk from every fictional castaway who had a companion, a wreck to salvage, or a rescue within months.

## What Selkirk Ate, and Why Goats Changed the Island

Selkirk's diet deserves a specific note, because it shows how an introduced species can both save and damage an island. Feral goats, left by earlier sailors as a living food store for future ships, gave Selkirk meat, milk from kids, and skins for clothes, without farming. That ready food is why a single man could survive four years without a garden large enough to feed him. It is also why, over centuries, goats damaged Juan Fernández vegetation, eating native plants and preventing forest regrowth, a problem conservationists still manage on the island today. Selkirk's survival, in other words, depended on an ecological change made by earlier visitors, and his hunting, by reducing goat numbers locally, may have briefly helped native plants near his huts, even as goats remained a problem island-wide.

His water and shelter choices were equally specific. Fresh streams gave him drinking water without desalination, pimento wood gave him huts that resisted rot and insects better than softer wood, and a hill lookout gave him both a view of approaching ships and a daily walk that structured his day. None of those resources required invention. All of them required noticing: which stream ran year-round, which wood lasted, which hill saw furthest. Survival, in Selkirk's case, was less about inventing tools and more about observing an island carefully for months, then using what was already there in a disciplined routine. That observation skill — watching, testing, remembering — is transferable to any new place, job, or crisis, where the first task is to learn the local resources before trying to change them.

## My Take:

Selkirk's decision to ask to be put ashore is, in my view, the most instructive mistake in survival literature. He was right about the ship and wrong about rescue, and both judgements were made with the same evidence: the ship was leaking, and ships visited the island. He weighed a certain risk — sailing on a sinking ship — against an uncertain risk — waiting alone — and chose the uncertain risk, because it felt like control. That is a common human error: we prefer a risk we choose, even if it is larger, to a risk imposed on us, even if it is smaller. Selkirk's four years are the price of that preference, and they should make any reader pause before choosing a dramatic, self-imposed hardship over a boring, imposed one that can be managed with others.

I think his first months, weeping and unable to sleep, are more important than his later goat-hunting skill. Survival manuals focus on food, fire, and shelter, as if those are the first problems. Selkirk shows that despair is the first problem, and that routine is the first solution. He did not survive by being the strongest man on the island. He survived by forcing himself to hunt, build, read, and watch, day after day, until routine replaced panic. That is a lesson that applies far beyond islands: after a job loss, a bereavement, or a long illness, the first task is not to solve everything. It is to establish a small, repeatable day — food, work, rest, a walk to a lookout — and let that day carry you until your mind recovers enough to plan further. Selkirk's lookout was not only for ships. It was for structure.

His hiding from Spanish ships also corrects a naive view of rescue. We assume any passing ship is salvation. For Selkirk, a ship could be prison, and hiding was rational, even though it prolonged solitude. That trade-off — safety versus rescue, freedom versus company — is one that people in difficult situations face repeatedly: a job that is safe but deadening, a relationship that is familiar but harmful, a country that is home but dangerous. Selkirk chose to wait for the right ship, not any ship, and that patience, while costly, kept him from a Spanish prison. Patience is often praised as a virtue, but here it was a strategy with a clear cost: more years alone, in exchange for not being captured. That is a harder, more honest version of patience than the proverb.

The loss of speech after four years should alarm any reader who thinks solitude is romantic. Selkirk, an adult who had spoken English all his life, struggled to speak when found. He had kept language alive by reading and singing, but without conversation, it faded. That is a warning for modern solitude, which is often voluntary: working from home, living alone, communicating only by text, and going days without a real conversation. The brain, like a muscle, needs social use to stay fluent, not only in language, but in empathy, humour, and timing. Selkirk's cats and goats gave him company, but they could not give him conversation, and the difference showed in his first words to Rogers's men. A castaway story that ends with “he forgot how to talk” is not a romance. It is a caution.

I also think Defoe's changes to Selkirk's story tell us what readers want from survival. Defoe gave Crusoe a wreck full of tools, a companion in Friday, a tropical island, and 28 years to build a civilisation. Readers loved it, because it turns survival into progress: each year, Crusoe is better off, more civilised, more in control. Selkirk's real story offers no such progress. Each year, he was older, more worn, more alone, and no closer to rescue, except by chance. Defoe's version is more comforting, but Selkirk's version is more truthful: survival is often not progress, but endurance, and endurance does not always feel heroic. It feels like hunting the same goats, watching the same sea, and waiting for a ship that may be Spanish.

Finally, Selkirk's return to sea after rescue, and his death at sea in 1721, should prevent a simple happy ending. He was not “saved” in the sense of living happily ever after on land. He returned to the same world of privateering, crews, and captains that had marooned him, because that was the work he knew, and because island life, for all its freedom, had no wages, no family, and no future. Rescue is not the end of a survival story. It is the start of a second adjustment: learning to speak again, to live with others again, to accept orders again, after years of being your own captain. Selkirk managed that adjustment well enough to become a mate and a prize captain, but he never became a rich or settled man. His island years gave him a story, not a fortune, and that, in my view, is the most honest ending of all: survival gives you life back, not a reward, and life back is enough, if you use it.

There is a final, practical point about rescue that Selkirk's story clarifies. Rescue did not come because he signalled well, built a large fire, or wrote a message in sand, as in films. It came because a British privateer, on a similar mission to his own original voyage, happened to call at the same island group, for the same reason his first ship had: resupply. That means his rescue depended on the island remaining a known stopping point, not on his individual signalling. A castaway on a truly unknown island, with no reason for ships to call, faces a different, much harder problem. Selkirk's island was remote, but it was on a route, and that route knowledge — that ships used Juan Fernández — was part of why he asked to be put ashore in the first place. His gamble was not “someone will find me anywhere.” It was “ships come here,” and that specific, route-based gamble, while slow to pay off, was rational in a way a random island gamble would not be.

That route knowledge also explains why he hid from Spanish ships rather than signalling them. A Spanish ship on that route was not a rescue ship for an English privateer. It was a capture ship, and capture meant prison in Spanish America, as his former shipmates learned. Hiding, therefore, was not paranoia. It was a correct assessment of who used the route and what they would do with an enemy sailor. Modern readers, who assume any rescuer is neutral, miss that political layer, but it was central to Selkirk's daily decisions for four years: watch every sail, judge its flag and course, hide if it is Spanish, reveal yourself only if it is British. Solitude, in his case, was not only physical isolation. It was political isolation, where the wrong rescue was worse than no rescue.
