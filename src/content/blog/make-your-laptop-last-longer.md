The first boot is a small ceremony. The box opens, the plastic peels away, the screen lights up clean and fast, and for a moment the laptop feels like it will be fast forever. Then life happens. It rides in bags without padding. It sits on blankets that block its vents. It stays plugged in for months at a time. Three years later it groans at every task, and its owner starts shopping for a replacement — even though the machine itself was built to last far longer.

Most laptops do not die of old age. They die of heat, dust, drops, and neglect. Treat those four enemies well, and an ordinary laptop can serve five, six, even seven years. That is the whole secret, and it costs almost nothing.

## Heat is the enemy that never sleeps

Every laptop makes heat while it works, and it must breathe that heat out through its vents. When the vents are blocked, the heat stays inside, and the machine slows itself down to survive. Over months and years, constant heat wears down the battery and stresses the parts inside.

The fix is mostly about surfaces. Use the laptop on a hard, flat table where air can move underneath it. A bed, a blanket, or a lap blocks the vents underneath and turns the laptop into a small oven. If you often work on soft surfaces, a simple laptop stand or even a hard board underneath changes everything. Keep the vents clear of dust too — a gentle blast of compressed air through the vents every few months, with the laptop switched off, clears the blanket of dust that builds up inside.

Watch for the warning signs. A fan that screams all day, a base too hot to rest your hands on, a laptop that slows down after twenty minutes of work — these are the machine asking for air. Give it air before it gives up. In my view, heat is the quiet killer of laptops, far more than age, because it works slowly and nobody notices until the damage is done.

## Charge wisely and the battery will thank you

The battery is usually the first part of a laptop to fade, and charging habits decide how fast that happens. Two extremes wear a battery out: sitting at 100% charge for weeks on end, and running down to empty again and again. The middle is kinder.

If you mostly use the laptop plugged in at a desk, many laptops offer a setting that caps charging at around 80%. Use it if your machine has it. If you use the laptop on battery, try not to let it die completely before charging — plugging in around 20% is a gentler habit. And if the laptop will sit unused for weeks, store it at roughly half charge in a cool place, not fully charged in a hot room.

None of this needs obsession. Batteries are consumables; they fade no matter what. The point is to slow the fade from two years to four or five, so the battery is replaced once, cheaply, instead of the whole laptop being replaced early.

## Clean it gently and regularly

A laptop collects more dirt than its owner admits. Crumbs between keys, fingerprints on the screen, dust in the vents, a sticky touchpad. Cleaning is not vanity — it is maintenance. A keyboard full of crumbs can jam keys. A screen wiped with a rough cloth can scratch. Dust in the vents becomes the heat problem from the first section.

Clean the screen with a soft, slightly damp microfibre cloth, never with paper towels or harsh cleaners. Wipe the keyboard with the same cloth, and tip the laptop gently upside down now and then to shake loose crumbs. Keep food and drinks at a small distance — one spilled cup of tea has ended more laptops than any virus. For example, a laptop that survives six years without a spill costs its owner nothing extra, while one ruined by a single cup needs a replacement worth years of careful saving.

## Keep the software lean so the machine stays fast

A laptop that feels slow is often not dying — it is drowning. Programs installed years ago still start with the machine. Browser tabs multiply. The hard drive fills to the last corner, leaving no room for the system to breathe.

Twice a year, spend an hour on a software clean-up. Remove programs you no longer use. Stop unnecessary programs from starting automatically. Move old photos and videos to an external drive or cloud storage, and leave proper free space on the main drive. Install system updates when they arrive, since makers fix slowness and security problems through them.

This hour is worth real money. A laptop that feels fast does not get replaced. A laptop that feels slow gets replaced even when its parts are perfectly healthy. Most "old laptop" complaints are software complaints wearing a hardware costume.

## Carry it like it matters, because it does

Most laptop deaths are drops, not age. A laptop bouncing loose in a bag full of books, chargers, and keys is an accident waiting for a bus ride. A padded sleeve or a laptop compartment costs little and absorbs the knocks that crack screens and dent corners.

The same care applies at home. Do not lift a laptop by its screen — the hinges are not handles. Do not stack heavy things on a closed laptop. Close it gently rather than slamming it, and let it sleep properly instead of stuffing it hot into a bag, where it will cook itself in its own heat.

For example, compare two identical laptops bought on the same day. One travels in a padded sleeve, lives on a desk, and gets cleaned twice a year. The other rides loose, works on a blanket, and never gets cleaned. After four years the first still earns its keep, while the second is already shopping for its replacement. The difference was never the machine. It was the handling.

## The honest limits of laptop care

Care extends life, but it does not grant immortality. Batteries eventually fade and need replacing — a normal, affordable repair. Screens crack, keyboards wear out, and some parts simply reach their end. When a repair costs more than the laptop is worth, replacement is the sensible choice, not a failure of care.

The goal was never to keep one laptop forever. The goal is to replace it when you choose to, for a better machine or a real need — not when a preventable death forces your hand. A laptop that lasts six years instead of three halves what you spend per year on computing. That is saving, done quietly, one habit at a time.

## Common questions

### How long should a laptop last with good care?

With decent care — cool surfaces, sensible charging, regular cleaning, and safe carrying — an ordinary laptop can serve five to seven years for everyday work. Batteries may need replacing once in that time, which is normal and far cheaper than a new machine.

### Does heat really damage a laptop?

Yes. Blocked vents trap heat inside, and constant heat wears down the battery and stresses internal parts. Using the laptop on a hard surface, keeping vents clear of dust, and giving it air are the simplest ways to protect it.

### Should I keep my laptop plugged in all the time?

It is better to avoid keeping it at 100% charge for weeks on end. If your laptop offers a charge limit around 80% for desk use, switch it on. Otherwise, let the battery cycle gently — charge around 20% and avoid running it to empty every time.

### Why is my laptop slow — is it dying?

Often it is not dying at all. Too many startup programs, a full drive, and years of unused software make a healthy laptop feel slow. A twice-yearly clean-up — removing unused programs, freeing drive space, and installing updates — restores most of the lost speed.

### How can I protect my laptop while travelling?

Carry it in a padded sleeve or a dedicated laptop compartment, never loose among books and keys. Lift it by its base, not the screen, and let it cool before slipping it into a bag. Most laptop deaths are drops, and padding is the cheapest insurance there is.
