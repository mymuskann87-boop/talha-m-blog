When the bins are not collected, the roads are full of potholes, or the water stops running, residents blame one person: the mayor. When a city feels well run, the same person gets the credit. But what does a mayor actually do, day by day, and how much power does the office really hold? The answer is less simple than the blame suggests, because mayors in different cities hold very different amounts of power. This post explains the job in simple words: the five main responsibilities, the difference between strong and weak mayors, and the unelected official who often does much of the daily running of a city.

## The Head Of The City

A mayor is the highest-ranking municipal executive in a city or town. The exact powers of the office vary enormously from place to place, but the core idea is the same everywhere: the mayor is the person at the top of the city's government, answerable to the people who live there. In most places, mayors are elected by the residents themselves, for a term of two or four years, although in some systems the council chooses the mayor from among its own members.

The mayor's duties, as described in general reference works, cover a wide range: overseeing the work of city departments, executing the laws and ordinances passed by the council, appointing and overseeing officials, and managing the delivery of public services. The mayor is the public face of the city, the person who speaks for it to higher levels of government and to the outside world. When a city faces a crisis, a flood, a fire, or a major accident, it is the mayor residents expect to see, and to hear from.

## Five Responsibilities That Define The Job

MasterClass, in its guide to the office, lists five responsibilities that define what a mayor does. The first is political leadership: the mayor is the political leader of the city, setting its direction and representing its people. The second is policy generation: the mayor proposes policies, plans and a budget, and works to get them adopted. The third is working with the council: the mayor must build support among councillors for those proposals, because in most systems the council must approve them. The fourth is leading government agencies: the mayor oversees the departments that deliver services, from water to roads to refuse collection. The fifth is appointments: the mayor appoints officials, and in many systems can remove them too.

Those five cover the whole job, but the balance between them changes from city to city. In some cities, the mayor proposes the budget and the council can only amend it. In others, the council writes the budget and the mayor can only suggest changes. In some, the mayor appoints every department head. In others, the council must confirm each appointment. The title is the same. The power is not.

## Strong Mayors And Weak Mayors

The difference between a strong-mayor system and a weak-mayor system is the single most important distinction in understanding any mayor. In a strong-mayor system, such as those in New York City and Chicago, the mayor has considerable power: appointing and removing department heads, preparing the budget, vetoing council decisions, and directing the administration. The mayor is, in effect, the city's chief executive, with a council that checks and approves but does not run the departments.

In a weak-mayor system, such as Dallas, the mayor has much less executive power. The council holds more of the authority, and the mayor's role is closer to that of a chairperson: presiding over meetings, representing the city, and having one vote among many. The day-to-day administration is often in the hands of a professional city manager. A weak mayor can still be influential, through persuasion and public standing, but cannot simply order a department to act. The label "weak" does not mean the mayor is a weak person. It means the office itself was designed to share power with the council.

## The City Manager: The Unelected Executive

In many cities, especially in weak-mayor systems, the daily running of the city is done by a city manager. The city manager is an unelected professional, appointed by the council, whose job is to implement the laws and policies the council adopts, manage the city's departments, prepare the budget for the council's approval, and run the administration. The mayor and council set the direction. The city manager makes it happen.

The distinction matters for residents. When a service fails, the fault may lie with the elected leadership that set the policy, or with the administration that failed to deliver it, or with both. Knowing which is which helps residents direct their complaints to the right place. It also explains why some cities seem well run regardless of who is mayor: a strong professional administration can keep services working through political changes, while a weak one can make even a good mayor look helpless.

## Working With The Council

Whatever the system, a mayor must work with a council. The council is the city's legislature: it passes local laws, called ordinances or bylaws, approves the budget, and oversees the administration. In strong-mayor cities, the mayor can veto council decisions, and the council can override the veto with a larger majority. In weak-mayor cities, the council holds the initiative, and the mayor must persuade a majority of councillors to support each proposal.

This is why coalition politics matters at local level. When no party has a majority on a council, the mayor must build support across party lines, and may depend on a coalition agreement to survive a vote of no confidence. The mayor's job is therefore partly political and partly managerial: keeping the council together while keeping the services running. A mayor who can do one but not the other will struggle, because the two halves of the job feed each other. A council that trusts the mayor will back the budget. A budget that delivers services will keep the council's trust.

## What A Mayor Cannot Do

A mayor's power has clear limits, and residents are often surprised by them. A mayor cannot change national law, cannot set national taxes, and cannot order the police or courts in ways that belong to higher levels of government. A mayor cannot spend money the council has not approved, in most systems, and cannot ignore the council's laws. In countries with a federal structure, such as Pakistan, a mayor's authority is further limited by the division of powers between the federal government, the province, and the local council.

A mayor also cannot fix everything at once. Municipal budgets are limited, and the biggest costs — water, sanitation, electricity distribution, roads, refuse removal — consume most of the money before any new project is considered. A mayor who promises a new park, a new road and lower taxes in the same speech is promising three things that pull against each other. The honest test of a mayor is not the list of promises, but what was delivered with the money actually available.

## How Mayors Are Chosen

Mayors are chosen in two main ways. In most large cities, residents elect the mayor directly, by voting for a named person. The term is usually two or four years, and the mayor may stand for re-election. In other systems, the council elects the mayor from among its members, often as part of a coalition negotiation, and the mayor can be removed by a vote of the council. The direct-election model gives the mayor a personal mandate from the voters. The council-election model ties the mayor closely to the council majority, and makes the mayor more dependent on keeping that majority together.

Both models are democratic, but they create different kinds of mayor. A directly elected mayor can claim to speak for the whole city, and can sometimes overrule the council's objections by appealing to the voters. A council-elected mayor must keep the council's confidence every day, and is closer to a prime minister at local level. Knowing which model a city uses explains much of how its mayor behaves.

## Why The Job Matters To Daily Life

The mayor's job matters because local government touches daily life more directly than any other level. Municipalities handle water, sanitation, electricity distribution, refuse removal, local roads, planning, and municipal finances. When those services work, life is easier in ways people barely notice. When they fail, the failure is immediate and personal: a tap that runs dry, a street that floods, a bin that stays full for a week.

That is why the question "what does a mayor do" is worth asking before an election, not after one. The answer is not one thing, but five: lead politically, propose policy, work with the council, lead the city's departments, and appoint the people who run them. A good mayor does all five, within the limits of the system, and is judged by residents on whether the services they depend on actually work.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What does a mayor do in simple words?**

A mayor is the highest-ranking executive of a city or town, leading its government and answering to its residents. The job covers five main responsibilities: political leadership, proposing policies and a budget, working with the council to get them approved, leading the city departments that deliver services, and appointing officials. In practice, the mayor is the public face of the city, the person who speaks for it to higher levels of government and is expected to be visible when the city faces a crisis.

**Is a mayor more powerful than the city council?**

It depends on the system. In a strong-mayor system, such as New York City or Chicago, the mayor has considerable power to appoint and remove department heads, prepare the budget, and veto council decisions. In a weak-mayor system, such as Dallas, the council holds more authority, the mayor's role is closer to that of a chairperson, and the day-to-day administration is often run by a professional city manager. The title is the same in both systems, but the power of the office is very different.

**What is the difference between a mayor and a city manager?**

A mayor is an elected political leader, while a city manager is an unelected professional appointed by the council. The mayor and council set the direction of the city, and the city manager implements the laws and policies the council adopts, manages the departments, prepares the budget, and runs the administration day to day. The distinction matters for residents: when a service fails, the fault may lie with the elected leadership that set the policy, or with the administration that failed to deliver it, or with both.

**How is a mayor elected?**

In other systems, the council elects the mayor from among its own members, often as part of a coalition negotiation, and the mayor can be removed by a vote of the council. **What services does a mayor control?**
