Open any gamer's shelf — physical or digital — and you will find the same quiet confession. Rows of games, most of them barely touched. One finished with love. Three started and abandoned. A dozen bought in a sale and never opened. The shelf is not a library. It is a receipt drawer with better artwork.

Video games are electronic games played on screens, and they are genuinely good fun — this post is written by someone who enjoys them, not someone scolding them. But gaming has a spending trap built into it: new releases, seasonal sales, extra content, and the feeling that everyone else is already playing the new thing. The way out is not to stop playing. It is to play what you already own, buy with patience, and give gaming a budget like any other pleasure.

## Finish the backlog before buying anything new

The backlog is the pile of games you own but have not finished. Every gamer has one. It grows because buying feels like playing — the excitement of a new purchase borrows the joy of the game itself, and by the time the download finishes, the excitement has moved on to the next purchase.

So make a rule: no new game until one backlog game is finished or honestly abandoned. Not punished through — finished, or deliberately set aside after a fair try. In my view, the backlog is the best game store you already own, because every title in it was chosen by you, paid for by you, and is waiting without another charge.

This single rule changes the maths of gaming. A player with ten unfinished games owns months of entertainment already. Buying an eleventh game before touching the ten does not add fun — it adds guilt and a thinner wallet. Work through the shelf first. The new releases will still be there when you reach them, usually cheaper.

## Wait for genuine sales instead of launch-day prices

New games cost the most on the day they launch, and that price is aimed at impatience. Within months, most big titles go on sale — sometimes for half the launch price or less — during seasonal sales, holiday events, and anniversary offers. The game has not changed. Only the price has, and patience is what collects the difference.

Make a wishlist instead of a purchase. Put the games you want on it, set a price you are willing to pay, and wait. When a sale reaches your price, buy it then. This turns sales from traps into tools: instead of a sale persuading you to buy something you never wanted, your wishlist tells the sale exactly what you are waiting for.

Beware the false sale, too. A discount on a game you would never have bought at full price is not a saving — it is spending dressed as saving. The question before every sale purchase is simple: "Would I have wanted this game yesterday, before the discount appeared?" If the answer is no, the discount is doing the wanting for you.

## Try before you buy with free games and free weekends

The cheapest game is the one you discover you do not like before paying for it. Free games, free weekends, and trial versions exist for exactly this reason. Many excellent games cost nothing at all, supported by optional extras you can ignore. Many paid games offer free weekends where the full game opens for a few days.

Use these chances properly. Play the free weekend fully before deciding. If the game still excites you on the last free day, it is worth its price. If your interest faded by day two, the free weekend just saved you the full cost. The same applies to free games: try several, keep the ones that hold you, and delete the rest without guilt. A free game you love beats a paid game you abandon.

Watch the extras, though. Free games often sell costumes, shortcuts, and currency inside the game. Those small purchases add up faster than a single game purchase, because each one feels too small to count. Count them. A monthly total for in-game purchases, written down honestly, ends most overspending on its own.

## Give gaming a monthly budget like any other pleasure

Gaming deserves a budget, not because it is wasteful, but because it is a pleasure — and pleasures without budgets grow quietly. Decide a monthly amount that fits the household's real finances, and track gaming spending against it the way you would track eating out or cinema trips. For example, a budget of a small fixed sum each month still buys several sale games a year, and it removes the guilt from every purchase inside the budget.

Tracking is the part most players skip, and it is the part that works. Write down every gaming purchase for one month — games, extras, subscriptions. The total usually surprises its owner, and surprise is a better teacher than any lecture. After that month, the budget stops being a guess and becomes a number you chose with open eyes.

A budget also protects the fun. A player who overspends on games feels guilty while playing them, which spoils the very thing the money bought. A player inside a budget plays freely. The budget is not the enemy of gaming. It is what keeps gaming enjoyable.

## Sell or swap finished games where the platform allows

Finished games still hold value. Where the platform allows it — physical discs, certain stores, trading with friends — selling or swapping a finished game turns a dead purchase back into money for the next one. A game finished and sold for even part of its price cost far less than a game finished and left on a shelf forever.

Check the platform's rules first. Digital games often cannot be resold, and that is a real difference between physical and digital buying worth remembering before your next purchase. Where resale is allowed, keep discs and cases in good condition — a scratched disc with a missing case sells for little. Where it is not allowed, swapping recommendations with friends costs nothing and often leads to the next good game faster than any store page.

## The honest limits of gaming budgets

None of this is an argument against gaming. Gaming is real entertainment, real skill-building, and real friendship for millions of people. A budget that bans gaming will break within a week, and a gamer who feels punished will simply hide the spending instead of stopping it.

The honest goal is smaller: spend on games the way you spend on anything you love — deliberately, within a number you chose, and on the games you actually play. The shelf stops growing. The wallet stops leaking. And the games themselves, played properly instead of collected anxiously, become fun again.

## My Take: Gaming Budgets Reward Finishing Before Buying

The post offers five habits. Finish the backlog before buying anything new. Wait for genuine sales instead of launch-day prices. Try before you buy with free games and free weekends. Give gaming a monthly budget like any other pleasure. Sell or swap finished games where the platform allows. 

My judgement is that the backlog rule is the moral centre. Treat it as a rule about attention, not only money. Guilt then produces the worst purchase of all. Finishing the backlog breaks that cycle. A player who finishes one owned game before buying another learns two things. Whether the next purchase is truly wanted or merely wanted in the moment. That knowledge is worth more than any sale. It prevents the purchase that would become the next backlog entry. Require the list to shrink by one before it grows by one. Keep that rule for three months. 

Sales and try-before-buy are a test of patience versus impulse. It is about recognising that a game's price falls as novelty fades. The game itself does not change. A player who waits pays less for the same experience. The word genuine matters. Not every sale is a real discount. Trying before buying is the evidence protecting the wait. A few hours reveal whether the game suits taste, time and machine. Before money is spent. A player who tries first and waits for a genuine sale buys twice wisely. Once with time, once with money. A player who buys on launch day, unseen, buys twice blindly. Paying the highest price for the least information. Over a year, the difference is not small. It is a shelf of finished favourites versus a pile of expensive guesses.

The budget and sell-or-swap rules complete the system. They need defending against the objection that budgeting kills fun. A monthly budget does not forbid gaming. It gives gaming a fair share of household money, protected from guilt. The amount is decided calmly in advance, not regretted afterwards. A budgeted pleasure is enjoyed more, not less. The player is not spending money belonging to rent, food or savings. Selling or swapping extends the budget, but only where rules genuinely allow it. Digital games often cannot be resold. Pretending otherwise leads to frustration. Where swapping is allowed, a finished game another player will enjoy is better used than stored. Where it is not allowed, the backlog rule matters even more. The purchase is permanent and should be made slowly. Honest limits close the post properly. A budget cannot make an unaffordable hobby affordable. Gaming, like every pleasure, must fit the life paying for it. So finish the backlog first. Try before buying. Wait for genuine sales. Budget monthly. Sell or swap where allowed. The fun was never in the buying. It was in the playing.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How can I cut gaming costs without quitting games?**

Finish the games you already own before buying new ones, wait for genuine sales, try free weekends before paying, and set a monthly gaming budget. These steps cut spending while keeping all the fun — the goal is to play more deliberately, not to play less.

**Is waiting for game sales really worth it?**

Yes. Most big titles drop significantly in price within months of launch during seasonal and holiday sales, while the game itself stays the same. A wishlist with a price you are willing to pay turns sales into tools instead of traps.

**Are free games a good way to save on gaming costs?**

They are, when used properly. Many excellent games cost nothing, and free weekends let you test a paid game fully before buying. Just watch in-game purchases — small extras add up fast, so track their monthly total honestly.

**Should I set a monthly budget for gaming costs?**

Yes. Decide a fixed monthly amount that fits your household finances and track every gaming purchase against it for one month. The total usually surprises players, and a budget you chose yourself removes guilt from every purchase inside it.

**Can I sell finished games to reduce gaming costs?**

Where the platform allows it — physical discs, certain stores, trading with friends — yes, and it turns a finished purchase back into money for the next game. Digital games often cannot be resold, so check the rules before buying, and keep discs and cases in good condition.
