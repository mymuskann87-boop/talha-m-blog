The queue moved slowly, the way queues at cash machines always do. Five people, one machine, and by the time the fifth person walked away, four of them had paid a small fee they never noticed. The machine did not announce it loudly. The fee appeared on the statement days later, wearing the quiet name of a service charge.

An automated teller machine, or ATM, lets you withdraw cash, check your balance, and sometimes deposit money without entering a bank. The machine belongs to a bank, and the bank that owns it may charge people who bank elsewhere. That charge is the ATM fee, and it is one of the easiest fees in personal finance to reduce to zero.

## Why ATM fees exist

When you use your own bank's machine, the bank serves its own customer, and the withdrawal is usually free or covered by your account terms. When you use another bank's machine, that bank is serving a stranger, and it charges for the favour. Your own bank may add its own small charge on top. The result is a fee for pressing buttons on the wrong box.

Some accounts also include a limited number of free withdrawals per month, wherever you withdraw. After that limit, every withdrawal costs. Both rules are printed in the account terms. Almost nobody reads them until the fees have already added up.

## Step one: use your own bank's machines

This single habit removes most ATM fees. Before you travel somewhere new, check where your own bank's machines are in that area. Most banking apps show machine locations on a map. A five-minute walk to your own bank's machine beats a fee at the nearest one every time.

If your own bank has few machines near you, this habit needs a partner: withdraw less often. Two planned withdrawals a month from your own bank's machine cost less than eight hurried ones from whichever machine is closest.

## Step two: withdraw fewer, larger amounts — carefully

Every withdrawal can carry a fee, so fewer withdrawals usually mean fewer fees. For example, four withdrawals at 25 each cost 100 in a month, while one withdrawal of the same total costs 25 or nothing at all. The arithmetic is simple, and it surprises people every time they do it.

But this step carries a safety warning that matters more than the saving: carrying large amounts of cash has its own risk. Do not walk around with money you cannot afford to lose. Balance the saving with safety. Withdraw an amount that covers a reasonable stretch — a week, not a month — and keep the rest in the account where it belongs.

## Step three: pay by card or mobile where it costs nothing

The cheapest withdrawal is the one you never make. Where card and mobile payments are accepted without a fee, use them for everyday spending and keep cash for the places that truly need it — the small shop, the street vendor, the bus fare.

This is not about abandoning cash. It is about spending cash only where cash is needed. Every purchase paid by card is one less reason to visit a machine, and one less chance of a fee.

## Step four: know your free limit and count

If your account allows a certain number of free withdrawals per month, learn that number and count your withdrawals against it. Keep a tiny note on your phone: one mark per withdrawal, and the month's total beside it. When you approach the limit, switch to card payments until the month turns over.

In my view, counting withdrawals is the dullest habit in this post and the one that saves the most money, because a limit you never count is a limit you always cross.

## Step five: plan cash before you travel

Areas with few machines — a village visit, a long road trip, a busy festival — are where ATM fees multiply, because people withdraw in a hurry from whatever machine they find. Plan before you go. Withdraw what you need from your own bank's machine before leaving, and keep a small reserve for the return journey.

Travelling with a plan also means travelling calmer. The person searching for a machine at midnight is not making good money decisions. The person who planned on Friday afternoon is.

## The honest limits of avoiding ATM fees

Some fees cannot be avoided, and pretending otherwise would be dishonest. In some places, only one bank's machine exists, and using it is the only choice. In an emergency, the nearest machine is the right machine, fee or no fee. Safety always beats saving: a well-lit machine in a safe place is worth more than a free machine in a risky one.

Also, some accounts charge for withdrawals everywhere, including their own machines, beyond a limit. In that case, the saving comes from fewer withdrawals and more card payments, not from machine loyalty. Know your own account's rules, because the right habit depends on them.

## Teach the family the same habit

Fees multiply when several people share one account. A spouse with a card, a grown child with a card, an elderly parent who withdraws in a hurry — each person's uncounted withdrawals eat the same free limit, and nobody knows who crossed it. The account does not care whose hand pressed the buttons. It only counts.

Sit together once and agree on three simple rules. First, everyone's routine withdrawals come from your own bank's machines. Second, small everyday buys go on the card, so cash withdrawals stay rare. Third, one shared note counts the month's withdrawals, and anyone who withdraws adds a mark. The note lives on the family phone or on the fridge — wherever everyone will actually see it.

Children learn this faster than adults expect, especially when the rule is explained as a game rather than a lecture: the family tries to reach the end of the month without a single fee. Elderly parents deserve a gentler version — show them the nearest own-bank machine on a calm afternoon, walk there together once, and save its location in their phone. A habit taught kindly lasts longer than a rule delivered as a scolding, and a family that counts together rarely pays fees apart. End each month with a two-minute family review: open the shared note, count the marks, and check whether any fee appeared on the statement. If the month was fee-free, say so out loud — a small victory noticed is a habit repeated. If a fee did appear, find the moment together without blame: the rushed withdrawal, the wrong machine, the forgotten count, or the trip where no own-bank machine existed at all. The review is not an inspection. It is the family reminding itself, gently, that the money stayed where it belonged.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Why does another bank's ATM charge me a fee?**

That bank is serving someone who is not its customer, so it charges for the service, and your own bank may add a small charge too. Using your own bank's machine usually avoids both charges.

**Does withdrawing larger amounts really save money?**

Usually, yes, because each withdrawal can carry a fee. For example, four withdrawals at 25 each cost 100 in fees, while one withdrawal costs 25 or nothing. But balance this with safety — never carry cash you cannot afford to lose.

**How do free withdrawal limits work?**

Some accounts allow a set number of free withdrawals per month, wherever you withdraw. After that limit, each withdrawal costs a fee. Learn your limit and count your withdrawals so you never cross it unknowingly.

**Should I stop using cash completely?**

No. Cash is needed in many places — small shops, street vendors, bus fares. The goal is to use cash where it is needed and card or mobile payments where they are free, so you visit machines less often.

**What should I do about ATM fees when travelling?**

Plan before you go. Withdraw what you need from your own bank's machine before leaving, and keep a small reserve for the return journey. Searching for a machine in a hurry is when fees multiply.
