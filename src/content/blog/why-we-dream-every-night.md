Every night, without asking, your brain puts on a show. You fly, you fall, you meet people you have not seen in years, you lose your teeth, you arrive late for an examination you never studied for, and then you wake up, and most of it is gone within minutes, leaving only a strange feeling and a few fragments. You did not choose the show. You did not write it. You did not even know, while it was happening, that it was not real. Why do we dream? It is one of the oldest questions humans have asked, and one of the least settled in science: after a century of research, there is still no single answer that everyone accepts, and the honest answer begins with admitting that.

We dream, most vividly, during a stage of sleep called REM sleep, named for the rapid eye movements that happen beneath our closed eyelids. REM stands for rapid eye movement, not restless eye movement, and during REM sleep, the brain is highly active, almost as active as when we are awake, while the body is mostly still. Dreams also happen in other stages of sleep, but REM dreams are the longest, the strangest, and the ones we are most likely to remember, if we wake during or just after them. Understanding dreams means understanding REM sleep, the brain's nightly theatre, in which the show is staged.

## The Brain Asleep, But Far From Quiet

Sleep is not a switching off. It is a change of state, in which the brain does different work from the work it does while we are awake. During REM sleep, the brain's activity resembles waking activity in many ways: regions involved in emotion, memory, and visual processing are highly active, while regions involved in logical reasoning and self-monitoring are quieter. That pattern explains much of what dreams feel like: vivid, emotional, visual, and strangely uncritical, as if the brain's editor had gone home, leaving the artists and the archivists to work unsupervised.

During REM sleep, the body is largely paralysed, a condition called atonia, which prevents us from acting out our dreams. Without it, a dream of running would become actual running, and a dream of fighting would become actual fighting, with dangerous results for the dreamer and anyone sharing the bed. The paralysis is a safety mechanism, and it usually lifts the moment we wake, although sometimes it lingers for seconds, producing the frightening experience of sleep paralysis, in which a person is awake and aware but cannot move. The theatre is safe because the audience is strapped into its seat.

REM sleep arrives in cycles through the night. The first REM period is short, perhaps ten minutes, and each successive period grows longer, with the longest, lasting up to an hour, occurring in the early morning, just before waking. In total, an adult spends about one to two hours per night in REM sleep, spread across four or five periods, and dreams in each of them, although we remember only a fraction. If you are woken during REM sleep, you are far more likely to remember a dream, about 80 percent of the time, than if you are woken at another stage, which is why dreams are most vivid in the memory just after an alarm, and fade so quickly afterwards.

## The Protoconsciousness Idea: Dreams As Rehearsal

One influential idea comes from the psychiatrist J. Allan Hobson, who proposed that dreaming is a form of protoconsciousness, a primitive, early form of consciousness that prepares the brain for waking life. In this view, dreaming is the brain's virtual-reality model of the world, a simulation it runs each night, in which it practises the basic business of consciousness: perceiving, moving, feeling, and responding, in a safe, offline environment, before the real world demands it again in the morning.

Hobson's idea, developed with colleagues, is that the brain, during REM sleep, generates a model of the world from within, without input from the senses, and that this model tunes the brain's systems for the waking day ahead. The dream is not a message, a puzzle, or a prophecy. It is a rehearsal, a tuning, a warming up, like an orchestra tuning before a concert, in which each section plays, not yet in harmony, but preparing to play together. The strangeness of dreams, in this view, is not a flaw. It is the nature of a system running without external correction, generating its own world, and learning, each night, how to generate the waking world more effectively.


## Why The Idea Fits The Brain

This idea fits the neuroscience: the brain regions active in dreaming are those involved in perception, emotion, and movement, while the regions that check reality, plan logically, and monitor the self are relatively quiet. The dream feels real because the brain is using the same machinery it uses to perceive reality, and it feels strange because the machinery that normally corrects errors, by comparing perception with the outside world, is offline. The brain is practising being conscious, in the dark, with no audience but itself.

## Other Theories: Emotions, Memories, And Cleaning Up

Hobson's idea is one of several, and the field has not settled on a single answer. Other researchers propose different functions, and the theories are not mutually exclusive: dreaming may serve several purposes at once, and different dreams may serve different purposes on different nights. Among the leading ideas, four recur most often in the research literature, and each captures part of what dreams seem to do.

The first is emotion processing. The psychologist Matthew Walker has proposed that REM sleep, and dreaming, helps process emotional experiences, taking the sting out of painful memories by replaying them in a brain state in which stress chemistry is reduced, so that the memory can be stored without its full emotional charge. A painful event, dreamed and re-dreamed, becomes less painful over time, not because it is forgotten, but because it is filed, with its emotional intensity lowered. Dreams, in this view, are the brain's therapy sessions, conducted nightly, without a therapist.


## Dreams And Memory

The second is memory. The researcher Robert Stickgold has proposed that dreaming helps modify, consolidate, and integrate memories, connecting new experiences with old knowledge, and extracting general lessons from specific events. During sleep, the brain replays experiences, strengthens some connections, weakens others, and builds associations that were not obvious during waking life. Dreams may be the visible surface of that work: fragments of the day, recombined with older memories, in new patterns, as the brain sorts what to keep, what to connect, and what to discard.

The third is creativity. Dreams recombine elements in ways waking thought rarely does, because the logical constraints are loosened, and the result can be new ideas, new connections, and new solutions. Many people report waking with the answer to a problem, the line of a poem, or the shape of an invention, and the history of science and art contains famous examples of insights that arrived in dreams or on waking. Dreaming, in this view, is the brain's brainstorming session, in which unlikely combinations are tried, most discarded, and a few kept, because they work.


## Threat Rehearsal: Practising Danger

The fourth is threat rehearsal, the idea that dreams practise responses to danger, allowing the brain to simulate threatening situations safely, and to prepare reactions that might be needed in waking life. Dreams of being chased, attacked, or falling, which are among the most common dream themes worldwide, fit this idea, although they fit the other theories too, and no single theory explains every dream.

## What Dreams Are Made Of

Whatever their purpose, dreams are built from the materials of waking life, recombined. The people, places, objects, and events of the day appear in dreams, mixed with older memories, fears, hopes, and fragments of media, in combinations that are often impossible in waking life: a childhood home with extra rooms, a familiar person with a stranger's face, a journey that begins in one city and ends in another, without any travel in between. The brain, in dreaming, is not inventing from nothing. It is remixing what it knows, freely, without the waking mind's insistence on consistency.

That remixing is why dreams are so hard to remember, and so hard to describe. Waking memory is organised by logic, narrative, and sequence: this happened, then this, because of this. Dream memory is organised by association, emotion, and image: this felt like that, this reminded me of that, this image followed that image for no reason I can name. Trying to recall a dream in waking words is translating from one language to another, and much is lost in translation, which is why a dream that felt profound in the night can sound trivial in the morning, and why writing dreams down immediately, before the waking mind reorganises them, preserves more of their strangeness.


## Why No Two People Dream Alike

It is also why two people can dream about the same event and produce entirely different dreams. The materials differ, the associations differ, and the brain's nightly recombination differs. Dreams are personal in the deepest sense: they are made of one person's memories, emotions, and experiences, recombined by one person's brain, in one person's sleep. No one else can dream your dreams, and no one else can fully understand them, because no one else has your materials.

## Nightmares, Lucid Dreams, And When Dreams Go Wrong

Not all dreaming is benign. Nightmares, dreams that wake the dreamer in fear, are common, especially in childhood, and usually harmless, but frequent, severe nightmares can disrupt sleep, cause daytime distress, and signal underlying stress, trauma, or illness. Nightmare distress, more than nightmare frequency alone, is what matters: a person who dreams frightening dreams but sleeps well and functions well in the day is different from a person whose nights are dominated by terror that follows them into waking life.

One treatment for recurrent nightmares is imagery rehearsal therapy, in which the dreamer, while awake, rewrites the nightmare with a different, less frightening ending, and rehearses the new version mentally, until the dream itself changes. The technique works for many people, and it illustrates a hopeful truth about dreaming: dreams are not fixed, and the waking mind can influence them, by changing the materials and associations the dreaming brain has to work with. Lucid dreaming, in which the dreamer becomes aware, during the dream, that they are dreaming, and can sometimes influence the dream's course, is another example of waking and dreaming minds meeting, although it is less common, and harder to learn, than popular accounts suggest.

Dreams that are consistently violent, that involve acting out movements during sleep, or that are accompanied by severe sleep disruption, deserve medical attention, because they can signal sleep disorders, neurological conditions, or the effects of medication. Most dreams, however, are normal, healthy, and harmless, even when they are strange, frightening, or sad. A nightmare is not a warning, a prophecy, or a symptom, in most cases. It is a dream, doing whatever dreams do, in a brain that is working as it should.

## The Honest Answer: We Do Not Fully Know

After a century of research, the honest answer to "why do we dream?" is that we do not fully know. The theories described here, protoconsciousness, emotion processing, memory consolidation, creativity, and threat rehearsal, each have evidence, each have critics, and none has won universal acceptance. It is likely that dreaming serves more than one function, that different theories capture different aspects of a complex process, and that the full answer will require more research, better tools, and a willingness to accept that the brain's nightly theatre may have no single purpose, but many, overlapping and interacting.

What we do know is substantial. We know that dreaming is tied to REM sleep, that the brain is highly active during it, that the body is paralysed for safety, that dreams draw on waking experience and older memory, that they are emotional and visual and uncritical, and that they are forgotten quickly unless we wake during them and record them. We know that dreaming is universal, across cultures and across much of the animal kingdom, which suggests it is ancient and important, not a human invention or a quirk. And we know that losing REM sleep, through deprivation or disruption, impairs mood, memory, and learning, which suggests that whatever dreaming does, the brain needs it.

Why do we dream every night? Perhaps to rehearse consciousness, to process emotion, to sort memory, to spark creativity, and to prepare for danger, all at once, in a theatre the brain builds for itself, each night, from the materials of the day. Or perhaps for reasons we have not yet imagined, which future research will reveal. The show goes on, whether we understand it or not, and each morning, we wake from it, remember a fragment, and carry on with the day, a little tuned, a little processed, a little more ready, because our brains spent the night dreaming.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Why do we dream every night?**
There is no single settled answer, but leading theories propose that dreaming tunes the brain for waking life, processes emotions, sorts and consolidates memories, sparks creativity, and rehearses responses to threats. The psychiatrist J. Allan Hobson proposed that dreaming is a form of protoconsciousness, a virtual-reality model the brain runs each night to prepare for waking consciousness. Other researchers emphasise emotion processing, memory integration, and creativity, and dreaming likely serves several of these purposes at once, on different nights.

**What is REM sleep and why does it matter for dreams?**
REM sleep, named for the rapid eye movements beneath closed eyelids, is the stage of sleep in which the most vivid dreams occur. During REM sleep the brain is highly active, almost as active as when awake, while the body is largely paralysed to prevent us acting out our dreams. Adults spend about one to two hours per night in REM sleep, spread across four or five periods that grow longer through the night, and if you are woken during REM sleep you are far more likely to remember a dream, about 80 percent of the time.

**Why do we forget most dreams?**
Dreams are built from associations, emotions, and images, rather than the logic, narrative, and sequence that waking memory uses, so recalling a dream in waking words is like translating between languages, and much is lost. Dreams are most vivid just after waking, especially if you wake during REM sleep, and they fade within minutes as the waking mind reorganises experience. Writing dreams down immediately, before that reorganisation happens, preserves more of their strangeness and detail.

**Are nightmares normal?**
Yes, nightmares are common, especially in childhood, and usually harmless, but frequent, severe nightmares that disrupt sleep and cause daytime distress can signal stress, trauma, or illness and deserve attention. One effective treatment for recurrent nightmares is imagery rehearsal therapy, in which the dreamer rewrites the nightmare with a less frightening ending while awake and rehearses the new version until the dream itself changes. Dreams that involve acting out movements, or severe sleep disruption, should be discussed with a doctor.

**Can dreams predict the future?**
There is no scientific evidence that dreams predict the future, and the theories with the strongest support treat dreaming as rehearsal, processing, and recombination of past experience, not prophecy. Dreams draw on waking experience and older memory, recombined freely, which is why they can feel meaningful and occasionally seem to anticipate events, when a dream's elements coincidentally match something that later happens. A dream that feels like a warning is usually the brain processing a fear or concern it already held while awake.
