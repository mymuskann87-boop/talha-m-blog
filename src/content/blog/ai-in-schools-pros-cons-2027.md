## Personalised Learning at a Student's Own Pace

One of the strongest arguments for AI in schools is personalisation. In a traditional classroom of thirty students, a teacher cannot give every child individual attention at every moment, and students learn at different speeds. AI tutoring systems can adjust difficulty, offer extra practice where a student struggles, and move faster where a student is confident. A student who needs more time with fractions can receive more examples, while a student who has mastered fractions can move on, rather than waiting for the whole class.

This personalisation can also help with feedback. Instead of waiting days for a marked assignment, a student can receive immediate hints, corrections and explanations, which helps them correct misunderstandings while the work is still fresh. For language learning, AI can provide pronunciation practice and conversation simulation that would be impossible to offer to every student individually. For revision, AI can generate quizzes tailored to a student's weak areas, making study time more efficient than re-reading notes.

However, personalisation has limits. An AI system personalises based on data and patterns, not on a deep understanding of a child's life, confidence, or learning style. A student who is struggling because of anxiety, a difficult home situation, or a misunderstanding of instructions may need human encouragement and a different kind of help, not simply more practice questions. Personalisation works best when it supports a teacher's judgement, not when it replaces the teacher's knowledge of the student as a person.

## Teacher Workload, Planning and Classroom Support

Teachers face heavy workloads, including lesson planning, marking, administration and communication with parents. AI can reduce some of that burden by drafting lesson outlines, generating differentiated worksheets, summarising reading material, and helping to create quizzes at different difficulty levels. Used well, this can free teacher time for the work that matters most: teaching, mentoring, and supporting individual students.

AI can also help teachers analyse class performance. By summarising which topics a class found difficult, a system can help a teacher plan the next lesson more effectively, rather than relying only on impression. For new teachers, AI can provide starting points for lesson design and classroom activities, which can build confidence. For experienced teachers, it can save time on routine preparation, allowing more attention to be given to students who need it.

The risk is over-reliance. If lesson plans, feedback and assessments are generated without careful review, errors, inappropriate content or mismatched difficulty can enter the classroom. AI-generated material may also lack local context, cultural relevance, or alignment with the school's curriculum. Teachers therefore need time and training to review AI output critically, and schools need clear policies: AI is a drafting assistant, not an automatic curriculum. The time saved by AI should be reinvested in teaching, not simply used to increase workload elsewhere.

## Cheating, Homework and What Learning Really Means

The most debated issue is cheating. AI can write essays, solve maths problems, and complete homework assignments, which raises a fundamental question: if a student submits AI-generated work, what has the student actually learned? Schools worry, rightly, that easy access to AI answers could undermine practice, critical thinking and writing skills, especially if assessment only measures the final product rather than the learning process.

Responses to this challenge vary. Some schools ban AI tools for certain tasks, others teach students to use AI transparently as a study aid, and many are redesigning assessments to focus on process: drafts, explanations, in-class work, presentations and oral discussion, where understanding can be demonstrated directly. A student who can explain an idea in their own words, defend an argument, or solve a problem without AI has shown learning in a way that a submitted essay alone cannot prove. Assessment reform, rather than detection alone, is likely to be the more durable response.

It is also important to distinguish between cheating and legitimate help. Using AI to brainstorm ideas, check grammar, explain a concept, or practise with quizzes is different from submitting AI-generated work as one's own. Schools that teach that distinction clearly, with examples, help students develop honest habits for a world in which AI tools will be available throughout their working lives. Banning tools entirely may delay that learning, while unrestricted use may undermine it. The balance lies in teaching responsible use, with clear rules for each task.

## Privacy, Data and Children's Safety

Schools handle sensitive data about children, including names, grades, learning needs, behaviour records and, in some cases, health information. AI tools that process student work, voice, or images raise privacy questions that schools must take seriously. Before adopting a tool, a school should ask: what data are collected, where are they stored, how long are they kept, are they used to train models, and can they be deleted? Tools designed for adults may not meet the higher privacy standards appropriate for children.

Parental consent and transparency are also important. Parents should be informed, in plain language, about which AI tools are used, for what purpose, and what safeguards exist. Students, especially older ones, should also understand what they are sharing when they use a tool, and should be taught not to enter personal details, passwords, or sensitive information into AI chatbots. Schools should prefer tools with clear education privacy protections, and should avoid tools that require unnecessary data collection.

Safety extends beyond privacy. AI systems can produce biased, inaccurate or inappropriate content, and children may not recognise those problems. A history summary, a science explanation, or a story generated by AI can contain errors presented confidently. Teaching students to verify AI output, to cross-check important facts, and to report concerning content is therefore part of digital literacy, not an optional extra. Schools need filtering, supervision and age-appropriate tools, especially for younger children.

## Equity, Access and the Digital Divide

AI in schools can either reduce or widen inequality, depending on how it is introduced. On the positive side, AI tutoring, translation and accessibility tools can help students who lack private tutoring, who are learning in a second language, or who have special educational needs. Text-to-speech, speech-to-text, simplified explanations and visual supports can make learning more accessible, and free or school-provided tools can give disadvantaged students resources that were once available only to wealthier families.

On the negative side, access is uneven. Students with reliable internet, modern devices and quiet study space at home can use AI tools far more easily than students without those resources. If homework or revision increasingly assumes AI access, students without it will be disadvantaged through no fault of their own. Schools must therefore ensure that AI-supported learning can also happen in school, with school devices and time, rather than assuming home access. Otherwise, a tool intended to help all students may mainly help those who are already advantaged.

Equity also concerns language and culture. AI systems trained mainly on English-language data from certain countries may perform less well for other languages, dialects or cultural contexts, and may present biased perspectives. Schools serving diverse communities should test tools for their own students, notice whose language and culture are represented, and supplement AI material with local, diverse resources. Technology should expand representation in the classroom, not narrow it to a single dominant perspective.

## Building a Wise School AI Policy

A wise school AI policy starts with purpose: what educational problem is AI solving, and for whom? Schools should adopt tools to support clear learning goals — such as personalised practice, accessibility, or teacher time — rather than adopting technology because it is new. Each tool should be piloted with a small group, evaluated with teachers and students, and reviewed for learning impact, workload, privacy and equity before wider rollout.

Training is essential for both teachers and students. Teachers need practical training in prompting, reviewing AI output, redesigning assessments, and recognising AI limitations. Students need age-appropriate lessons in how AI works, how to use it honestly, how to verify its output, and how to protect their privacy. Parents need clear communication about school policy and about how to support responsible use at home. Without training, even a good tool can be used poorly.

Finally, policy should be reviewed regularly, because AI tools change quickly. A tool's features, privacy terms, pricing and accuracy can shift within a school year, and a policy written once and forgotten will become outdated. Schools should assign responsibility for monitoring AI tools, gathering feedback from teachers, students and parents, and updating guidance. The goal is not to chase every new feature, but to maintain a steady, thoughtful approach in which technology serves learning, teachers remain central, and students develop both knowledge and judgement.

## Special Needs, Language Learners and Different Classrooms

AI tools can be particularly helpful for students with special educational needs and for students learning in a second language, when they are chosen and supervised carefully. Text-to-speech can help a student with reading difficulties access grade-level content, speech-to-text can help a student who struggles with writing to express ideas, and simplified summaries can help a student build understanding before tackling a full text. For language learners, translation support, pronunciation practice and bilingual explanations can reduce the barrier to participating in class, rather than leaving the student silent while others discuss.

These benefits depend on individualisation. A tool that helps one student may distract or confuse another, and a school should involve special needs coordinators, language specialists and the students themselves in choosing tools. A student who uses AI support should also be taught how and when to use it independently, so support builds skills rather than creating dependence. For example, a student might use text-to-speech for a first read, then attempt a passage without it, gradually building reading stamina. Support that is gradually reduced as skills grow is more educational than support that is always on.

Different classrooms also need different approaches. A primary classroom with young children needs stricter supervision, simpler tools, and more emphasis on play, discussion and hands-on learning, because young children learn through interaction and cannot reliably judge AI output. A secondary classroom can introduce more independent AI use, critical evaluation, and subject-specific tools, with explicit teaching about honesty and verification. A vocational or adult classroom may focus on workplace AI skills, such as using AI tools for drafting, data analysis or design, with industry-relevant examples. One policy for all ages and subjects will fit none of them well.

## Parents, Homework and Learning at Home

AI in schools does not stop at the school gate; it affects homework and home learning too. Parents may wonder whether to allow AI help with homework, how to tell whether a child actually understands the work, and what to do if a child uses AI to complete assignments quickly. A helpful approach is to focus on understanding rather than completion: ask the child to explain the answer, to solve a similar problem without AI, or to describe what they learned. If the child can explain, AI was likely used as a learning aid; if they cannot, it was likely used as a shortcut, and the homework needs revisiting.

Schools can support parents by communicating clearly: which tasks allow AI assistance, which do not, and how parents can help without doing the work for the child. Simple guidance — such as "AI may help explain, but the final answer must be the student's own work, in their own words" — is more useful than a vague ban that parents cannot enforce. Parents can also model good habits: using AI to look up explanations together, checking AI answers against textbooks or trusted sources, and discussing when AI output seems wrong or biased. Those conversations teach critical thinking at home as well as at school.

Home access differences must also be considered. If homework increasingly assumes AI access, students without devices or reliable internet at home will be disadvantaged, and parents without digital confidence may feel unable to help. Schools should provide in-school time and devices for AI-supported homework where possible, and should offer alternatives that do not require AI, so learning does not depend on home technology. Equity at home is part of equity at school, and a policy that ignores home circumstances will widen gaps even with the best intentions.

## Assessment Redesign in Practice

Redesigning assessment for an AI world does not mean abandoning written work; it means valuing process as well as product. A teacher might ask students to submit drafts with revisions, to annotate where they used AI help and how they checked it, to explain their reasoning in a short oral presentation, or to complete part of an assignment in class without AI. Those methods do not eliminate AI use, but they make learning visible: a teacher can see how a student thought, where they struggled, and what they understood, rather than only seeing a polished final text that could have come from anywhere.

In-class assessment also has limits and must be used thoughtfully. Timed, handwritten exams can verify independent knowledge, but they can disadvantage students with anxiety, dyslexia, or other needs, and they do not reflect how work is done in real life, where tools and collaboration are normal. A balanced assessment system therefore combines methods: some in-class work to verify understanding, some coursework to allow research and revision, some oral work to test explanation, and some project work to test creativity and collaboration. That balance is fairer and more informative than relying on any single method, with or without AI.

Transparency about AI use in assessed work is a key part of redesign. Schools should provide simple disclosure formats, such as a short note stating what AI tool was used, for what purpose, and how the output was checked and edited. That disclosure teaches honesty, helps teachers judge the student's own contribution, and normalises the idea that AI assistance, when declared and used appropriately, is different from hidden substitution. Over time, students who learn to disclose and reflect on AI use will carry that habit into workplaces, where transparency about tool use is increasingly expected.

## My Take:

My view is that AI in schools is neither a miracle nor a disaster; it is a powerful tool whose value depends entirely on how schools use it. Personalised practice, accessibility support and reduced teacher workload are real benefits, and they should not be dismissed because of fear. At the same time, cheating, privacy risks, bias and unequal access are real harms, and they should not be dismissed because of enthusiasm. A school that holds both truths at once — benefits and risks, together — is more likely to make wise decisions than one that adopts AI uncritically or bans it entirely.

I am particularly concerned about assessment. If schools continue to assess only final products that AI can generate, they will create a system that rewards AI use rather than learning, and then blame students for following the incentives. Redesigning assessment to value process, explanation, discussion and in-class demonstration is harder than buying a detection tool, but it is more honest and more educational. Students should be assessed on what they understand, not on whether they can produce a polished product by any means.

I also think teachers must remain at the centre, not as supervisors of machines, but as the people who know their students. An AI system can tell a teacher that a student got ten questions wrong; only a teacher can tell whether that student is tired, anxious, confused by wording, or missing a foundational concept. Protecting teacher time, training and professional judgement is therefore not a sentimental point; it is a practical requirement for AI to work well in schools. Technology that supports teachers will help students. Technology that sidelines teachers will not, however advanced it appears.

Finally, I believe students deserve to be taught about AI, not merely protected from it. They will live and work in a world where AI tools are common, and they need skills to use them honestly, critically and creatively. A student who learns to prompt well, verify output, recognise bias, and decide when not to use AI has gained a life skill, not just a school skill. Schools that teach those habits, alongside subject knowledge, are preparing students for reality, rather than for an imagined world in which AI does not exist.

I would also warn against judging AI in schools only by exam results in the first year. New tools take time to integrate, teachers need training, and students need time to learn responsible use, so early results may reflect implementation rather than the tool itself. A fair evaluation looks over a longer period, at multiple measures: understanding, engagement, teacher workload, equity, and student attitudes, not only test scores. A tool that raises scores but increases cheating, widens gaps, or reduces teacher morale is not a success, however impressive the headline figure.

Another point concerns student voice. Students are the people most affected by school AI policy, yet they are often the last to be consulted. Schools should ask students what helps them learn, what confuses them about AI rules, and what worries them about privacy or fairness. That consultation often reveals practical issues adults miss: unclear rules for different subjects, pressure to use AI to keep up with peers, or anxiety about being falsely accused of cheating by detection tools. Listening to students does not mean letting them set policy alone, but it does mean policy will be clearer, fairer and more likely to be followed.

Finally, I believe the goal of AI in schools should be to produce graduates who can think with AI, not merely through AI. Thinking with AI means using it to explore ideas, test arguments, create drafts and learn faster, while retaining the ability to think independently, to question output, and to create original work. That ability will matter in every career these students enter, because AI tools will be present in workplaces, healthcare, law, design and public life. Schools that teach that balanced skill — confident use plus independent judgement — are preparing students for the world as it is, not for a world in which AI is either banned or blindly trusted.

Another practical point is teacher wellbeing. AI is often presented as reducing workload, but if it is introduced without training, without time to review output, and without reducing other duties, it can increase workload and stress instead. Schools should measure workload honestly after introducing AI tools, listen to teachers, and adjust expectations accordingly. A tool that saves marking time but creates new administrative tasks, or that requires extensive checking for errors, has not truly saved time. Protecting teacher time and morale is essential, because exhausted teachers cannot provide the human support that makes AI useful in the first place.

I also think schools should be careful with AI detection tools. These tools claim to identify AI-generated text, but they can produce false positives, especially for students writing in a second language or using assistive tools, and they can create unfair accusations that are difficult to disprove. Detection results should never be the sole basis for a disciplinary decision; they should prompt a conversation, a review of drafts, or an oral check of understanding, rather than an automatic penalty. Fairness in assessment requires human judgement, and a detection score, however confident it appears, is not proof of cheating.

A further consideration is the role of play and curiosity. Young children learn through exploration, questions, mistakes and interaction, not through optimised, personalised drills alone. AI tools that turn learning into constant testing, scoring and ranking may reduce curiosity, especially if they replace discussion, storytelling, art and outdoor learning. Schools should protect time for unplugged, creative and social learning, using AI as one tool among many, not as the default for every lesson. A classroom that balances technology with human interaction will nurture both skills and joy in learning.

Finally, I believe schools should prepare students not only to use AI, but to shape its future. Today's students will become tomorrow's developers, policymakers, teachers and citizens who decide how AI is used in society. Teaching them to ask ethical questions — who benefits, who is harmed, whose data are used, whose perspective is missing — is as important as teaching them to prompt effectively. A generation that can use AI skilfully and question it critically will build fairer, wiser systems than one that merely consumes them. That, ultimately, is the deepest purpose of AI education in schools.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What are the main benefits of AI in schools?**
Personalised practice, immediate feedback, accessibility support, and reduced teacher workload for routine planning and marking are commonly cited benefits.

**What are the main risks of AI in schools?**
Cheating, over-reliance, privacy concerns, bias, inaccurate output, and unequal access for students without reliable devices or internet are the main risks.

**Should students be allowed to use AI for homework?**
It depends on the task and school policy; using AI to explain concepts or practise is different from submitting AI-generated work as one's own, and schools should make that distinction clear.

**How can schools protect student privacy with AI tools?**
Schools should check what data a tool collects, how long it is kept, whether it is used for training, and whether it meets education privacy standards, and should inform parents in plain language.

**Will AI replace teachers?**
No, AI cannot replace teacher judgement, knowledge of individual students, empathy, classroom management, or value-based decisions; it works best as a support tool for teachers.

**Does AI widen inequality in schools?**
It can, if it assumes home internet and devices that some students lack, or if it performs poorly for certain languages and cultures, so schools should provide in-school access and test tools for their own students.
