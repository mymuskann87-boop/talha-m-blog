Last October, a small boy knocked on my door wearing nothing more than an old white bedsheet with two holes cut for eyes. He carried a plastic bucket in one hand and wore the biggest grin I had ever seen. The whole street stopped to look at him, and people laughed and cheered in the best possible way. Ten minutes later, another child walked past in a costume that must have cost a small fortune, all shiny plastic and bright lights, and nobody said a word. That night taught me something I still remember: a costume does not need a big price tag to win hearts.

Most of us have everything we need for a great costume already sitting at home. It hides in wardrobes, in old boxes, and in the back of drawers. The trick is to look at ordinary things with fresh eyes. A black bin bag can become a witch's cape. A cardboard box can turn into a robot. An old shirt with a few patches can make a scarecrow that looks like it walked straight out of a field. Children love this kind of magic even more than adults do, because they get to help build it.

## The night a bedsheet won

Let me start with the simplest costume of all, because it never fails. Take an old white sheet, fold it in half, and ask an adult to cut two eye holes. Add a few grey smudges with an old pencil or some light face paint, and you have a ghost. It sounds too easy, but that is exactly why it works. The ghost looks soft, a little silly, and very friendly, and people smile the moment they see it.

If you want to dress it up a little, tie a ribbon around the waist or add a small hat on top. A paper chain around the neck makes a ghost look like it came from a very old house. Keep the sheet short enough that nobody trips over it, and make the eye holes wide enough to see clearly. A costume you can walk in beats a fancy one you keep tripping over.

## Cardboard can do magic

Cardboard is the secret weapon of cheap costumes. A big box, some paint, and a pair of safe scissors can build almost anything. Cut a hole for the head and two for the arms, paint it silver, and you have a robot. Add bottle caps as buttons and a kitchen colander as a helmet, and suddenly the robot looks like it came from a real workshop.

I think a costume made at home carries something a shop costume never can: a story. When a child says, "I painted this myself," people listen. They ask questions. They laugh at the crooked button or the wobbly antenna. That little story is what makes a costume memorable, not the price on the label.

A cardboard box can also become a television, a car, or a house with windows. Paint it, glue on paper shapes, and let the child decide the details. The more the child helps, the prouder they feel wearing it.

## Raid the wardrobe first

Before you buy anything, open every wardrobe in the house. An old suit jacket and a pair of sunglasses can make a detective. A long skirt and a scarf can make a fortune teller. A striped shirt and a paper eye patch can make a pirate. Grown-ups often forget what they own, and children love digging through clothes like treasure hunters.

Ask grandparents too. Old hats, shawls, and coats often sit unused for years, and they can turn an ordinary costume into something special. A long coat with the collar turned up looks mysterious on anyone. A wide-brimmed hat changes a whole face. Borrow with permission, of course, and return everything in good shape.

If something is missing, check a charity shop before a costume shop. A second-hand cape or hat costs very little, and you can use it again next year. Some families even swap costumes with neighbours, so every child gets something new without anyone spending much.

## Small touches, big difference

The cheapest costumes look expensive when the small details are right. Face paint does more work than almost anything else. A few whiskers turn a child into a cat. A green nose and pointy ears turn them into a witch's helper. A dab of white powder and dark circles under the eyes turn them into a sleepy vampire.

Props help too, but they should be light and safe. A cardboard wand, a paper lantern, or a small bucket all add to the look. Avoid anything sharp, heavy, or hard to carry. The best prop is one a child can hold all evening without getting tired of it.

One last tip: test the whole costume the night before. Let the child walk around the house in it, sit down in it, and climb stairs in it. If something falls off or feels tight, fix it while there is still time. A comfortable child is a happy child, and a happy child is the best costume of all.

So this Halloween, skip the expensive shop aisle. Grab a sheet, a box, and a little paint, and build something together. The photos will look wonderful, the children will feel proud, and your wallet will stay happy.
