A warm home does not require a high thermostat setting. It requires heat to stay inside, to reach the rooms you use, and to be produced efficiently. Most heating waste comes from three sources: heat escaping through gaps, heat warming empty rooms, and heat blocked by furniture or curtains. Fixing those three sources costs far less than raising the temperature, and it often improves comfort more than a higher setting. The United States Department of Energy guidance cited in heating guides says turning a thermostat back by 7 to 10 degrees Fahrenheit for eight hours a day can save as much as 10 percent a year on heating and cooling, while other guides note that turning a thermostat down by one degree can cut heating use by roughly 10 percent in some homes. Exact savings vary by home, climate, insulation and heating system, so treat those figures as examples of scale, not as promises for your bill. All costs below are examples only and vary by region and supplier.

## Stop Heat Escaping: Draught-Proofing First

Draught-proofing is the cheapest high-impact step in most homes. Warm air escapes through gaps around doors, windows, letterboxes, keyholes, pipe entries and loft hatches, while cold air enters through the same gaps. Self-adhesive foam or rubber seals for windows, brush strips for doors, and a letterbox brush can be fitted in an afternoon for a low cost; one guide puts draught-proofing at around £5 to £30 per door or window when fitted yourself. Start by finding draughts on a windy day: hold a hand near door and window edges, look for curtains moving when windows are closed, or use a lit incense stick carefully away from curtains to see air movement, following fire safety care. Seal the worst gaps first, usually the front door, back door, and windows in the rooms you use most. Do not seal a room completely if it needs ventilation for a gas appliance, cooker, or damp control; ventilation is a safety and health need, not waste. A chimney that is not used can also leak large amounts of warm air; a chimney balloon or draught stopper, costing around £20 to £40 in one guide, can block it, but it must be removed before lighting a fire. Draught-proofing does not make a home airtight; it makes it less leaky, so the heating system warms the air once, rather than warming a constant stream of cold air entering through gaps.

## Use Curtains, Doors and Sunlight as Free Insulation

Curtains are insulation you already own, if you use them at the right times. Open curtains on sunny windows during the day to let free solar heat in, especially on south-facing windows in the northern hemisphere, and close thick or lined curtains at dusk to add a layer between the room and cold glass. That simple timing rule costs nothing and works every day. Keep curtains from hanging over radiators, because a curtain over a radiator traps warm air behind it and sends heat toward the window instead of into the room. Tuck curtains behind radiators or shorten them where possible in winter. Internal doors also matter: close doors to rooms you are heating, and keep doors to unused rooms closed so heat does not drift into empty spaces. If you have a hallway that is always cold, closing the living room door keeps that room warmer with the same heating. Rugs on cold floors add comfort underfoot and reduce the feeling of cold, even if they do not insulate the whole house. In a home with hard floors, a rug in the main living area, plus warm socks or slippers, can make a lower thermostat setting feel comfortable. These habits are small, but they change the experience of warmth: a room that holds its heat feels warmer than a room that is constantly reheated, even at the same thermostat number.

## Set the Thermostat for Comfort, Not for Speed

A common myth is that turning a thermostat up high heats a home faster. It does not; a heating system heats at roughly the same rate, and a high setting only overshoots the target temperature and wastes energy. Set the thermostat to a steady, comfortable level, often suggested between 18°C and 21°C for main living rooms in British Gas guidance, and use a timer so heating runs when you need it, not all day. A programmable or smart thermostat can lower the temperature automatically while you are away or asleep, then warm the home before you wake or return, which removes the need to remember manual changes. If you use a smart thermostat, check that its schedule matches your real life, not an idealised schedule. A home that is empty from 9 a.m. to 5 p.m. does not need full heating all day, but a home with a baby, an older person, or someone working from home may need a steadier temperature for health and comfort. Health guidance matters here: very low indoor temperatures can be risky for older people, young children, and people with certain health conditions, so do not cut heating to unsafe levels to save money. The goal is a steady, comfortable temperature in the rooms you use, not the lowest possible number. If you feel cold at 19°C, check draughts, clothing and radiator blockages before raising the setting to 23°C.

## Radiators, Boilers and Heating System Care

Radiators waste heat when they are blocked, airlocked, or heating the wall behind them. Keep furniture, curtains and drying clothes away from radiators so warm air can circulate into the room. Bleed a radiator if the top is cold while the bottom is warm, because trapped air prevents full heating; use a radiator key, a cloth, and a container, and check system pressure afterwards if your system requires it. A reflective panel behind a radiator on an external wall can reflect some heat back into the room instead of letting it warm the wall, and British Gas suggests a foil-covered card as a simple version. For boilers, annual servicing by a qualified professional keeps the system efficient and safe, and it can prevent breakdowns in cold weather. One efficiency step mentioned in heating guides is lowering a combi boiler's flow temperature to around 60°C or a little lower, as suggested by the Money Saving Boiler Challenge, which can help a condensing boiler run more efficiently while still warming the home, though the right setting depends on your system and radiators, so check manufacturer guidance or ask a professional if unsure. Do not close too many vents or radiators in a forced-air system, because that can disturb airflow and reduce efficiency; heating guides often advise keeping at least most vents open. Small maintenance steps, done once before winter, prevent the midwinter breakdown that costs far more than the service.

## Heat the Person and the Room You Use, Not the Whole House

Heating a whole house to the same temperature is expensive and often unnecessary. Use radiator valves or zone controls to heat the rooms you use, and lower heating in spare bedrooms, hallways and storage rooms, while keeping them above a level that prevents damp or frozen pipes. In cold weather, do not turn heating off completely in an empty home if pipes could freeze; a low background setting protects pipes and prevents damp. Heat the person as well as the room: wear a warm layer indoors, use a blanket on the sofa, and keep feet warm, before reaching for the thermostat. A hot drink, a warm meal, and physical movement also raise comfort without raising the bill. In the evening, gather in one warm room rather than heating three rooms for three people, and use a door draft excluder to keep that room's heat inside. If you use a portable electric heater, remember that all plug-in electric heaters convert electricity to heat at a similar efficiency, so a fan heater, oil-filled radiator, panel heater or convector does not offer a cheaper unit of heat; the saving comes from heating one room for a short time instead of the whole house, not from the heater type. Use a portable heater safely, away from curtains and bedding, with a timer, and never leave it unattended. A heated blanket or hot water bottle can also warm a person directly for far less energy than warming a whole room.

## Insulation, Windows and Longer-Term Upgrades

Draught-proofing and habits help immediately, but insulation decides long-term bills. Loft insulation, cavity wall insulation where suitable, and floor insulation reduce heat loss through the building itself, though costs, suitability and payback vary widely by home and should be checked with a qualified survey or energy audit. Window film or secondary glazing can add a layer of air in winter for single-glazed windows, at a lower cost than replacing windows, while double or triple glazing is a larger investment that also improves comfort and noise. Pipe lagging, insulating hot water pipes with foam tubes, keeps water hotter for longer, prevents pipes freezing, and makes heating and hot water more efficient. Before spending on a large upgrade, get an energy audit or a professional assessment, check for grants or support schemes in your region, and prioritise the measure that addresses your home's largest heat loss. A home with a cold loft needs loft insulation before new windows; a home with single glazing and severe window draughts may benefit from window work first. Do not buy an upgrade because it is advertised as efficient; buy it because it fixes a specific loss in your home. Keep records of any upgrade, including installer details and guarantees, because that information helps future maintenance and resale.

## Daily Habits and Mistakes That Quietly Raise the Bill

Several daily habits raise heating bills without improving comfort. Leaving heating on all day in an empty home, heating unused rooms to full temperature, blocking radiators with furniture, drying clothes on radiators, and constantly turning the thermostat up and down all waste energy. Drying clothes on radiators blocks heat and adds moisture, which can lead to damp; use a drying rack away from the radiator, or a tumble dryer only when needed, with ventilation. Cooking can add warmth, especially baking, but do not use an oven as a heater; it is inefficient and can be unsafe. Keep vents and radiators clear, check that thermostatic radiator valves are not covered by curtains, and avoid placing a thermostat near a heat source, in direct sunlight, or in a draughty hallway, because a badly placed thermostat misreads the home's temperature. At night, lower the temperature for sleeping if you are comfortable, but use warm bedding rather than heating the bedroom to daytime levels. In the morning, heat the rooms you will use first, not the whole house at once. Review your bill monthly, not only when it arrives as a shock: compare usage, not only cost, because prices change, and look for a sudden rise that may signal a draught, a fault, or a changed habit. Small, consistent habits, repeated daily, save more than a single large purchase made once.

## Damp, Mould and Ventilation: Warmth Must Stay Healthy

A warm home that is damp or mouldy is not a healthy home, and heating decisions affect damp directly. Warm air holds moisture from cooking, bathing, breathing and drying clothes; when that air meets a cold wall or window, condensation forms, and persistent condensation can lead to mould. Draught-proofing helps, but sealing a home completely without ventilation can worsen damp. Keep trickle vents open where fitted, use an extractor fan when cooking or bathing, open a window briefly after a shower, and avoid drying clothes on radiators, which adds large amounts of moisture to a room. If mould appears, clean it following health guidance, find the moisture source, and check for leaks, cold bridges, or poor ventilation, rather than only painting over it. In rented homes, report persistent damp or mould to the landlord in writing, keep photographs and dates, and check local housing guidance for your rights. For owners, a damp survey can distinguish condensation from a leak or rising damp, which need different fixes. A heating plan that keeps rooms at a steady, moderate temperature, with controlled ventilation, protects both the building and the people inside it, while a plan that alternates between overheating and cold, damp rooms damages both.

## Renters Versus Owners: What You Can Change

Renters and owners face different heating choices, and advice that assumes ownership can frustrate renters. Renters can usually draught-proof with removable seals, use curtains, rugs, door excluders, and radiator care, set thermostat timers, and report faults, without altering the building. Removable window film, temporary secondary glazing, and chimney balloons in unused fireplaces are also options where the tenancy allows them, but check the agreement before fitting anything permanent. Owners can consider loft, wall and floor insulation, window replacement, boiler upgrades, and zone controls, with professional advice and grant checks. Both groups should check the heating tariff, meter readings, and whether a smart meter or time-of-use tariff would help or hurt, given their actual usage pattern. A time-of-use tariff that is cheap at night helps only if heating or hot water can be shifted to night hours; otherwise it can raise costs. Before any large spend, get at least two quotes, check installer credentials and guarantees, and prioritise the fix that addresses your largest measured loss, not the upgrade with the most advertising. A renter who draught-proofs well and uses curtains wisely can often save as much, proportionally, as an owner who insulates, because both are stopping the same waste at different scales.

## My Take:

My view is that most people try to solve a cold home by buying more heat, when they should first stop losing the heat they already pay for. A draughty door, a curtain over a radiator, and an empty room heated to full temperature will defeat even a new boiler, because the system is working against leaks and waste. I would therefore start every winter with a draught check, a radiator check, and a thermostat check, before spending money on anything else. Those three checks cost almost nothing, take an afternoon, and often change the feeling of a home more than a higher thermostat setting. A home that holds its heat feels warmer, calmer and more comfortable, even at a lower number, because the temperature is steady rather than constantly rising and falling. That steadiness is what comfort actually is; a high number on a thermostat is not comfort if the room cools again within an hour.

I also think clothing and room choice are underrated heating strategies. Wearing a warm layer indoors is not a sign of poverty; it is a sensible response to winter. A sweater, warm socks, and a blanket on the sofa can make a room at 19°C feel comfortable, while a person in summer clothes will feel cold at 22°C and demand more heat. Similarly, gathering in one warm room in the evening is not a hardship; it is how families naturally live in winter, sharing warmth, conversation and light. I would heat the room I am in, close its door, and enjoy that warmth, rather than heating three empty rooms and sitting alone in one of them. That choice saves money, but it also creates a better evening, because people are together. Heating policy and family life are not separate; a warm, shared room is both cheaper and warmer in human terms.

My view on thermostats is that they should be set once, sensibly, and then left alone. Constantly turning a thermostat up and down does not make a home warmer faster; it creates overshoot, discomfort, and waste. I would set a main room temperature that is comfortable for the people who live there, use a timer for away and sleep times, and adjust clothing or draughts before adjusting the number. I would also be careful about health: a home that is too cold is not a saving, it is a risk, especially for older people, babies, and people with health conditions. Saving money should never mean living in a cold, damp home. If a household is struggling to afford heating, the answer is not to turn heating off, but to seek support, check tariffs, improve draught-proofing, and use community resources where available. A warm home is a health need, not a luxury, and any advice that ignores that fact is incomplete.

I am sceptical of portable heaters marketed as cheap heating miracles. All electric heaters produce heat at a similar efficiency, so a fan heater is not cheaper per unit of heat than an oil-filled radiator. The saving, if there is one, comes from heating one room for a short time instead of the whole house, not from the heater itself. I would use a portable heater only for a specific, short-term need, with a timer and safety care, and I would not buy one expecting it to cut a whole-house bill. Similarly, I would not buy a smart thermostat expecting it to save money on its own. A smart thermostat saves money only if its schedule matches real life; a schedule that heats an empty home all day, smartly, is still waste. Technology helps when it automates a good decision you have already made. It does not replace that decision.

I also believe insulation is the honest long-term answer, even though it is less exciting than a new heater or a gadget. Loft insulation, wall insulation where suitable, window improvements and pipe lagging reduce the need for heat itself, rather than producing heat more cheaply. That distinction matters: a well-insulated home needs less heating every winter, for decades, while a new heater still needs fuel every day. I would prioritise insulation after draught-proofing, get a professional assessment before a large spend, and check for grants or support schemes. I would not buy insulation as a fashion item; I would buy it to fix a specific loss, such as a cold loft or single-glazed windows. Over time, that approach pays back in comfort, lower bills, and a home that is easier to keep warm in a cold snap, when heating matters most.

Finally, I would treat a heating bill as information, not as a shock. Compare usage month by month, look for sudden rises, and ask what changed: a new draught, a blocked radiator, a changed schedule, or a fault. A bill that rises suddenly often has a cause that can be fixed, and a bill that falls after draught-proofing is proof that the work mattered. I would also keep a simple winter checklist on the fridge: seal doors, close curtains at dusk, keep radiators clear, bleed cold radiators, service the boiler, and set the timer. That checklist, repeated each autumn, prevents the midwinter crisis where a home is cold, a boiler fails, and a bill arrives at the same time. Warmth, in my view, is a system: a building that holds heat, a heating system that runs efficiently, habits that protect warmth, and people who are warmly dressed and together in one room. Build that system, and a high bill becomes unnecessary, not because you endured cold, but because you stopped wasting heat.

I would also challenge the idea that a warm home must look or feel a certain way. Thick curtains, a rug, a blanket, and a closed door are not signs of hardship; they are traditional, sensible winter habits that worked long before smart thermostats. Modern heating advice sometimes implies that comfort requires constant, even temperature in every room, but most people are comfortable in a warm living room, a cooler bedroom for sleep, and a hallway that is simply not freezing. I would accept that variation, dress for it, and spend money on the rooms that matter most. That acceptance is not resignation; it is a realistic standard that keeps bills manageable without making a home feel like a test of endurance. A home that is warm where you live, sleep, and gather is a warm home, even if a spare room is cooler.
