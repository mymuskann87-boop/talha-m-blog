Some crimes end with an arrest. Some end with a chase, a trial, a prison door closing. And then there is this one — a crime that ends with a man stepping out of the back of a flying passenger jet, at night, in the rain, over a dark forest, holding a bag of money. He was never seen again. Not that night, not the next week, not in more than fifty years since.

It is the only hijacking in American history that was never solved. The FBI chased it for 45 years. This is the full, true story of the man the world calls D. B. Cooper — a name that, as you will see, was itself a mistake.

## A Quiet Man With A Briefcase

On the afternoon of 24 November 1971 — the day before Thanksgiving — a man walked up to the Northwest Orient Airlines counter at the airport in Portland, Oregon. There was nothing special about him. He looked to be in his mid-40s, neat and ordinary, dressed in a business suit, a white shirt and a black tie, carrying a plain attaché case. He paid cash for a one-way ticket to Seattle, Washington, a short flight of about half an hour. The name he gave was Dan Cooper.

His flight, Flight 305, took off with 36 passengers and six crew members aboard. The man sat quietly, and while the plane waited and then climbed, he ordered a drink — bourbon and soda — like any tired businessman going home.

Shortly after 3:00 p.m., he handed a note to a flight attendant. Attendants received silly notes from passengers all the time, and she nearly slipped it into her pocket unread. But the man insisted she look at it. The note said he had a bomb. He told her to sit beside him, and he opened his case just a crack — enough for her to glimpse wires and red sticks inside. Then he calmly dictated his demands, and she wrote them down and carried them to the captain.

The demands were these: $200,000 in cash — all of it in twenty-dollar bills — and four parachutes, waiting for him when the plane landed in Seattle.

## The Exchange At Seattle

The plane circled while people on the ground hurried to gather the money and the parachutes. Ten thousand twenty-dollar bills had to be collected, and their serial numbers were recorded, so that every single note could be recognised later if it ever appeared. The airline's president agreed to pay. In those days, the thinking was simple: give the hijacker what he asks, keep everyone alive, and catch him later.

When Flight 305 landed at Seattle, the trade was made. The money and the four parachutes came aboard. In return, all 36 passengers walked free, along with one of the flight attendants. Cooper kept a small crew with him: the captain, the co-pilot, a flight engineer and one flight attendant.

Then he gave his new orders. Fly to Mexico City, with a fuel stop on the way in Reno, Nevada. And fly the way he said: low and slow — below 10,000 feet, at a gentle speed, with the landing gear down and the wing flaps lowered. Those instructions showed he understood this particular aeroplane, a Boeing 727, very well. He also wanted the cabin left unpressurised. There was a reason for that, and it had to do with a staircase.

The Boeing 727 had something most planes do not: a set of stairs built into its tail, which could be lowered from the back of the plane. Cooper knew about those stairs. Soon, the only flight attendant left in the cabin with him was sent forward to join the others in the cockpit, with the curtain closed between them and him. At the back of that plane, alone, sat a man with $200,000 and four parachutes.

Why four parachutes? Investigators later believed the number was a clever shield. If the authorities thought he might push a crew member out with him, they would never dare give him a faulty parachute. Every chute had to be real, and safe.

## The Jump Into The Storm

A little after 8:00 p.m., somewhere on the road from Seattle toward Reno, over the forests of the Pacific Northwest, the crew felt the plane move — a small bump, a shift, as if the tail had suddenly become lighter. A warning light in the cockpit told them the rear stairs had been opened.

Outside, the night was about as bad as a night can be. Rain was falling. Cloud swallowed everything. Below lay miles and miles of thick, dark forest — cold, wet, and almost empty of people. Into that night, nearly two miles above the ground, the man stepped out with the money tied to his body.

When the plane landed safely at Reno and the crew walked to the back, the cabin was empty. The stairs were still partly down. The man was gone. So was the money bag, and two of the four parachutes — one of them, it turned out, was an old training parachute that had been sewn shut and could never have opened. He had used cord from one of the spare parachutes to tie the money bag closed.

He had left a few things behind: cigarette ends, fingerprints, and his cheap black clip-on tie, which he had taken off before the jump. Years later, that tie would give investigators a DNA sample. It would never give them a name.

## NORJAK: The Hunt That Found Nothing

The FBI opened its investigation while the plane was still in the air. They gave it a code name: NORJAK, short for Northwest Hijacking. It grew into one of the biggest manhunts in the Bureau's history.

Agents interviewed hundreds of people and followed leads across the whole country. Soldiers and volunteers searched the forests where he was believed to have come down. By the fifth anniversary of the hijacking, the FBI had considered more than 800 suspects — and crossed out all but about two dozen of them. Over the whole life of the case, the number of names checked passed a thousand.

One name kept tempting them. Less than five months after Cooper's jump, another man, Richard Floyd McCoy, hijacked a plane in a very similar way and escaped by parachute. He was caught. Many people were sure the two men were one. But the flight attendants who had sat beside Cooper gave descriptions that matched each other closely — and McCoy did not match them. He was ruled out.

The fingerprints lifted from the plane matched nobody in the records. The artist's sketches — a calm, thin-faced man in a suit — became famous, but a sketch is not a name. Year after year, the file grew thicker and the answer stayed the same: nothing.

## The Money That Came Back From The River

For almost nine years, the only things anyone had were questions. Then, in February 1980, an eight-year-old boy named Brian Ingram was digging in the sand on Tena Bar, a beach on the Columbia River near Vancouver, Washington, when he uncovered something strange: bundles of rotting twenty-dollar bills, still held together, half buried in the sand.

The money was checked, bill by bill, against the recorded list. It was Cooper's money: $5,800 of the ransom, eaten by water and time.

That find raised more questions than it answered. How had the money travelled to that riverbank? When had it got there? And where was the rest — more than $194,000 that has never been found, and never, as far as anyone can prove, been spent? Not one more bill from that ransom has ever been confirmed to show up anywhere in the world.

The $5,800 is, to this day, the only piece of the ransom ever recovered — the single hard clue that part of Cooper's story ended somewhere near that river.

## Did He Survive The Jump?

Here is where honesty matters, because nobody knows. There is no body, no parachute found in the trees, no confession, no grave. There are only arguments, and they point both ways.

The FBI's own thinking leaned toward a dark answer. The parachute he jumped with could not be steered. He went out in city clothes and ordinary shoes — about the worst outfit imaginable for landing in wild forest on a freezing, rainy night. He jumped into deep woods in total darkness. Even a trained parachutist, the Bureau noted, would have found that jump hard to survive, and the evidence suggests Cooper was no expert. In that view, the money found by the river fits the story: the bag fell, or was carried by water, while its owner never made it out of the forest.

Others argue the other side with just as much force. A man who planned every detail so calmly — the exact bills, the four parachutes, the low and slow flight, the tail stairs — may have known exactly what he was doing. Perhaps he landed, walked out, and lived quietly for the rest of his life, never daring to spend money whose numbers were written down in an FBI file.

Both stories are possible. Neither has ever been proven. That is the heart of this case: it is not a puzzle missing one piece — it is missing almost all of them.

## The Name That Was A Mistake, And The Case That Went Quiet

Even the famous name is wrong. The hijacker called himself Dan Cooper. In the confusion of the early reporting, a different man with the initials "D. B." was questioned by police, and through a media mix-up, those initials attached themselves to the hijacker in print. "D. B. Cooper" was born — a name invented by error, belonging to a man who may never have existed, for a man whose real name nobody knows.

The FBI kept the case officially alive for 45 years — one of the longest investigations in its history. Then, in July 2016, the Bureau announced it was moving its people and resources to other work. The files would be kept. If strong new physical evidence ever appeared, the case could breathe again. But as things stand, the hunt is over, and the hijacker won.

It remains what the FBI itself calls one of the great unsolved mysteries in its history, and the only skyjacking in American commercial aviation that was never solved. Somewhere out there — in a forest, along a river, or in a quiet life lived under another name — the last page of this story exists. Nobody has ever found it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Was the hijacker's real name D. B. Cooper?**
No. He bought his ticket under the name Dan Cooper, which was almost certainly false. The "D. B." part came from a press mix-up early in the story, when reporters confused him with another man who had been questioned. The wrong name stuck, and the world has used it ever since.

**How much money did he take, and was any of it found?**
He received $200,000, all in twenty-dollar bills whose serial numbers had been recorded. The only part ever found was $5,800 in rotting bills, discovered by a boy on a Columbia River beach in 1980. The rest has never been recovered, and no other ransom bill has ever been confirmed to appear anywhere.

**Did Cooper survive the parachute jump?**
Nobody knows. The FBI long suspected he probably did not: his parachute could not be steered, he wore ordinary clothes and shoes, and he jumped at night into rough forest in rain. But no body was ever found, so survival cannot be ruled out either. His fate is unproven in both directions.

**Why did he ask for four parachutes?**
Investigators believe it was a protection trick. If the crew might be forced to jump with him, the authorities would have to supply four genuine, working parachutes — they could not risk giving him a fake one. In the end he jumped alone, taking two parachutes, one of which was a sewn-shut training chute.

**Is the D. B. Cooper case still open?**
Not actively. In July 2016, after 45 years, the FBI redirected the case's resources to other investigations. The files are preserved, and the Bureau has said it would look again if real new evidence appears — but no agent is hunting Cooper today.
