June had decorated the village hall every Christmas for nine years, and every year she did it alone. It was not that no one offered to help. People offered. June smiled, thanked them, and arrived at eight in the morning with her boxes of paper chains, her ladder, and her list, written in pencil, in the same notebook she had used since the first year. Decorating was her job, in the way that some jobs choose you, and she liked doing it alone, in the quiet, while the village was still asleep and the hall smelled of cold wood and dust.

This year was different, and June knew it the moment she unlocked the door on the morning of Christmas Eve. The hall was colder than usual. The string of lights along the stage, the ones that had worked for five years without a single complaint, was dead. And in the corner by the radiator, curled into the smallest possible shape, was a thin ginger cat, watching her with the steady, hopeful look of a creature that has decided, quite firmly, to be adopted.

June looked at the cat. The cat looked at June. Outside, the first snow of December was beginning to fall, soft and slow, over the lane and the green and the roofs of the village.

"Oh, no," June said, out loud, to no one in particular. "Not this year. Not a cat, and not broken lights, and not Christmas Eve."

The cat blinked, slowly, and did not move.

## The Carpenter With The Wrong Ladder

June was standing on a chair, holding the dead string of lights and trying to decide whether to cry or to laugh, when the hall door opened and a man came in carrying a ladder that was too long for the doorway. He turned it sideways, apologised to the door frame, and set it down with a sigh.

"Sorry," he said. "Mrs. Alden said the roof was leaking over the stage, and I said I would come and look before the dance tonight. I didn't know anyone was here."

June climbed down from the chair. She was thirty-four, wore a red jumper with a small hole at the cuff, and had a pencil behind her ear, which she only remembered when the man smiled at it.

"I'm June," she said. "I do the decorations. The roof can wait until after Christmas, can't it? The dance is tonight."

"I'm Theo," the man said. "I'm the carpenter. And the roof cannot wait, because the leak is directly above the stage, and if it rains, or if the snow melts, the star will get wet, and then the star will fall, and then there will be no dance, only a very damp hall and a great deal of explaining."

June looked up at the star. It was a large paper star, gold, a little bent at one point, which she had made herself nine years ago. It hung above the stage, slightly crooked, exactly as it had hung every year.

"How long have you lived here?" June asked.

"Eleven days," Theo said. "I moved into the cottage at the end of the lane. The one with the blue door. Mrs. Alden said the village needed a carpenter, and I needed somewhere quiet. So far, the village has been very quiet, and the cottage has been very cold, and I have been very glad of both."

The cat, in the corner, stretched, stood up, and walked across the hall to Theo, as if it had been waiting for him all along. It wound around his legs and sat down on his boot.

"That cat," June said, "has been here five minutes, and it already likes you better than me, and I have lived here for nine years."

"Maybe," Theo said, bending down to scratch the cat behind its ear, "the cat knows a good roof when it sees one."

## The Lights That Would Not Work

Theo looked at the roof. June looked at the lights. The lights were the bigger problem, because a dance without lights is only a room full of people standing in the dark, and June had promised the village a dance with lights. She had promised it to Mrs. Alden, to the choir, and to herself, in pencil, in her notebook, on the first page, under the words: LIGHTS. TREE. STAR. MUSIC.

"The plug is loose," Theo said, from the top of his ladder, without being asked. He had finished with the roof and was looking down at the string of lights as if it were a small, patient animal. "May I?"

June handed him the lights. He turned the plug over in his hands, tightened something with a small screwdriver from his pocket, and plugged it in. The lights came on, one by one, warm and gold, all the way along the stage, and the hall, which had been grey and cold, suddenly looked like Christmas.

June stared at them. "Nine years," she said. "Nine years I have fought with those lights, and you fix them in thirty seconds with a screwdriver you keep in your pocket."

"I keep a great many things in my pockets," Theo said, climbing down. "Screws. Pencils. Biscuits, for emergencies. Would you like a biscuit? It is nearly an emergency. It is Christmas Eve, and we have a hall to finish."

June laughed, properly laughed, for the first time that day. The cat, from its place on Theo's boot, looked extremely pleased with itself.

## The Tree That Leaned Left

The tree was the next problem. It was a tall fir, cut that morning, and it leaned to the left in a way that suggested it had opinions of its own. June had tied it to the wall with string for nine years, and every year it had leaned a little more, as if it were slowly giving up.

Theo looked at the tree. He looked at the string. He looked at June. "We can do better than string," he said, and disappeared out of the door, and came back with a wooden stand he had made in his workshop, from offcuts, in the time it had taken June to untangle the paper chains.

They put the tree in the stand together. It stood straight. It stood so straight that June stepped back and stared at it, and then she laughed again, and then, to her great surprise, she felt her eyes fill with tears, because a tree standing straight should not make anyone cry, and yet there it was.

"Are you all right?" Theo asked, quietly.

"I'm fine," June said, wiping her eyes with the cuff of her red jumper. "It's only that I have been doing this alone for nine years, and I forgot what it was like to do it with someone. I forgot that it could be easier. And kinder. And more fun."

Theo did not say anything wise. He handed her a paper chain, and together they hung it across the hall, and the cat followed them from chair to chair, supervising, and the snow kept falling outside, soft and slow, over the lane and the green.

## The Star That Needed Saving

At noon, the snow turned to sleet, and the leak over the stage, which Theo had patched, began to drip again, one slow drop at a time, directly onto the gold star. June climbed the ladder herself this time, because the star was hers, and she would not let anyone else touch it, and Theo held the ladder steady below, and told her, calmly, exactly where to put her feet.

June reached the star. It was wet at one point, and the paper was soft, and she held it gently, the way you hold something that has lasted nine years and might not last a tenth, and she thought, quite suddenly, of all the Christmases she had spent in this hall, alone, hanging the same star, and how she had told herself, every year, that alone was fine, that alone was peaceful, that alone was what she wanted.

It was not what she wanted. It was only what she had grown used to.

"Pass it down," Theo said, from below. "I can fix it. I have glue. I have gold paint. I have a great many things in my pockets."

June passed the star down. Theo took it as carefully as if it were made of glass. He sat at the table by the stage, with the cat on his knee, and repaired the star while June made tea in the small kitchen, and the hall filled with the smell of tea and glue and cold air and Christmas, all at once.

## Tea In The Small Kitchen

They drank the tea sitting on the edge of the stage, with their feet dangling, like children, and the lights warm above them, and the tree straight behind them, and the cat asleep between them, purring in its sleep. Theo told June about the cottage with the blue door, and the workshop, and the eleven days of quiet, and how he had almost not come to the village at all, because he had been afraid that quiet would turn into lonely.

June told Theo about the nine years of decorating alone, and the notebook, and the pencil, and the list, and how she had made the star the first year, when she was twenty-five and newly arrived in the village herself, and how she had hung it crooked on purpose, because a perfect star had seemed too much to hope for.

"I like it crooked," Theo said.

"I like it fixed," June said, and they both laughed, and the cat woke up, and looked at them with great disapproval, and went back to sleep.

Outside, the sleet stopped. The snow began again, thicker now, and the lane went white, and the village went quiet, and the hall, warm and lit and full of paper chains, felt like the safest place in the world.

## The Snow That Kept Falling

By mid-afternoon, the snow was falling so thickly that the lane outside had vanished under white, and the green looked like a Christmas card, and the roofs of the village were soft at the edges, as if the whole world had been wrapped in cotton wool. Inside the hall, June and Theo hung the last of the paper chains, and the cat, tired of supervising, fell asleep on a pile of coats by the door, twitching in its dreams.

June stood at the window for a moment, watching the snow, and Theo stood beside her, and neither of them said anything, because the hall was finished, and the lights were warm, and the tree was straight, and there was nothing left to do but wait for the village to arrive. It was the quietest moment of the day, and the happiest, and June thought, with a small shock of surprise, that she had not felt lonely all day, not once, not even for a second, and that nine years of quiet mornings had not prepared her for how good it felt to share one.

"Do you think it will stop?" Theo asked, looking at the snow.

"I hope not," June said. "Snow on Christmas Eve is the one thing I cannot make with paper and glue. I have always wanted it, and this year, it came, and so did you, and so did the cat, and I think, on the whole, this is my favourite Christmas Eve of all nine."

## The Dance That Almost Did Not Happen

At six in the evening, the village began to arrive. Mrs. Alden arrived first, with mince pies. The choir arrived next, with song sheets. Children arrived in coats too big for them, and old men arrived in suits they had worn for forty years, and the hall filled with voices and laughter and the smell of mince pies and hot chocolate.

June stood by the door, in her red jumper, with her notebook in her hand, checking her list. Lights. Tree. Star. Music. All done. All perfect, or as perfect as a village hall dance can be, which is to say, slightly crooked, slightly noisy, and entirely wonderful.

Theo stood beside her, in his carpenter's jacket, with the cat, which had refused to leave, sitting on his boot as if it owned the hall. "You did this," Theo said. "Nine years of it. The village is lucky to have you."

"We did this," June said. "This year, anyway. And the star is straight this year, and I don't know how I feel about that."

"Feel good about it," Theo said. "A straight star is allowed, once in a while. Even a crooked one needs a rest."

The music started. It was an old record player, scratchy and warm, playing the songs the village had danced to for as long as anyone could remember. Couples took to the floor. Children spun in circles. Mrs. Alden danced with the postman. And June stood by the door, watching, and felt, for the first time in nine years, not like the woman who decorated the hall, but like a guest at her own party.

## A Dance, A Star, A Beginning

Theo held out his hand. "May I have this dance?" he asked, formally, as if they were at a ball, and not in a village hall with a cat on his boot and a paper star above the stage.

June looked at his hand. She looked at the dance floor. She looked at the star, straight and gold above the stage, and she thought about nine years of doing everything alone, and eleven days of a new neighbour, and one Christmas Eve that had fixed the lights, the tree, the roof and the star, and possibly, quietly, something else as well.

"Yes," she said, and took his hand, and they danced, slowly, in the middle of the hall, while the snow fell outside and the music played and the village watched, and smiled, and pretended not to watch at all.

They danced one dance. Then another. Then June's feet hurt, and Theo's jacket smelled of glue and tea, and the cat, tired of being ignored, climbed onto June's chair and went to sleep on her coat, and Mrs. Alden, passing by with a plate of mince pies, whispered, loud enough for the whole hall to hear, "About time."

June laughed. Theo laughed. The record player crackled. The star shone above the stage, straight and gold, and June thought that perhaps a star did not need to be crooked to be hers, and perhaps a hall did not need to be decorated alone to be hers either, and perhaps, just perhaps, next Christmas would be different in the best possible way.

## The Walk Home In The Snow

At midnight, the dance ended, the lights were switched off, the hall was locked, and June and Theo walked home through the snow, side by side, carrying the empty boxes, the ladder, and the cat, which Theo carried in his jacket, like a small, warm secret.

At the end of the lane, by the cottage with the blue door, Theo stopped. "June," he said, "I have lived here eleven days, and I have fixed a roof, a string of lights, a tree and a star. I have also, I think, found a cat, and a village, and a dance. May I come back tomorrow, to help you take the decorations down?"

June looked at him, at the snow on his shoulders, at the cat asleep in his jacket, at the blue door behind him, and she smiled, the way people smile when a wish they did not know they had made comes true on Christmas Eve.

"Yes," she said. "Come back tomorrow. And the day after that. And bring biscuits. It is nearly an emergency. It is Christmas, and we have a whole year to plan for."

Theo laughed, and June laughed, and they stood in the snow for a moment longer, not saying anything, because some moments do not need words, only snow, and lights in windows, and a cat asleep in a jacket, and the feeling, warm and certain, that something has begun.

## Next Christmas, Together

The next morning, Christmas Day, June woke to snow, and silence, and a message on her phone. It was from Theo. It said: The cat has a name. It is Star. I hope that is all right. Merry Christmas, June.

June laughed, and cried a little, and wrote back: Star is perfect. Merry Christmas, Theo. See you tomorrow, for the taking down. Bring biscuits.

And in the hall, locked and dark and quiet, the gold star hung straight above the stage, waiting for next year, when June would not decorate alone, and Theo would bring his ladder and his screwdriver and his biscuits, and Star the cat would supervise from the radiator, and the village would dance, and the snow would fall, soft and slow, over the lane and the green, as it always had, and as, June hoped, it always would.
