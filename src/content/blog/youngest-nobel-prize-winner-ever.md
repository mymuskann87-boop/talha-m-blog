The school morning starts gently in Mingora. The Swat Valley is green and bright, the river runs cold from the mountains, and a schoolgirl pulls her bag onto her shoulder and thinks about ordinary things: homework, friends, the lesson she liked most yesterday. School is not just a building to her. It is woven into her family, because her father runs a school in the city, and classrooms have been part of her life for as long as she can remember.

Her name is Malala Yousafzai, and on mornings like this one, nobody watching her could have guessed where her love of lessons would lead.

## A childhood built around a school

Malala was born on 12 July 1997 in Mingora, the largest city in the Swat Valley in northern Pakistan. She was named after Malalai, an Afghan heroine remembered for her courage long ago, and she grew up as the daughter of Ziauddin Yousafzai, a teacher and education advocate who established and ran the Khushal Girls High School and College, the school she attended.

Learning was simply the air in that house. Malala later wrote that, as a small child, she would toddle into her father's classes before she could even talk properly, and act as if she were the teacher. She had two younger brothers, a sharp mind, and a growing belief that a classroom was a place she had every right to be.

## The years the valley fell quiet

When Malala was ten years old, life in Swat began to change. In 2007 the Taliban took control of the valley, and strict rules followed. Girls were banned from attending school. Dancing and watching television were forbidden. Violence became part of daily life, and the group made its opposition to girls' education a central part of its campaign. By the end of 2008, the Taliban had destroyed some 400 schools.

For most children, this was frightening. For a girl whose whole world was her father's school, it was also deeply personal. A door she walked through every day was being pushed shut in front of her.

## Eleven years old, and already speaking

Malala refused to go quietly. In September 2008, at the age of eleven, she stood up at a press club in Peshawar, where her father had taken her to protest the school closings, and gave her first speech, asking how the Taliban dared to take away her basic right to education. The speech was reported across Pakistan.

Early in 2009, the BBC's Urdu service was looking for a student who could describe life under Taliban rule. Malala began writing an anonymous diary for BBC Urdu under the pen name Gul Makai. She was eleven years old. Her first entry, headed "I am afraid," described her fear of a full-blown war in her beautiful valley and her nightmares about being too frightened to go to school. In her entries she wrote about being forced to stay at home, and she questioned why the Taliban should decide her future.

By February 2009 she had also appeared on national television, speaking calmly about the closed schools, and a *New York Times* documentary about her life introduced her to viewers far beyond Pakistan. Then, in May 2009, fighting reached Swat, and Malala's family had to leave their home and seek safety far away. When they were able to return weeks later, she did not lower her voice. She kept giving interviews and campaigned openly beside her father. In 2011 she was nominated for the International Children's Peace Prize, and that same year she received Pakistan's first National Youth Peace Prize.

## The dark day, and the long road back

On 9 October 2012, Malala was fifteen years old and riding the bus home from school, chatting with her friends about schoolwork, when a Taliban gunman stopped the bus and shot her.

She survived. That same day she was airlifted to a military hospital in Peshawar, and four days later she was flown to an intensive care unit in Birmingham, England. The attack drew protests and support from around the world, and a global petition in her name helped lead to Pakistan's first Right to Education bill. The weeks that followed were full of treatment and slow recovery. She needed several operations, yet she had suffered no major brain damage, and the world watched a schoolgirl begin to heal.

In March 2013, after weeks of therapy, she walked back into a classroom, this time in Birmingham. For a girl who had been told that girls must not learn, simply sitting down at a school desk again was its own quiet victory.

## A sixteenth birthday spent addressing the world

On 12 July 2013, her sixteenth birthday, Malala stood before the United Nations in New York and spoke to the world. The day was named Malala Day in her honour. She told her audience: "The extremists are afraid of books and pens. The power of education frightens them."

Later that year she published her autobiography, *I Am Malala*, and the European Parliament awarded her the Sakharov Prize for Freedom of Thought. She also received the United Nations Human Rights Prize in 2013. In her book she had written a line that surprised many people: "I don't want revenge on the Taliban. I want education for sons and daughters of the Taliban." A teenager from a mountain valley was now one of the most recognised voices on Earth, and she was using that voice for one simple idea: every child, in every country, deserves to go to school.

## Oslo, at seventeen

On 10 October 2014, the Norwegian Nobel Committee announced that the Nobel Peace Prize would be awarded jointly to Malala and to Kailash Satyarthi, a children's rights activist from India who had spent his life fighting child labour. The committee praised Malala for showing, under the most dangerous circumstances, that young people too can improve their own situation. Many also noticed the pairing itself: a 60-year-old Hindu from India and a 17-year-old Muslim from Pakistan, honoured together for the same cause, like a small bridge built between two nations, two faiths and two generations.

Malala was seventeen. She became the youngest Nobel Prize laureate in history, younger than any winner in any category since the prizes began. Accepting the award, she said: "This award is not just for me. It is for those forgotten children who want education. It is for those frightened children who want peace. It is for those voiceless children who want change." In December 2014 she received the prize at the award ceremony in Oslo City Hall, still only seventeen years old.

I keep returning to one detail in all of this: she did not wait for the world to become safe before she found her courage. The courage came first, while the danger was still real, and that is what makes her story so different from a simple tale of success.

## The work continues

Malala did not treat the medal as a finish line. With her father, she co-founded the Malala Fund, which supports girls' education through local partners and campaigns around the world for quality secondary schooling for all girls. In 2014 she travelled to Jordan to meet Syrian refugees, to Kenya to meet young students, and to northern Nigeria, where she spoke up for the schoolgirls kidnapped by Boko Haram, a group that, like the Taliban, tries to stop girls from learning. In 2015 she helped open a girls' school in Lebanon for refugees from the Syrian war.

She also kept faith with her own education. She finished school in England and graduated from the University of Oxford in 2020. Today the Malala Fund backs education projects in six countries, and Malala remains one of the world's most persistent voices for the millions of girls who are still out of school. Running through all of her work is one steady message: a child's right to education is a foundation for peace, and teaching children is one of the surest ways to defeat extremism before it takes hold.

Some doors are closed with locks and threats. Others are opened by a girl carrying books, walking through them anyway, and holding them open behind her for everyone else. That, more than the medal, is what the world celebrated in Oslo.

## My Take: Malala's Prize Honours Courage, Not Only Youth

Malala Yousafzai was born on 12 July 1997 in Mingora, the largest city in the Swat Valley in northern Pakistan. In 2007, the Taliban took control of the valley, girls were banned from attending school, dancing and watching television were forbidden, and violence became part of daily life. Malala refused to go quietly. On 9 October 2012, aged fifteen, riding the bus home from school, she was shot by a Taliban gunman, and she survived, after treatment in Peshawar and abroad.

In my view, the prize, when it came, honoured a course of action, not a single moment, and that distinction is what makes Malala's youth meaningful rather than merely remarkable. On 12 July 2013, her sixteenth birthday, she stood before the United Nations in New York, on a day named Malala Day in her honour, and told her audience that the extremists are afraid of books and pens, and that the power of education frightens them. On 10 October 2014, the Norwegian Nobel Committee announced that the Nobel Peace Prize would be awarded jointly to Malala and to Kailash Satyarthi, a children's rights activist from India who had spent his life fighting child labour. 

The work after the prize, in my view, is the test that separates a symbol from a campaigner, and Malala passed it by treating the medal as a beginning. She did not treat the medal as a finish line. With her father, she co-founded the Malala Fund, which supports girls' education through local partners and campaigns around the world for quality secondary schooling for all girls. The Fund's work, through local partners, is in my view the practical answer to the question every prize raises: what happens after the applause? Youth made the prize historic. The work after it made the prize useful.

What should the reader do differently? First, do not remember Malala only as the youngest winner, aged seventeen. Remember the sequence: a speech at eleven, a shooting at fifteen, a United Nations speech at sixteen, and a prize at seventeen, each step taken while the danger was real. Second, judge the prize by the work it supports, not by the record it sets. The Malala Fund's support for girls' education, through local partners, is the measure that matters, and the reader who follows that work will understand the prize better than the reader who only remembers the age. Third, take the sentence from her United Nations speech seriously, as an argument, not as a slogan. In my view, if education frightens extremists, then defending education is not a gentle cause. It is a direct challenge to those who fear it, and Malala's courage was to make that challenge, as a child, in public, and to keep making it after the world had noticed. That is what the prize honours, and that is what the reader should not forget.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How old was Malala when she won the Nobel Peace Prize?**
She was 17 years old. The Nobel Peace Prize was announced on 10 October 2014, when she was seventeen, and she received it in Oslo that December, making her the youngest Nobel laureate in history. The committee praised her for showing, under the most dangerous circumstances, that young people too can improve their own situation. Many also noticed the pairing itself: a 60-year-old Hindu from India, Kailash Satyarthi, and a 17-year-old Muslim from Pakistan, honoured together for the same cause, like a small bridge built between two nations, two faiths and two generations.

**Who shared the 2014 Nobel Peace Prize with her?**
Kailash Satyarthi, an Indian activist honoured for his long struggle against child labour and for children's rights. The Nobel Committee described the two as partners in the same cause: the right of every child to education.

**Why did Malala write under the name Gul Makai?**
Gul Makai was the pen name she used for her anonymous BBC Urdu diary in 2009. Writing under another name let an eleven-year-old describe life under Taliban rule while keeping her identity hidden for safety.

**What is the Malala Fund?**
It is the organisation Malala co-founded with her father. It funds education projects with local partners, currently in six countries, and presses governments to give every girl a quality secondary education.

**Did Malala finish her own studies?**
Yes. She went back to school in Birmingham in March 2013, a few months after she was attacked on 9 October 2012, when a Taliban gunman stopped her school bus and shot her. For a girl who had been told that girls must not learn, simply sitting down at a school desk again was its own quiet victory, after weeks of treatment and slow recovery that included several operations. She later graduated from the University of Oxford in 2020, completing the education she had been shot for defending, and she went on co-founding the Malala Fund with her father to press governments to give every girl a quality secondary education.
