Dear cousin,

You asked me about the flat, and since you are too polite to ask the real question, I will ask it for you: how much rent is too much? Everyone you ask will give you a different number, and most of them will be guessing. So let us work it out properly, the way I wish someone had worked it out with me before my first lease.

First, a plain fact to hold on to. Renting means paying to live in a place owned by someone else, usually every month. You do not own the walls. You are buying the right to live inside them, month by month, and that is a perfectly normal way to live. But because rent is usually the biggest single bill in a household, getting it wrong hurts more than any other bill. A rent that is slightly too high does not hurt once. It hurts twelve times a year, quietly, in the money that is never left for anything else.

## The third-of-income guide, and why it bends

Money advisors commonly suggest keeping rent near a third of income where possible. Say that slowly, because the words around it matter as much as the number. It is a guide, not a law. It is a way to test a flat, not a verdict on your life. If the rent is near a third of what you bring home, the rest of life — food, transport, savings, a little fun — still has room to breathe. If the rent is half your income, something else in life gets squeezed every single month, and the squeeze usually lands on savings first, which is the worst place for it to land.

But guides bend, and this one bends in expensive cities. In a city where every flat costs more, a third may be impossible, and that does not make you a failure. It makes you a person in an expensive city, and the question changes from "is this a third?" to "is this the best I can do without breaking everything else?" Compare three or four real flats, not dream flats. The cheapest flat that is safe, near your work or study, and livable is usually the right answer, even if it is smaller than the one in the photographs you liked.

## Share the biggest bill in the house

The fastest way to cut rent is also the oldest: do not pay it alone. A trusted roommate splits the biggest bill in the household straight down the middle, and often splits the internet, the electricity and the food basics too. Two people in a two-room place each pay far less than one person in a one-room place, and the place is usually better — a real kitchen, a real sitting area, a home instead of a box.

Sharing needs care in choosing. Live with someone whose hours, habits and money honesty you trust, and write down the split before moving in: who pays what, on which date, and what happens if one person leaves. A written note between friends is not unfriendly. It is what keeps friends friendly. The horror stories about shared flats almost always start with "we never talked about money." Talk about it first, in daylight, before the keys change hands.

## The cheaper area, and the maths of the commute

A flat one stop further out, or a twenty-minute bus ride further from the centre, can cost noticeably less, and this is where the maths earns its keep. Take the monthly rent saving, then subtract the extra transport cost for the month — the pass, the fuel, the fares. If the saving is still clearly bigger, the cheaper area wins. If the transport eats the whole saving, it does not, and you should stay central.

There is a second cost to count, and it is not money: time. A commute that grows by an hour a day costs ten hours a week, and ten tired hours are worth something. So weigh both. A slightly longer commute for a much lower rent is a good trade. A much longer commute for a tiny saving is a bad one. Do the maths for your own case, with your own numbers, because nobody else's commute is yours.

## Asking about the rent, and reading the real price

At renewal time, something worth knowing: landlords sometimes prefer a good tenant at the same rent over an empty place. Finding a new tenant costs them time, effort and empty months, and a tenant who pays on time and looks after the place is worth keeping. So a polite question costs nothing: "Would you consider keeping the rent the same for another year?" Ask it calmly, in writing, before the renewal date, and accept the answer gracefully either way. Sometimes the answer is yes. Sometimes it is no. The question itself is free, and the worst outcome is the same rent you already pay.

In my view, a good tenant is worth more than a small increase, because an empty flat earns nothing at all while the landlord searches, and a reliable tenant is the quietest income a landlord has. That is not a trick to use against anyone. It is simply the other side of the table, seen clearly, and seeing both sides makes a fair conversation possible.

Before signing anything, read the real price, not just the headline rent. Ask what is included: water, building maintenance, parking, any service charges. A flat that looks cheap and then adds charges for everything is not cheap. A flat that looks slightly dearer but includes water and maintenance may be the better deal. Read the whole page, not just the big number, and ask about anything unclear before the signature, not after.

## The honest limit: moving costs money too

Moving is not free, and a saving guide that forgets this is lying by omission. There is the transport of your things, the deposits, the days off work, the small army of costs that arrive with boxes. So moving only pays when the monthly saving is big enough to cover the moving cost within a few months. For example, if moving costs the equivalent of two months' saving, it pays for itself quickly. If it costs a year's saving, stay where you are and negotiate instead. And never move in a rush to a place you have not visited in daylight, checked for water and light, and met the neighbours of. A cheap flat with no water is not cheap. It is a daily problem with a low rent.

One last thing, cousin. The goal is not the cheapest roof in the city. The goal is a home that leaves you money for the rest of your life — food, learning, family, and a little saving for the future. A rent that lets you live, not just survive, is the right rent, whatever the number turns out to be.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How much of my income should go to rent?**

Advisors commonly suggest around a third where possible, but this is a guide, not a law. In expensive cities it may not be achievable, and then the right question is whether the rent still leaves room for food, transport and some saving.

**Is sharing a flat really that much cheaper?**

Yes, because rent is usually the biggest bill, and a roommate splits it directly. Shared bills for internet and utilities add further savings. The key is choosing a trustworthy roommate and agreeing the money split in writing before moving in.

**Can I really negotiate rent with a landlord?**

You can always ask politely, especially at renewal time. Landlords sometimes prefer keeping a reliable tenant at the same rent over the cost and empty months of finding a new one. There is no promise, but the question costs nothing.

**What hidden costs should I check before signing?**

Ask whether water, maintenance, parking and service charges are included. Also check the deposit terms and who pays for repairs. The real monthly price is the rent plus everything compulsory that comes with it.

**When is moving for cheaper rent a bad idea?**

When the moving costs take a year or more of the monthly saving to repay, or when the cheaper place costs far more in transport and time. Do both maths — money and hours — before deciding.
