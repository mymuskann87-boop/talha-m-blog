The monsoon diary of one house begins the same way every year: with a small brown stain on the ceiling that nobody noticed in May. By July, the stain has grown a darker edge. By August, a steady drip lands in a bucket in the corner of the room, and the family is paying for a repair that would have cost a fraction in the dry season — plus a ruined mattress, a damp wall, and a week of inconvenience. The rainy season does not send one big bill. It sends a hundred small ones, and they add up to more than most families expect. This post follows one house through one season, and the five lessons that kept its costs down.

## Check roofs, drains and seals before the rains

The first lesson is also the cheapest, and it must happen before the first heavy rain: check roofs, drains, and window seals. Walk around the house in the dry weeks and look for what water will find — a cracked tile, a blocked drain, a window seal that has shrunk away from its frame, a gap under a door. Water is patient and thorough. It will find every opening you missed.

One leak repair early beats a flooded room later, and the arithmetic is not close. Sealing a gap costs a little time and a small tube of sealant. Repairing a flooded room costs plaster, paint, possibly furniture, and days of disruption. Clean the drains so water flows away from the house instead of pooling against its walls. Check the roof from the ground for slipped or broken tiles, and get them fixed while a repairman can work dry and safely.

In my view, the cheapest roof repair is the one done in sunshine, because rain charges extra for everything — including the repairman's time.

Keep a short pre-rain checklist on paper: roof, drains, windows, doors, outdoor wiring covers. Go through it once a year, before the season turns, and the house meets the rain ready instead of surprised.

## Protect shoes, bags and electronics

The second lesson is about the rainy season's hidden bill: rain damage. Shoes soaked daily rot and split. Bags without covers let water into books, papers, and phones. Electronics caught in a downpour can fail completely — and a failed phone is one of the most expensive rainy-season costs there is.

Covers are cheap. A rain cover for a bag, a waterproof pouch for a phone, a pair of simple rain shoes for wet days — these cost very little and protect things worth far more. Keep a plastic bag inside every school bag and work bag during the season, so protection is always at hand when a sudden shower starts. Dry shoes properly after wet days: stuff them with newspaper, keep them in moving air, and never dry them on direct heat, which cracks leather and warps soles.

For the home, the same rule applies. Move electronics, books, and important papers away from windows and walls that leak. A little distance from a damp wall saves a lot of replacement cost. And keep chargers and power strips off the floor during heavy rain, especially in rooms where water has entered before.

## Keep a small stock of essentials

The third lesson is about storm days. When heavy rain shuts the roads, the family that has nothing in the house pays emergency prices — or pays a neighbour, a delivery rider, or a shop that stayed open at a premium. A small stock of essentials, bought at normal prices before the season, removes that whole category of spending.

Stock the basics: rice, lentils, flour, oil, salt, tea, soap, candles or a charged torch, basic medicines the family already uses, and drinking water. Nothing exotic, nothing in bulk beyond what the family will use. Rotate the stock — use the oldest first, replace as you go — so nothing expires. The stock is not hoarding. It is a buffer, the same idea as an emergency fund, but in the kitchen cupboard.

The same thinking applies to transport. On a storm day, a planned delay costs nothing, while a forced emergency trip costs whatever the moment demands. If the journey can wait a day, let it wait. The rain will pass. The bill for rushing through it may not.

## Dry clothes with air flow

The fourth lesson lives in the laundry corner. In the rainy season, clothes dried without air flow develop damp smells, and damp-smelling clothes get washed again — double water, double soap, double effort, for the same shirt. Worse, clothes left damp for days can grow mould spots that ruin them completely.

Dry clothes indoors with air flow: near an open window, under a fan, spread out rather than bunched together. Do not pile wet clothes in a corner or a closed room. If the house has a covered balcony or a ventilated space, use it. Wash smaller loads more often, so each load dries faster, instead of one giant load that stays wet for days.

A simple habit saves the most: wash in the morning, so clothes have the whole day to dry, rather than at night, when they sit wet and still until morning. And keep one set of rain clothes — a raincoat, waterproof shoes — separate from the everyday set, so the everyday set never has to go out in a storm and come back soaked.

## Plan travel with rain in mind

The fifth lesson is travel. Plan travel with rain in mind, because a delayed trip is cheaper than a ruined one. Check the forecast before leaving. Carry a cover for bags and electronics. Leave earlier when rain is expected, so there is no need to rush through flooded roads or pay extra for the fastest ride home.

For regular commuters, the rainy season rewards one small investment: a good raincoat and waterproof bag covers, bought once, used all season. Compare that with the cost of replacing a soaked phone, a ruined pair of shoes, or a bag of spoiled books, and the raincoat is the cheapest thing in the story.

There are honest limits. Some rain damage cannot be prevented. A truly exceptional storm will overwhelm good preparation, and no checklist stops every leak. This post reduces the cost, it never promises to eliminate it. But the difference between a house that prepared and a house that did not is visible in every bill of the season: one pays for sealant and covers, the other pays for repairs and replacements. Preparation is not free, but it is always cheaper than repair.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What should I check in my house before the rainy season?**

Roofs, drains, window seals, and gaps under doors. Water finds every opening, so fix cracked tiles, clear blocked drains, and seal shrunken window seals in the dry weeks. One small repair in sunshine costs far less than a flooded room later, plus ruined furniture and days of disruption.

**How do I protect my phone and shoes from rain damage?**

Use cheap covers: a rain cover for bags, a waterproof pouch for phones, and rain shoes for wet days. Keep a plastic bag inside every school and work bag during the season. Dry wet shoes with newspaper stuffing in moving air, never on direct heat, which cracks leather and warps soles.

**Should I stock food before the rainy season?**

Yes, a small stock of basics bought at normal prices: rice, lentils, flour, oil, salt, tea, soap, a charged torch, and the family's regular medicines. Rotate the stock, oldest first, so nothing expires. On storm days, this buffer removes emergency-price shopping entirely.

**Why do clothes smell damp in the rainy season, and how do I stop it?**

Clothes dried without air flow stay damp, develop smells, and get washed again — double water and soap for the same shirt. Dry indoors near an open window or under a fan, spread out rather than bunched. Wash in the morning so clothes have the whole day to dry, and keep a separate rain set so everyday clothes never go out in storms.

**Is it worth delaying travel during heavy rain?**

Yes. A delayed trip costs nothing, while rushing through flooded roads risks a ruined phone, spoiled books, or extra transport charges. Check the forecast, leave earlier when rain is expected, and invest once in a good raincoat and waterproof bag covers for the whole season.
