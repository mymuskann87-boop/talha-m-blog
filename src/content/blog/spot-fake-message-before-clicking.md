Let us do something different. Instead of talking about fake messages in general, let us open one together and take it apart, line by line. Messages like this land on thousands of phones every day:

> **"Dear Customer, your bank account will be BLOCKED today due to incomplete verification. Verify now to avoid closure: [link]"**

Four lines. Four cracks. Watch:

1. **"Dear Customer"** — your bank knows your name. This sender does not.
2. **"will be BLOCKED today"** — fear, on purpose. A scared person does not check details; a scared person taps.
3. **"incomplete verification"** — it sounds official but says nothing. Blocked for what, exactly? Real problems come with real details.
4. **The link** — this is the whole point of the message. Everything before it is just pressure pushing your thumb toward it.

That is phishing in one small example. Below is the full picture: what these messages are, every warning sign, the newer traps like QR codes and WhatsApp codes, the sixty-second check that defeats almost all of them, and what to do if you already tapped.

## What a Phishing Message Really Is

Phishing is a trick message — an email, a text, or a chat message — that pretends to come from a company you trust, such as your bank, a delivery service, or a utility company. Its job is to push you to tap a link or open an attachment. The link leads to a fake copy of a real sign-in or payment page, and whatever you type there — password, card number, account details — goes straight to the scammer.

The U.S. Federal Trade Commission (FTC), the agency that protects American consumers, says scammers use stories like an overdue invoice, a problem with your account, or a request to confirm your card or bank number. If they collect personal details such as your date of birth or government ID number, they can go further and try to steal your identity itself. And this is no rare trick: the FTC says email was the top way scammers contacted people in 2024.

## Warning Sign One: The Message Rushes You

Real companies inform you. Scammers alarm you. Read the message again and feel its temperature. Does it shout? "Urgent action required." "Your account will be closed." "Your account has been compromised." "Act now or lose your money."

That fear is not an accident — it is the tool. The FTC gives beautifully simple advice for exactly this moment: slow down. Ask yourself two questions. Do I even have an account with this company? Do I actually know this sender? If the answer to either is no, you are almost certainly looking at a fake. And even if the answer is yes, still check it in the safe way described below — never through the message itself.

## Warning Sign Two: The Sender's Address Is Almost Right

Look closely at who really sent the message — not the name it shows, but the actual address or number behind it. Scammers often register addresses that look similar to a company's real one, sometimes using free email accounts dressed up with a company name. One letter changed. One extra word added. A strange ending after the name.

Our eyes are lazy readers; they see the first few letters and relax. Scammers know this. So read the address slowly, letter by letter. Safety guides add one more warning: scammers can even "spoof" a number, so a fake message can appear inside the same thread as real messages from your bank. Even a familiar thread is not proof — the content still has to pass the other checks.

## Warning Sign Three: It Does Not Know Your Name

Phishing messages are fired at thousands of people at once, like seeds scattered on a field. The sender usually has your phone number or email address — but not your name. So the message opens with a cold, generic greeting: "Dear Customer." "Dear Member." Or it greets nobody at all and dives straight into the threat.

Your real bank, on its real app, knows who you are. A message about your money that cannot name you deserves deep suspicion. The same goes for sloppy spelling, broken grammar and cheap-looking design inside the message — small errors that a big, careful company would rarely send, but a scam factory working at speed produces every day.

## Warning Sign Four: The Link and the Attachment

Everything in the message pushes toward one action: tap here. That link is the hook. It opens a page that may copy the real company's colours, logo and layout almost perfectly — logos are easy to steal, so a familiar-looking page proves nothing. What gives the page away is its address: the name slightly misspelled, an extra letter where you would not expect one, or a number quietly sitting in place of a letter. Check addresses character by character before you ever type a password. On many phones you can press and hold a link to preview its real address without opening it — if the preview looks strange, stop there.

Attachments are the other weapon. An unexpected file — an "invoice," a "receipt," a "delivery form" — can carry harmful software that records what you type or opens your device to strangers. The rule from consumer-protection experts is blunt: do not open attachments and do not download anything from a message you were not expecting. And remember this solid fact: a genuine company will never ask you, in an unsolicited message, to hand over your password or bank and card numbers to "verify" yourself.

## Three Newer Traps This Post Must Add

**The QR code trap.** A QR code is simply a link you cannot read before it opens — so it deserves exactly the same suspicion as any unexpected link. Scammers now send QR codes inside fake messages and even stick their own codes over real ones in public places. If a message pushes you to scan and pay or scan and log in, treat it like a stranger's link: do not scan, reach the company your own way.

**The WhatsApp code trap.** This one steals whole accounts. Someone — often pretending to be a friend, or WhatsApp support — asks you to share a 6-digit code that just arrived on your phone. That code is your WhatsApp registration code, and WhatsApp itself warns: never share it with anyone. Hand it over and the scammer registers your number on their phone, locks you out, and starts messaging your family from "your" account. No real company, and no real friend, ever needs that code from you.

**The "new number" family trap.** A message arrives from an unknown number: "Hi, this is my new number, I lost my phone — please send money, it is urgent." It may use your son's, daughter's or brother's name. The trick works because it attacks love, not logic. The defence is one phone call: ring the person's *old* number, or another family member, and ask. Two minutes of checking defeat this scam completely.

The line I repeat in my own home is this: **any message that wants speed is asking you to switch off your brain — and that request itself is the loudest warning sign of all.**

## The Stories Scammers Tell Again and Again

The costume changes, but the play stays the same. The FTC lists the scripts scammers return to year after year: a claim that there was suspicious activity or a strange log-in on your account; a sudden problem with your account or payment information; a demand to confirm personal details; an invoice you do not recognise; a link to make a payment; a government refund you must register to receive; or a tempting coupon for free things.

Wrap one in a delivery company's name and you get the "parcel fee" message; wrap one in a bank's name and you get the "account blocked" message; wrap one in a prize or lottery name — a favourite in Pakistan, often dressed as a famous TV show or a welfare programme — and you get the "you have won" message that asks for a small "fee" first. Real prizes never ask winners to pay money to receive them. Learn the pattern — hurry, link, request for details or money — and you can see through every costume it wears.

## The Sixty Second Check Before You Click

When a suspicious message arrives, give yourself one minute and do this instead of tapping.

1. **Breathe and re-read it**, hunting for the warning signs above.
2. **Ask the FTC's two questions:** do I have an account with them, and do I know this sender?
3. **Do not reply.** Even a reply tells the scammer your number is active and invites more attempts.
4. **If it might possibly be real, reach the company your own way.** Open the official app yourself, type the official website address yourself, or call the number printed on your bank card or on a real bill. Never use the phone number, link or address supplied inside the suspicious message; those belong to the trap.

Two quiet habits add armour for the future. Turn on two-factor authentication on your important accounts — the FTC recommends this directly — and on WhatsApp too, where it adds a PIN only you know. And keep your security software updated, mark junk as spam so your filters learn, and delete the fake message once you have dealt with it.

## Already Clicked? Do These Steps Now

If you think you already fell for one, do not waste time on shame — spend it on speed. If you gave away personal or financial information, such as a card or bank account number, the FTC directs people to IdentityTheft.gov, which walks you through the exact steps for the kind of information you lost. Contact your bank through its official number as well, and tell them what happened.

If you opened an attachment or think harmful software may have downloaded, update your computer's security software and then run a full scan, as the FTC advises. Change the password of any account involved — from a clean, safe device — and switch on two-factor authentication.

Finally, report the message — it genuinely helps others. In the United States, forward phishing emails to reportphishing@apwg.org, forward phishing texts to 7726 (SPAM), and report the attempt to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov. In Pakistan, the PTA asks you to forward spam and fraud SMS — the sender's number and the message — to **9000** so the number can be blocked, and serious fraud can be reported to the FIA cybercrime helpline **1991**. Then delete the message and warn your family, because the same message is probably buzzing on their phones too.

The scammer's whole business runs on sixty hurried seconds. Give yourself sixty careful ones instead, and the trap is empty.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How can I tell if a message is fake?

Look for the pattern: it rushes you, the sender's address is slightly wrong, it does not use your name, and it pushes you to tap a link and enter private details. One sign is a warning; several together mean delete it.

### My bank sent me a link to confirm my account. Is it real?

Treat it as fake until you prove otherwise. Real companies do not ask for passwords or card numbers through unsolicited message links. Open the bank's official app or call the number on your card and ask them directly.

### Someone on WhatsApp is asking for a 6-digit code sent to my phone. Should I share it?

Never. That code is your WhatsApp registration code. WhatsApp warns users never to share it with anyone — whoever holds it can take over your account and lock you out.

### A family member messaged from a new number asking for money. What should I do?

Do not send anything yet. Call that person on their old number, or check with another family member first. Scammers copy this story every day, and one verification call defeats it.

### Is a QR code in a message safer than a link?

No. A QR code is a link you cannot read first. If the message is unexpected and the code leads to a payment or login page, treat it exactly like a suspicious link — do not scan it; reach the company your own way.

### What if the message knows my name? Does that make it safe?

No. Names can leak from many places, and a scammer with your name is still a scammer. Use the same check: never enter the message through its own link; reach the company your own way.

### I clicked the link but did not type anything. Am I safe?

Probably, but do not relax completely. Close the page, do not download anything it offers, and run a security scan if anything was downloaded. If you typed any details, follow the recovery steps in this post immediately.

### Where can I report a phishing message?

In the US: forward phishing emails to reportphishing@apwg.org, texts to 7726 (SPAM), and report at ReportFraud.ftc.gov. In Pakistan: forward the fraud SMS to PTA on 9000, and report serious fraud to the FIA cybercrime helpline 1991.
