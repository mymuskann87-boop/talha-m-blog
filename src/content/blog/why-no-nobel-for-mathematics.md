Every October, the world waits for the same exciting announcements. One day a name is read out for physics. Then chemistry. Then medicine, literature, and peace. Camera crews gather, phones ring in quiet laboratories, and grown scientists cry with happiness.

But if you love numbers, you may have noticed something strange. Year after year, decade after decade, one giant subject never gets its turn: mathematics. The language of the universe itself has no Nobel Prize. Why not? Was it an accident? A grudge? A scandal involving a broken heart?

The honest answer is more interesting than any rumour — and it ends with mathematicians building two magnificent prizes of their own.

## It All Begins With One Man's Will

The whole story starts with a single document. Alfred Nobel was a Swedish inventor, entrepreneur, scientist and businessman. In 1895, he signed his last will and left most of his fortune to create a set of prizes. According to NobelPrize.org, the official home of the prize, his will said the awards should go to people who had done the greatest good for humankind.

The will named five areas, and only five: physics, chemistry, physiology or medicine, literature, and peace. The first prizes were handed out in 1901, and they have been given almost every year since.

Read that list of five once more. Physics is there. Chemistry is there. Mathematics is simply not there — and Nobel never wrote a single line explaining why. That silence is where all the guessing began.

One detail makes the puzzle deeper. In 1968, Sweden's central bank created an extra prize for economic sciences in Nobel's memory, and NobelPrize.org itself calls that addition an exception. So the list could grow — yet in more than a century, nobody has ever added mathematics.

## The Honest Answer: Nobody Knows The Real Reason

Here is the part most articles will not tell you plainly: the real reason is not known.

NobelPrize.org has a page where it answers the public's most common questions, and this question is right there on it — why is there no Nobel Prize in mathematics, engineering, biology or environmental science? The official answer does not give a hidden motive at all. It simply points back to the will: the prizes are the ones Nobel himself designated, and the economics prize was a much later exception.

In other words, the man who could have told us died in 1896 without explaining. Everything else — everything — is either legend or educated guessing. Let us look at the legend first, because it is a wonderful story. It is also, as far as historians can tell, not true.

## The Famous Legend Of The Love Rival

The most repeated tale goes like this. Alfred Nobel, the story claims, loved a woman. That woman — in different versions she is his fiancée, his wife, or his lover — rejected him, or betrayed him, for a brilliant Swedish mathematician named Gösta Mittag-Leffler. Nobel, heartbroken and furious, supposedly struck mathematics out of his prizes so that his rival could never win one.

It has everything a great story needs: love, betrayal, revenge. There is only one problem. There is no historical evidence for it at all.

A detailed mathematics history page kept by the University of Waterloo goes through the tale point by point. First, Nobel was never married — so the "wife" in the story never existed. Second, there is no evidence that Nobel and Mittag-Leffler had any real contact, let alone a bitter feud. In fact, near the end of Nobel's life, Mittag-Leffler was busy trying to persuade him to leave a large part of his fortune to Stockholm's university college — hardly the behaviour of a hated enemy. The likely spark of the rumour was office gossip: two of Mittag-Leffler's academic rivals later spread the word that Nobel's coldness toward their institution came from a personal dislike, and the spicy version of the tale grew from there.

So enjoy the legend the way you enjoy a good film. Just do not mistake it for history. Historians cannot confirm it, and the facts we do have point the other way.

## What Historians Think Probably Happened

If revenge is out, what is left? Historians and mathematicians who have studied Nobel's life offer calmer, more sensible explanations — and they fit the man we actually know.

First, Nobel was a practical inventor, not a theory man. His will praised work of practical benefit to mankind — discoveries and inventions that change daily life. The Waterloo history page notes that he was simply not much interested in mathematics or theoretical science, and pure mathematics, in his day, looked like the most theoretical subject of all: beautiful, but far from factories, hospitals and dinner tables.

Second, mathematics may have seemed already provided for. In Nobel's own Scandinavia, mathematicians were already being honoured. King Oscar II of Sweden had set up prize competitions for mathematics across Europe, promoted with Mittag-Leffler's help, and famous names such as Hermite, Bertrand, Weierstrass and Poincaré were among those honoured by the king. If Nobel knew mathematics already had royal prizes at home, he may have felt there was no gap for him to fill.

None of this can be proven from a document in Nobel's own hand. But together, these explanations paint a believable picture: a practical man, funding practical good, in a country where maths already had its medals.

## The Fields Medal: Glory With A Time Limit

Mathematicians, in the end, did not wait forever for Stockholm to change its mind. They created their own highest honours — and the first of them came with a dramatic twist.

The Fields Medal, described by Encyclopaedia Britannica as often considered the mathematical equivalent of the Nobel Prize, was first awarded in 1936. It is named after John Charles Fields, a mathematics professor at the University of Toronto, whose efforts and estate helped create it. Unlike the Nobel Prizes, which arrive every year, the Fields Medal is given only once every four years, at the great world congress of mathematicians — and to at most four winners at a time.

Then comes the twist: you must be under 40 years old. The medal honours work already done, but also the promise of what a young mind may still achieve. It is a prize for rising suns, not setting ones. Jean-Pierre Serre, the youngest winner ever, received it in 1954 at the age of 27.

The age rule has produced real heartbreak. Andrew Wiles, the English mathematician who finally proved Fermat's Last Theorem — a problem that had resisted the world for centuries — published his proof in 1995, when he was already over 40. The medal was beyond his reach, so the International Mathematical Union did something special instead: in 1998 it presented him with a silver plaque honouring the achievement. And in 2014, Maryam Mirzakhani became the first woman to win the Fields Medal, making history of a happier kind.

## The Abel Prize: Norway Builds A True Twin

The second answer came from Norway, and it was designed to look much more like a Nobel. The Abel Prize is named after Niels Henrik Abel, a brilliant Norwegian mathematician of the 19th century. A memorial fund in his name was established on 1 January 2002, and the prize is run by the Norwegian Academy of Science and Letters.

Britannica explains that the Abel Prize is given every year for outstanding work in mathematics, that it is worth about one million dollars, and that one of its purposes is to raise the standing of mathematics in society — exactly the gap Nobel left behind. There is no age limit. Where the Fields Medal catches mathematicians young, the Abel Prize honours a whole life of work.

The first Abel Prize, in 2003, went to Jean-Pierre Serre — the same man who had won the Fields Medal at 27 almost fifty years earlier. The two prizes, between them, had bookended one extraordinary career.

So mathematics never got its Nobel. Instead, it got something arguably better suited to its nature: a medal that celebrates youth and promise every four years, and a great annual prize that celebrates a lifetime. Nobel's silence left a hole in the world's most famous prize list — and mathematicians filled it themselves.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is the love-rival story about Nobel and Mittag-Leffler true?**
There is no evidence for it, and historians treat it as a legend. Nobel never married, there is no record of a feud between the two men, and Mittag-Leffler was actually trying to win Nobel's support for a Stockholm institution late in Nobel's life.

**Did Alfred Nobel hate mathematics?**
There is no sign of hate. The more sensible explanation historians discuss is that Nobel, a practical inventor, aimed his prizes at work with direct benefit to humankind, and mathematics looked purely theoretical to him. He also lived in a Scandinavia where mathematicians were already receiving royal honours.

**What is the Fields Medal?**
It is one of the two highest honours in mathematics, first awarded in 1936. It is given every four years to between two and four mathematicians who must be under 40, rewarding both achievement and future promise.

**What is the Abel Prize?**
It is Norway's annual prize for outstanding work in mathematics, established through a fund created in 2002 and first awarded in 2003. It is worth about one million dollars and has no age limit, so it usually honours a lifetime of work.

**Will there ever be a Nobel Prize in mathematics?**
There is no sign of one. The categories were fixed in Nobel's will, and the only addition ever made — the economics prize in 1968 — is described by NobelPrize.org itself as an exception. Mathematicians have long since built their own great prizes instead.
