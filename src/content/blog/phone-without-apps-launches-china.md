Imagine switching on a brand-new phone and finding almost nothing waiting for you. There is no colourful wall of little squares. There is no row of favourite apps along the bottom. There is no familiar grid inviting you to tap, scroll, open, close, and then open something else.

At first glance, the phone looks unfinished, as if the most important part has been left out. A normal smartphone shows its power through its apps. Remove them, and most people would ask a very reasonable question: what is left to use?

That puzzle sits at the heart of the STEPX Neo, a smartphone launched in China by StepFun. The Chinese artificial intelligence startup has not forgotten the apps by accident. The empty-looking screen is part of a much bigger idea. Instead of asking the owner to move from app to app, the phone is built around an autonomous AI agent named Amoo.

The answer, then, is not that the phone does less. StepFun wants it to work in a different way.

## A Home Screen With Nothing to Open

For years, the home screen has been the front door of a smartphone. Each app has been like a separate room. One room handles one job, another room handles something else, and the owner keeps walking between them. The design is familiar, but it also puts most of the work on the person holding the phone.

The STEPX Neo challenges that habit. It does not begin with a grid of applications. Its software is arranged around Amoo, an agent placed inside the core of the phone. That single choice changes the question from “Which app should I open?” to “What can the agent handle from here?”

This is why the missing icons matter. They are not merely a visual trick or a cleaner design. They signal a different starting point. On a conventional phone, the apps stand in front and the intelligence stays behind them. On the STEPX Neo, the agent moves to the front of the experience.

It is easy to see why the idea creates curiosity. People are used to judging a phone by the services sitting on its screen. An empty screen gives them nothing to count. StepFun is asking them to judge the phone by what its built-in agent can do instead.

That is a bold change in something deeply familiar. The phone in a pocket may still look like a phone, but the way a person begins using it has been turned upside down.

## The Missing Piece Is Named Amoo

Amoo is the centre of the STEPX Neo. StepFun describes it as an autonomous AI agent, and it is built into the phone’s core software rather than treated as one more extra.

The word “autonomous” is important. It points to software designed to carry out tasks, not simply sit inside another program and wait to be helpful. StepFun’s argument is that older smartphone systems were never designed for that kind of autonomous task execution. The company is therefore not just adding a smarter assistant to an old pattern. It is questioning the pattern itself.

That difference may sound small, but it shapes the whole product. If the agent is only a guest inside an app, the app remains in charge. If the agent sits in the foundation, the phone can be organised around the agent from the beginning.

Amoo gives the empty home screen its explanation. The icons are absent because the phone is not built around opening separate applications in the usual way. Its central figure is software meant to work across the system.

## Built Into the Foundation, Not Added on Top

The STEPX Neo runs StepFun’s own software, called Step AOS 1.0. That detail matters because the company has not simply placed Amoo on top of a familiar system and hoped for the best. The agent and the operating software have been presented as parts of one design.

StepFun draws a clear contrast with iOS and Android. On those systems, artificial intelligence functions mainly as an assistant living inside applications. The apps still form the basic structure. A person moves through that structure, and the AI helps from within it.

StepFun wants to reverse the relationship. In Step AOS 1.0, the agent is part of the foundation. The foundation is the layer everything else depends on, so placing Amoo there makes the agent central rather than decorative.

Think of the difference between giving directions to a visitor in a house and designing the house around the person who manages it. A visitor can be useful, but the layout remains unchanged. A manager built into the design changes how the whole place is arranged.

That is the cleanest way to understand the STEPX Neo. Its novelty is not only the absence of icons. Its novelty is the position given to Amoo.

My honest view is that the empty screen is not the brave part; putting the agent at the centre of the whole phone is.

## Why Almost 100 Partners Matter

An agent at the centre of a phone would have limited value if it stood alone. Phones become useful when they connect with the services people and businesses rely on. StepFun seems to understand that challenge because nearly 100 corporate partners have integrated their platforms to work directly with the agent.

Two names in that group stand out: Alipay and Tencent. Their presence gives the project weight because the phone is not being presented as a lonely experiment. Platforms have been brought into the system so they can work with Amoo directly.

The number matters for another reason. Building an agent-first phone is not only a software problem. It is also a connection problem. If outside platforms refuse to fit into the new design, the agent remains trapped in a small world. Partner integrations widen that world.

Nearly 100 partners do not answer every question about the phone, but they do show that StepFun is not relying on the idea alone. The company has brought other platforms into its approach before the first sales event.

That support also makes the empty screen easier to explain. The services have not simply vanished from the story. The relationship between the phone and its platforms has been rearranged, with Amoo standing between them.

## A Phone First Shown in Shanghai

StepFun previewed the STEPX Neo in Shanghai earlier this year. During that preview, the company made its case against the limits of legacy smartphone systems. Its position is that those systems were created for a different age of phone use, one built around applications rather than autonomous agents.

That argument gives the launch a clear target. StepFun is not saying that the old systems lack features. It is saying their basic design belongs to another model. If software agents are meant to execute tasks on their own, then a system designed around manual app-hopping may not be the best home for them.

This is also why the STEPX Neo should not be treated as a normal phone with a strange home screen. The company has presented the hardware, the software, and the criticism of older systems as one package. The phone is the physical form of an argument.

Whether buyers accept that argument is another matter. A preview can explain a vision, but the product still has to earn trust when real people begin using it. The launch moves the idea from a Shanghai stage into the harder world of customers, prices, and first impressions.

## Price, Pre-Orders, and the First Sales Event

The STEPX Neo starts at 3,999 yuan, roughly $560. Pre-orders have opened ahead of its first sales event.

Those details place the phone in the real market rather than keeping it as a concept. StepFun is asking customers to pay for a new way of using a smartphone, not merely admire the idea from a distance.

The price also creates a useful test. If the phone were only a demonstration, the empty screen could be dismissed as a stunt. A priced product with pre-orders sends a different message: the company believes the agent-first design is ready to be judged as a consumer device.

Still, the price cannot explain the phone by itself. Its value will depend on the idea behind it. Buyers are being offered a handset whose central promise is not a wall of apps, but an agent built into the system’s core.

## The Hard Question Behind the Empty Screen

Every new design solves one problem by creating a new question. The STEPX Neo removes the familiar grid, but it also removes the comfort of the familiar grid. People know where things are on a normal phone. They understand the small rituals of tapping and switching. A new system has to make its own logic feel natural.

StepFun’s answer is Amoo. If the agent truly sits at the foundation, then the phone does not need to present itself as a box of separate tools. It can begin with the agent and let the connected platforms work through that centre.

The partner list supports the idea, and Step AOS 1.0 gives it a software home. Yet the concept remains a challenge to habit. Most people do not buy a phone hoping to relearn the first step of using it. The STEPX Neo asks them to see that missing first step as progress.

That tension makes the launch interesting. The phone’s screen may look empty, but the argument behind it is full: smartphones were built for apps, while StepFun believes they should now be built for agents.
