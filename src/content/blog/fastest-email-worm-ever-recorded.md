Picture a race where the runner does not get tired, does not sleep, and makes a copy of itself at every finish line. In January 2004, that runner entered the world's inboxes. Its name was MyDoom, and within days it had covered more ground than any email worm in history. Other worms spread fast. MyDoom spread like a stopwatch was chasing it. Security experts measured it, counted it, and tried to keep up with the numbers climbing in front of their eyes. More than two decades later, the record it set has never been beaten. This is the story of how a small email attachment turned into the biggest traffic jam the internet had ever seen.

## What MyDoom is, and what it does to a computer

MyDoom is a computer worm, and a worm is a special kind of harmful program. A normal virus usually needs a person to pass it along, but a worm copies itself and travels on its own. MyDoom lived inside email. It arrived as an attachment, and the moment someone opened that attachment, the worm woke up inside the machine and went to work.

Its first job was to dig in. MyDoom opened what experts call a backdoor, which is exactly what it sounds like: a hidden second entrance into the computer. The owner used the front door with a password, but strangers who knew about the backdoor could slip in quietly and take control of the machine. They could look around, use the computer for their own purposes, and the real owner might never notice a thing.

Its second job was to multiply. MyDoom searched through the victim's files, hunting for email addresses stored on the machine. Every address it found became a new target. The worm then sent fresh copies of itself to those addresses, using the victim's own computer as the launch pad. One opened attachment could quietly turn into dozens of new infected emails, and each of those could turn into dozens more. That is the engine behind every speed record MyDoom broke: it did not need to travel far, because every victim became a new starting line.

## How MyDoom spread so fast, and how a worm like it reaches you

Speed in a race like this comes down to one thing: how easily the bait is swallowed. MyDoom's emails did not look dramatic or frightening. They looked plain and boring, the kind of message anyone might receive on a normal workday. Many of them pretended to be delivery failure notices, the automatic messages that say an email you sent could not be delivered. Others looked like simple test messages. The subject lines were short and curious rather than alarming, and the real danger sat attached, waiting for one curious click.

That plainness was the whole trick, and it worked on an enormous scale. At its peak, experts estimated that roughly one in every twelve emails moving around the world carried MyDoom. Stop and picture that number properly. Count twelve emails arriving anywhere on Earth, in any office or home, and one of them was the worm. Mail servers groaned under the weight of it, and email and internet traffic noticeably slowed across the globe as systems struggled to carry the flood.

Email was not its only road. MyDoom also spread through the Kazaa file-sharing network, a popular place where people downloaded and shared files with each other. The worm placed copies of itself inside shared folders, dressed up as files other people might want. Anyone who downloaded and opened one of those files gave the worm a new home and a new list of addresses to attack.

This is exactly how a worm like MyDoom can still reach an ordinary person today. It does not knock down the door with noise and flashing warnings. It arrives looking like the most forgettable message in the inbox, or it hides inside a free download that seems harmless. The person who opens it is usually not careless or foolish. They are busy, the message looks routine, and one small click is all the worm ever needed.

## The damage: a flooded website, a huge reward, and a record-breaking bill

MyDoom did more than travel. Built into the worm was a weapon aimed at a single target: the website of the SCO Group, a software company. On a set schedule, the army of infected computers all turned toward that website at once and flooded it with junk visits. This kind of attack is called a denial-of-service attack. The idea is simple. If thousands upon thousands of computers demand the website's attention at the same moment, the site cannot cope, like a small shop door crushed by an entire stadium trying to enter at once. The flood was meant to knock the site offline, and infected machines carried out the attack whether their owners knew it or not.

The company fought back in an unusual way. SCO offered a reward of $250,000 for information leading to the arrest of the worm's creator. It was a serious sum of money, and it made headlines around the world. Yet for all the money on the table and all the experts hunting, the creator of MyDoom was never caught. To this day, nobody knows for certain who built the fastest email worm in history.

The story did not end with one target. A later variant of MyDoom appeared with a similar flood aimed at Microsoft's website, showing that the worm's design could be pointed at new victims. Meanwhile, the everyday damage kept piling up quietly: slowed networks, clogged inboxes, lost working hours, and cleanup work in offices everywhere. When experts later added up the total economic cost of MyDoom, counting lost work and the price of cleaning and repairing systems, the highest estimates reached as high as $38 billion. That figure is an estimate at the top end, not a settled number everyone agrees on, but even the cautious readings agree on this: no email worm before or since has matched MyDoom's combination of speed, size, and cost.

## How to protect yourself from a worm like MyDoom

The good news hidden inside this record-breaking story is that worms like MyDoom win through habits, and habits can be changed. None of the steps below need special skills. They need a few seconds of patience at the right moment.

### Treat unexpected attachments as guilty until proven innocent

The single most important step is the simplest: do not open an email attachment you were not expecting, even if the message looks official and even if it seems to come from a name you recognise. A worm sends itself from infected computers, so the sender's name proves nothing. If a delivery notice, an invoice, or a test message arrives with a file attached and you did not ask for it, delete it. If it might be real, contact the company or the person through a separate route you trust, like their official website or a phone number you already have, and ask before opening anything.

### Keep your antivirus switched on and updated

Antivirus software works like a guard who studies photographs of known criminals. MyDoom and its relatives are in those photographs now, but new threats appear constantly, which is why the updates matter as much as the program itself. Install a reputable antivirus, let it update itself automatically, and never switch off its protection because it feels slow or annoying. An out-of-date guard recognises yesterday's burglars, not today's.

### Update your system and your apps

The makers of your operating system and your email apps release updates that fix security holes, the same way a locksmith repairs a weak door. Worms look for those weak doors. Turning on automatic updates means the fixes arrive without you having to remember, and it quietly closes the gaps that worms and other harmful programs crawl through.

### Be careful with free downloads and file sharing

MyDoom's second road was a file-sharing network, and free downloads remain a favourite hiding place for harmful programs. A free game, song, or tool from an unofficial source can carry a passenger you never asked for. Download software only from official sites and trusted app stores, and be suspicious of any downloaded file that asks you to open extra attachments or install unexpected extras before it will work.

### Slow down for boring emails

This sounds strange, but it may be the most useful lesson of all. MyDoom proved that the plainest messages spread the furthest, because nobody fears a boring email. I honestly think the dull little delivery-failure message in your inbox deserves more suspicion than the dramatic warning full of capital letters. The dramatic one is probably ordinary spam. The dull one with an attachment might be a record-breaking worm wearing its most successful disguise.

## Common questions

### Is MyDoom still spreading today?

The great flood of January 2004 is long over, and modern email systems and antivirus programs recognise MyDoom quickly. Its speed record, though, still stands, which is why it is still taught as the classic example of how fast a worm can move. Copies and descendants of old worms can still turn up on unprotected machines, so basic protection still matters.

### Why is MyDoom called the fastest email worm ever?

Because of the sheer scale it reached in such a short time. At its peak, roughly one in every twelve emails worldwide was estimated to carry it, and it noticeably slowed email and internet traffic across the planet. No email worm since has matched that combination of speed and volume.

### Did they ever catch the person who made MyDoom?

No. The SCO Group offered a $250,000 reward for information leading to the creator's arrest, but the creator was never caught or publicly identified. The authorship of the fastest email worm in history remains an unsolved mystery.

### Can a worm like MyDoom infect a phone?

MyDoom itself was built for the computers and email systems of its time, not for modern phones. But the trick it used, a harmless-looking message with a dangerous attachment or link, works on any device. Phones are attacked through malicious links, fake apps, and infected downloads, so the same caution applies on a small screen.

### What should I do if I opened a suspicious attachment?

Disconnect the device from the internet to stop anything spreading further, then run a full scan with your antivirus software. Change your important passwords from a different, clean device, especially email and banking passwords. If the machine belongs to your workplace, tell the person in charge of computers straight away, because one infected machine can endanger the whole network.
