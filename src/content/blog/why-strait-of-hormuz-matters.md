Put your finger on a map, halfway between the Persian Gulf and the open ocean, and you will find a strip of water so narrow that you could drive its width in less than an hour. Ships carrying the fuel that heats homes, runs factories and fills cars across Asia must all squeeze through it. That strip of water is the Strait of Hormuz, and when trouble reaches it, the whole world feels it within days.

This post explains, in plain words, what the strait is, why so much energy passes through it, and why there is no easy way around it.

## A Narrow Strip Of Sea

The Strait of Hormuz connects the Persian Gulf to the Gulf of Oman and the Indian Ocean. It is the main way out to sea for oil producers around the Gulf. At its narrowest, the strait is just 21 nautical miles wide, about 38 kilometres. That is narrow for the size of the ships that use it. Huge tankers pass in both directions, in set lanes, with little room to spare.

The land on either side belongs to Iran on the northern shore and Oman on the southern shore, with the United Arab Emirates close by. The strait is not a wide open sea where ships can spread out. It is a corridor, and every ship going in or out of the Gulf must travel through the same corridor.

## A Chokepoint, In Plain Words

Experts call places like this maritime chokepoints. A chokepoint is a narrow stretch of sea through which a large share of world trade must pass, with no easy alternative route. Coastlines and shallow water force ships into tight lanes. Because ships cannot easily go another way, a single incident in a chokepoint can disrupt traffic almost at once.

The Hindu, explaining why the strait matters, describes chokepoints as critical nodes in the trading system. Between 70 and 80 percent of the world's oil moves by sea, and much of it passes through chokepoints of this kind. When one of them is disturbed, the effects travel far beyond the water itself.

## How Much Oil Passes Through

Under normal circumstances, about 21 million barrels of oil pass through the Strait of Hormuz each day. That is roughly one-fifth of the world's daily oil consumption. The figure matters because it turns a local stretch of water into a global concern. A fifth of the oil the world uses is not a small share. It is the fuel for a large part of daily life in many countries.

It is not only crude oil. Large volumes of liquefied natural gas, known as LNG, also move through the strait, much of it from Qatar and the United Arab Emirates. Gas heats homes and powers electricity plants. When both oil and gas use the same narrow route, a disruption can touch heating, transport and industry at the same time.

## Where The Energy Goes

Nearly 80 percent of the energy flows through the strait go to Asia. Countries such as India, China, Japan and South Korea depend on this route for a large part of their fuel. That dependence is why news from the strait is followed so closely in Asian capitals. A delay there is not an abstract market story. It can mean higher fuel prices at home within weeks.

Europe and other regions feel it too, through prices. Oil is traded worldwide, so a shortage or a scare in one place pushes prices up in many places. Shipping costs rise when insurers grow nervous and ship owners reroute vessels. Supply chains slow down. As The Hindu notes, disruption can feed inflation and slow economic growth, because fuel, fertilisers and petrochemicals all become dearer or harder to move.

## Why Ships Cannot Simply Go Elsewhere

The obvious question is why ships do not just take another route. The honest answer is that there is no good one. Some oil can be moved by pipeline, for example through Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline, but that carries only a portion of the volume normally moved by sea. It cannot replace the daily flow through the strait.

The sea alternative is worse. Sending tankers around the Cape of Good Hope, at the southern tip of Africa, adds two to three weeks to the journey and raises costs sharply. For ships already loaded and heading to Asia, that detour is long, expensive and slow. In a crisis, there is no quick substitute for the strait. That is what makes it so important, and so fragile.

## What Happens When The Strait Is Disturbed

When attacks or threats reach the strait, several things happen at once. Shipping companies grow cautious. Some ships wait. Some take longer routes. Insurance costs rise, because the risk of damage rises. Oil markets react to fear before any real shortage appears, so prices often jump on the news alone.

In 2026, the strait became the centre of an energy and security crisis after United States and Israeli strikes on Iran, followed by tighter Iranian restrictions on the waterway, according to The Hindu. The International Energy Agency described the situation as more severe than the oil shocks of the 1970s. Daily transits fell from around 130 vessels to just a few on several days, and most tankers stayed away while threats and a naval blockade continued.

Ordinary people feel these events through fuel prices and the cost of goods. The connection is not always visible, but it is real. A strait most people will never see helps decide what they pay for petrol, heating and food transport.

## The Law Of The Sea

The movement of ships through straits like Hormuz is governed mainly by the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea. Adopted in 1982 and in force since 1994, it is often described as the constitution for the oceans. It covers maritime security, protection of the marine environment and scientific research.

Under its rules, straits used for international navigation are subject to transit passage. That means ships and aircraft of all nations may pass continuously and without obstruction. Countries along the strait, such as Iran and Oman, may regulate shipping for safety and environmental reasons, but they cannot stop ships from passing or impose selective restrictions. The right is broadly recognised in international law, though in practice, as The Hindu notes, enforcement depends on naval strength and coordination between countries.

## Other Chokepoints Around The World

Hormuz is the most important, but it is not the only chokepoint. The Strait of Malacca, between the Malay Peninsula and the Indonesian island of Sumatra, is the shortest sea route between the Indian Ocean and the South China Sea, and the busiest shipping lane in the world. It handles nearly 30 percent of seaborne oil.

The Bab el-Mandeb Strait, between the Arabian Peninsula and the Horn of Africa, is the southern gateway to the Red Sea and the Suez Canal. The Suez Canal cuts across Egypt, linking the Red Sea to the Mediterranean and saving ships the long journey around Africa. The Panama Canal links the Atlantic and Pacific Oceans across Central America and handles about five percent of global trade.

Each of these places has shown how fragile the system is. Between 2023 and 2025, attacks near Bab el-Mandeb forced ships around the Cape of Good Hope. A drought in 2023–24 slowed traffic through the Panama Canal. Any long disruption at one chokepoint forces the same hard choice: wait, or take a much longer route.

## A Recent Warning From These Waters

In October 2026, the danger in the Strait of Hormuz stopped being theoretical for the shipping world. Two Singapore-registered container vessels were struck by unknown projectiles while passing through the strait. One of them, GFS Pride, caught fire and sank. The other, GFS Giselle, was damaged but reached a safe anchorage. All 43 sailors across the two ships survived.

The wider picture was grimmer. At least ten vessels were reported attacked in or near the strait after 11 October, the highest level since the war in the region began. For crews, insurers and port authorities, those attacks turned a map feature into a daily risk. For readers of this blog, the full account is in our report on the sinking.

## What It Means For Ordinary Readers

You do not need to follow shipping news every day to understand why the Strait of Hormuz matters. Remember three facts. First, about a fifth of the world's oil passes through a strip of water only 38 kilometres wide at its narrowest. Second, most of that energy goes to Asia, but price shocks reach everywhere. Third, there is no ready replacement route. Pipelines carry only part of the flow, and the sea detour takes weeks.

That is why every incident there becomes world news, and why governments, energy agencies and shipping firms watch the strait so closely. A narrow passage, a huge share of the world's fuel, and no easy alternative: that combination is what makes the Strait of Hormuz the world's most important maritime chokepoint.
