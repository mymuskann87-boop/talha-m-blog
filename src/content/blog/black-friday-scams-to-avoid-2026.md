Black Friday in 2026 falls on Friday, 27 November, with Cyber Monday on Monday, 30 November, and that concentration of urgency is exactly what scammers exploit. A price that looks too good to be true, a countdown timer, a social media ad for a famous brand, and a checkout page that asks for an unusual payment method are not separate accidents; they are parts of a system designed to make you pay before you check. The United States Federal Trade Commission's holiday shopping advice is consistent: research the seller, pay by credit card when you can, avoid gift card, wire transfer, payment app or cryptocurrency demands from unknown sellers, and keep your receipt and order number. This guide explains the specific scams behind that advice, how to spot each one in seconds, and what to do if you have already paid.

## Fake Stores and Lookalike Websites

A fake store copies a real retailer's logo, product photographs, layout and even its return policy wording, but it is not that retailer. Its web address is often close to the real one, with an extra word, a swapped letter, or an unusual ending, such as a brand name followed by "deals" or "sale". Prices are far below every other retailer, especially for a new gadget, console or designer brand, and the site may have no verifiable address, phone number, or business history. Reviews on the site itself may be generic, with similar wording, no dates, or no reviewer history. A padlock or "https" in the address bar does not prove safety; the FTC notes that scammers can use encryption too, so it only shows the connection is encrypted. Before buying from an unfamiliar store, type the retailer's address yourself instead of clicking an ad, search the seller's name and web address together with "review", "complaint" or "scam", and check whether the same product is listed at a similar price by known retailers. If the store appeared only this month, has no social media history, and sells only high-demand items at impossible prices, stop. A real discount is usually modest and consistent across retailers; an impossible discount is a warning, not an opportunity.

## Social Media Ads That Impersonate Real Brands

Social media ads are now a primary route for Black Friday scams. The FTC warns that scammers impersonate real companies and advertise brand-name products at unusually low prices on Facebook, Instagram and TikTok. The ad may use a familiar logo, a stolen product video, and comments that look enthusiastic, but clicking it leads to a fake site that takes your money and sends a counterfeit, a different product, or nothing. Comments can be fake or moderated to remove warnings, and the advertiser's page may have been created recently, with few posts and no real customer interaction. The defence is to treat an ad as an idea, not as a store. Note the product, close the ad, and search for the promotion on the retailer's official website or app. If the official site does not list the same price, the ad is not a secret deal; it is a trap. Also check the advertiser's profile: a real brand usually has a long history, verified status where available, and links to its official domain. A page created in November, selling only one viral product, with a domain that does not match the brand, should not receive payment. Screenshot the ad before closing it, because that screenshot helps a report later if you need to prove what you saw.

## Phishing Emails and Fake Order Problems

During Black Friday week, inboxes fill with order confirmations, shipping updates, refunds and account warnings, which makes phishing emails blend in. The classic play is an "account problem", "payment failed" or "thanks for your purchase" message that urges you to click a link to verify details. The page you land on mimics a real store and harvests passwords, card numbers and personal data. In 2026 reporting, the FTC also warned about Amazon-related refund scams in which people who tapped through landed on fake support pages. Treat any surprise receipt, refund or account problem as untrusted until you verify it yourself. Do not click the email link; open the retailer's app or type its address and check your real orders there. Check the sender's address carefully, but remember that a familiar display name can be faked, so the link destination matters more than the name. Look for urgency language, spelling errors, and a request for a password, one-time code or full card number, none of which a real retailer should ask for by email link. Keep multi-factor authentication on shopping accounts, use a unique password for each major retailer, and never share a one-time code with someone who contacts you first. If you clicked a phishing link, change the password immediately, check recent orders, and contact your card issuer.

## Fake Delivery Texts and Smishing

Smishing is phishing by text message, and it spikes when parcels are expected. A text claims a USPS, UPS, FedEx or other carrier has a delivery problem, asks you to pay a small redelivery fee, or asks you to confirm an address by clicking a link. The link leads to a lookalike site that collects card numbers and addresses. The FTC has warned shoppers to treat unexpected delivery texts with scepticism and to track parcels only through a retailer account or a carrier's official site. Legitimate carriers do not collect redelivery fees by text or ask for full card numbers to fix an address. If you are waiting for a parcel, ignore the message and check the order in the retailer's app or the carrier's official site by typing the address yourself. Do not reply to the text, do not call a number in the text, and do not download an attachment from an unknown sender. Report the message using your phone's spam reporting option where available, and delete it after reporting. A small fee is a powerful trick because it feels too small to be a scam, but the goal is not the fee; it is your card details and personal data, which can be used for larger fraud later. Any delivery message that demands immediate payment is a reason to stop, not to hurry.

## Gift Card, Wire Transfer and Cryptocurrency Payment Traps

The FTC's guidance on payment is blunt: only scammers tell you to pay with gift cards, and a seller that insists on a gift card, wire transfer, payment app or cryptocurrency is probably a scam. These methods are difficult or impossible to reverse, unlike a credit card dispute for an item that never arrived, was damaged, or was wrong. Gift card scams take two forms in Black Friday season. One is a direct demand: pay with gift card codes to unlock a discount, reserve an item, or pay a fee. The second is card draining, where tampered gift cards in stores are emptied soon after activation, a tactic singled out in fraud reporting as growing. Protect yourself by never sending gift card codes to an unfamiliar seller, never reading a code over the phone to someone who contacted you, and inspecting gift card packaging for tampering before buying one as a gift. For online shopping, prefer a credit card when you can, because you can dispute charges for double billing, missing items, or wrong or damaged goods. If a seller refuses normal checkout, sends a separate payment link, or changes the merchant name at checkout, stop and verify through the retailer's official support channel. A discount that requires an irreversible payment is not a discount; it is a transfer of risk from the seller to you.

## Fake Discounts, Price Bumps and Counterfeit Goods

Not every Black Friday scam steals your payment directly; some overcharge you for a product that is not worth the price. A common tactic is to raise the price shortly before the sale, then display a large percentage discount from the inflated "was" price. The final price may be the same as, or higher than, the price a few weeks earlier. A price-tracking study of the 2025 holiday season, reported in November 2026, found that roughly one-third of Black Friday and Cyber Monday deals tracked had been available for less earlier in the season, with luggage and microwaves among the categories most often cheaper before the sale. The defence is to check price history before Black Friday, not on the day. Note the price of items you want in early November, use a price tracker where available, and compare the final checkout total, including delivery and fees, with that earlier price. Counterfeit goods are a related risk: a fake store may send a product that looks like the brand but uses inferior materials, lacks a warranty, or fails safety checks. Buy electronics, skincare, supplements and branded goods from authorised sellers, check warranty terms, and be wary of a seller who cannot explain returns or warranty support. If a product arrives and looks wrong, photograph it, keep the packaging, and contact the seller and your card issuer promptly.

## Marketplace, Resale and Account Takeover Risks

Marketplaces and resale sites add a layer of risk during Black Friday, because scammers create new seller accounts, list popular items at low prices, and ask buyers to complete payment outside the marketplace's normal process. Once payment leaves the marketplace, its buyer protection may not apply. Always complete payment inside the marketplace, check the seller's history, ratings and return policy, and be wary of a seller with no history who suddenly lists many high-demand items. Account takeover is another seasonal risk: a scammer who gains access to your shopping, email or marketplace account can change delivery addresses, place orders, or send phishing messages to your contacts. Use strong, unique passwords, enable multi-factor authentication, and review account activity after Black Friday. If you receive a password reset you did not request, do not click it; go to the site directly and change your password. For children and older relatives who shop online, explain the same rules in simple terms: do not click surprise links, do not pay with gift cards for a deal, and ask a trusted person before buying from an unfamiliar store. A family rule that any unfamiliar store purchase waits twenty-four hours prevents most impulse scams, because a fake deal relies on urgency, and a day of checking removes that urgency.

## What to Do If You Paid a Scammer or Shared Details

Act quickly, in order, and keep records. First, contact your card issuer or bank immediately to dispute the charge and, if needed, replace the card. Explain that the seller was fraudulent, provide the order number, website, date and amount, and ask what dispute window applies. Second, if you shared a password or one-time code, change that password and any account that used the same password, enable multi-factor authentication, and check for changed addresses or orders. Third, report the scam to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov in the United States, and report the seller to the marketplace or social media platform where you found it. Reporting does not guarantee a refund, but it helps regulators track patterns and helps other shoppers. Fourth, save everything: screenshots of the ad, website, order confirmation, payment receipt, emails and texts. Do not pay anyone who promises to recover your money for an upfront fee; that is often a second scam targeting victims of the first. If you received a counterfeit or wrong item, photograph it before returning it, and follow the card issuer's instructions about returns. If personal data such as an identity document number was shared, follow official identity protection guidance in your country and monitor accounts for unusual activity. Speed matters more than embarrassment; reporting early improves the chance of stopping a charge and protecting others.

## A Sixty-Second Check Before You Pay

Before paying any unfamiliar store, run a sixty-second check that stops most scams. First, look at the web address: type it yourself, check for extra words, swapped letters, or a domain that does not match the brand. Second, search the seller's name plus "review" and "scam" and look for a real history, not only reviews on the seller's own site. Third, compare the price with two known retailers; if it is far below both, treat it as a warning. Fourth, read the return and contact pages: a real business gives an address, a return process, and a way to contact a human, while a fake site often gives vague wording or no address. Fifth, check the payment page: if it demands a gift card, wire transfer, cryptocurrency, or a separate payment link outside a marketplace, stop. Sixth, screenshot the product, price, seller and delivery promise before paying, so you have evidence for a dispute. Sixty seconds feels slow when a timer is counting down, but it is faster than weeks of disputing a charge, changing passwords, and explaining a loss. Make this check a habit for every unfamiliar store in November, not a special step for suspicious ones, because the most dangerous fake stores look professional.

## My Take:

My firm view is that Black Friday scams succeed not because shoppers are careless, but because the sale is designed to remove the pause that protects them. A countdown timer, a "only three left" warning, a social media ad that appears while you are scrolling, and a checkout page that asks for payment in seconds all push you to act before you think. Scammers copy that design because it works. My defence is therefore not intelligence; it is a rule that restores the pause. I would never buy from an unfamiliar store on the same day I discover it. I would note the product, close the ad, and check the official retailer, the price history, and the seller's history the next day. That twenty-four-hour rule costs nothing, removes urgency, and stops most fake-store scams, because a fake store relies on you paying before you check. If a deal is real, it will usually still be available after a check, or a similar deal will appear. If a deal disappears because you checked, it was probably not a deal worth trusting.

I also think payment method is the clearest line in Black Friday shopping. A seller who accepts a credit card through normal checkout is accountable, because a dispute is possible. A seller who demands a gift card, wire transfer, cryptocurrency or a payment link outside the marketplace is asking you to give up that accountability. I would refuse that demand without negotiation, even if the discount looks large. A large discount paid irreversibly is not a saving; it is a loss with a discount label. I would apply the same logic to marketplace purchases: if a seller asks to move payment outside the platform, I would walk away, because buyer protection is part of the price. People often focus on the product price and forget that payment protection, return rights and warranty support are also part of what they are buying. A cheap product without those protections is expensive when something goes wrong.

My view on social media ads is sceptical by design. I treat every Black Friday ad as an idea, not as evidence. A familiar logo, a convincing video and enthusiastic comments do not prove a seller is real, because all three can be copied or faked. I would check the advertiser's page age, its official domain, and whether the official brand lists the same offer. That check takes two minutes, and it is the difference between a bargain and a loss. I would also screenshot ads before closing them, because a screenshot helps a report and helps you remember what was promised. Many shoppers delete the evidence in frustration, then struggle to dispute the charge. Keeping a simple folder of order confirmations, screenshots and receipts during November is a habit worth building, especially for larger purchases.

I believe families need a simple, shared rule for Black Friday, not a long lecture about fraud. The rule I would teach children and older relatives is: do not click a surprise link, do not pay with a gift card for a deal, and ask a trusted person before buying from a store you have never used. Those three sentences cover phishing, smishing, gift card traps and fake stores, and they are easy to remember under pressure. I would also set a family rule that any purchase over a certain amount waits a day, and that any delivery message demanding a fee is ignored until checked in the retailer's app. Those rules do not require technical knowledge; they require a pause and a second person. Scammers target people who are alone, rushed, or embarrassed to ask. A family that normalises asking for a second check removes that advantage.

I am also wary of fake discounts that are technically legal but misleading. A price raised before a sale and then "discounted" back to normal is not a bargain, even if no money is stolen. I would therefore check price history in early November, note the prices of items I actually want, and judge Black Friday by the final total, not by the percentage badge. A study finding that about one-third of deals had been cheaper earlier in the season should change how we shop: Black Friday is a good time to buy items you have tracked and wanted, not a time to buy items because they are labelled as deals. I would make a short list before November, set a target price for each item, and buy only when the total beats that target. Everything else, however tempting, is not a saving; it is spending disguised as saving.

Finally, I would respond to a scam without shame and without paying a recovery scammer. Many victims stay silent because they feel foolish, but silence helps the scammer and delays a dispute. I would contact the card issuer immediately, change passwords, report to the FTC and the platform, and keep every screenshot and receipt. I would also tell family members what happened, because a shared warning can prevent the same ad from harming someone else. A scam is not a reflection of intelligence; it is a reflection of a system designed to exploit urgency, trust and festive excitement. The best response is calm, fast and documented: stop the payment, protect the account, report the seller, and restore the pause for the next purchase. That approach does not guarantee a refund, but it gives you the best chance, and it turns a bad experience into a warning that protects others.

I would pay special attention to children and older relatives, because scammers target both with different tactics. Children may click a game or toy ad, enter a parent's card details, or download a fake app that harvests data. Older relatives may receive a phone call, text, or email claiming an account problem, a delivery fee, or a refund, and feel pressured to act politely and quickly. The defence is the same family rule, explained without fear: never pay with a gift card for a deal, never click a surprise link, and always ask a trusted person before buying from an unfamiliar store. Set up purchase approvals on children's devices, keep card details out of shared devices, and agree that any surprise account message will be checked in the official app, never by replying to the message. Protecting vulnerable family members is not about restricting them; it is about giving them a simple, dignified rule they can use under pressure.
