To a child, Halloween is the best night of the year: costumes, sweets, and neighbours handing out treasure at every door. To a dog or a cat, it is the strangest night of the year: the doorbell ringing every two minutes, strangers in masks, flashing lights, strange smells, and bowls of food that can poison them. Animals do not understand the joke, and they cannot tell us when the fun has become fear. Keeping them safe on Halloween is a matter of planning for their experience of the night, not ours.

Animal welfare groups publish the same advice every year because the same accidents happen every year. PETA's Halloween safety release of October 2026 and a vet guide from Second Street Animal Hospital, citing the ASPCA, agree on the essentials: keep animals away from candy and decorations, keep cats indoors, give frightened pets a quiet room, and think twice before putting any animal in a costume. This guide brings that advice together, with the details that make each step work.

## Keep every sweet out of reach

Candy is the most dangerous part of Halloween for pets, and it is everywhere on the night. Chocolate is dangerous for both cats and dogs, and dark and baking chocolate are the most dangerous of all, because they contain higher levels of the substances dogs and cats cannot process. Second Street Animal Hospital, citing the ASPCA, warns that chocolate — especially dark and baking chocolate — can be dangerous for cats and dogs, and that xylitol, a sweetener used in sugar-free candy and gum, can cause serious problems.

Wrappers are a danger of their own. A dog that swallows foil or plastic wrapping can suffer gut irritation or a blockage, even if the sweet inside was harmless. Keep the candy bowl high, closed, and behind a door, not on a low table where a curious dog can reach it during the doorbell chaos. Hand out sweets from a bowl you control, and count the bowl back inside when trick-or-treating ends. Children coming home with bags of sweets need a safe place for the haul — a high shelf or a closed cupboard — because a dropped bag on the floor is an emergency waiting for a dog's nose.

If a pet does eat candy, do not wait for symptoms. Call a vet at once, or the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center on (888) 426-4435, the number Second Street Animal Hospital publishes in its guide. Note what was eaten, how much, and when, and keep the wrapper. Fast advice saves lives, and the call costs far less than the emergency visit it prevents.

## Secure cats indoors, especially black cats

Cats belong indoors on Halloween night, and black cats most of all. PETA's guidance is direct: secure cats indoors, because black cats in particular can be targeted by cruel pranksters on Halloween. The risk is real enough that shelters often pause black cat adoptions around the holiday, and the precaution costs nothing: keep every cat inside from the afternoon of 31 October until the morning after.

Indoor cats need their own protection too. An open front door, held for a group of trick-or-treaters, is an escape route. Put cats in a closed room before the first doorbell rings, with food, water, a litter tray, and a familiar bed. A cat that bolts through an open door into a street full of noise, costumes, and fireworks may not find its way home, and a frightened cat runs far and hides deep.

Identification matters even for indoor cats. Make sure collars and tags are on, and that microchip details are up to date, because the one night a cat is most likely to slip out is the night you most want it back quickly. PETA's advice pairs the two: a quiet room and up-to-date identification, so that even if the worst happens, the way home is marked.

## Give dogs a quiet room away from the door

Dogs experience Halloween as an invasion. The doorbell rings, the door opens, strangers in strange shapes shout, and the dog's territory is breached every two minutes for hours. PETA advises keeping dogs away from the door entirely, and Second Street agrees: keep all but the most social pets in a separate room during trick-or-treating, with identification and microchip details current.

Set the room up before the evening begins. A familiar bed, water, a chew or a food puzzle, and a radio or television playing soothing music or ordinary programmes will mask the doorbell and the voices. PETA suggests soothing music or a television for exactly this reason: the steady background sound turns the sharp noises of the night into a blur the dog can sleep through. Check on the dog between waves of visitors, and keep the routine calm. A dog that is fed, settled, and undisturbed will usually sleep through the whole event.

Do not take dogs trick-or-treating. PETA is explicit on this: the crowds, the costumes, the noise, and the sweets dropped on pavements make the streets a stressful and dangerous place for a dog on Halloween night. The walk can wait for a quiet morning. The dog will not miss the sweets, and it will miss the stress even less.

## Costumes: leave them to the humans

A dog in a pumpkin suit is a popular photograph, and a popular source of misery for the dog wearing it. PETA's advice is blunt: leave costumes to humans, because costumes can stress animals and restrict their movement, and small pieces can become choking hazards. An animal that freezes, flattens its ears, tucks its tail, or tries to chew the costume off is not enjoying the joke. It is asking to be released.

If a costume is truly wanted, Second Street's vet guidance sets the conditions: it must not limit the animal's movement, sight, or ability to breathe or bark, and it must have no dangling or chewable pieces. A simple bandana, worn briefly for a photograph and then removed, is the compromise PETA suggests, and it is the most an animal should be asked to wear. Watch the animal's body language the whole time, and remove the costume at the first sign of distress. A photograph is never worth an animal's fear.

Never dye or paint an animal's fur for Halloween, and never use human makeup on a pet's skin. Animals groom themselves by licking, and substances safe on human skin can be harmful when swallowed. The animal's own coat is costume enough, and it comes without risk.

## Decorations that trap, burn, and poison

Halloween decorations are designed to be strange and startling, and to an animal they are hazards with no warning labels. PETA warns against fake spiderwebs in particular: loose, stringy webs can trap birds and small wildlife outdoors, and can entangle a curious pet indoors. Skip the fake webs, or keep them high, tight, and out of reach of any animal, and remove them promptly after the holiday.

Flames are the next danger. Lit candles, jack-o'-lanterns, and open flames invite burns, and a wagging tail or a curious kitten can knock a pumpkin over in a second. Second Street notes that kittens have been burned by lit pumpkins, and both guides agree on the fix: use flameless candles and battery lights instead of real flames, and keep all lights and cords out of reach. Glow sticks deserve the same caution: their contents are not meant to be chewed, and a punctured glow stick can sicken a pet that bites it.

Cords, string, and small decorations complete the list. Keep electrical cords hidden or covered, keep small ornaments off low tables, and treat every decoration as something a pet will sniff, chew, or knock over. If it cannot survive a dog's nose or a cat's paw, it belongs out of reach or not in the house at all.

## Watch for fear, and know when to call for help

Fear looks different in every animal. A dog may pant, pace, tremble, hide, or bark without stopping. A cat may vanish under a bed and refuse food. A rabbit or small pet may thump, freeze, or stop eating. All of these are the animal saying the night is too much, and the answer is always the same: less noise, less light, less company, and a safe hiding place that nobody invades.

Prepare the safe place before the evening, keep the routine as normal as possible, and do not force a frightened animal to socialise or pose for photographs. Comfort is welcome, but chasing a hiding animal to cuddle it adds stress. Sit nearby, speak quietly, and let the animal choose whether to come out.

Know the emergency numbers before the night begins. Your own vet's out-of-hours number, and the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center on (888) 426-4435, should be saved in a phone before the first trick-or-treater arrives. Emergencies on Halloween happen after hours, in the dark, while the house is full of guests. The number saved in advance is the number that gets used in time.

## A calm night for every member of the house

Halloween can be a happy night for pets as well as people, but only if the humans plan for it. Candy locked away, cats secured indoors, dogs settled in a quiet room with music, no stressful costumes, flameless lights, and a phone with the vet's number saved. None of it is difficult. All of it is the difference between a pet that sleeps through the noise and a pet that spends the night in fear, or worse, in a vet's emergency room.

The animals in a house did not choose Halloween, and they cannot choose to stay safe. That choice belongs to the people who love them. Make it early, make it completely, and the whole household — two-legged and four-legged alike — gets to enjoy the night.

## The morning after: closing the night properly

Halloween safety does not end when the last trick-or-treater leaves. Before bed, gather every sweet bag into a closed cupboard, check that no candy fell behind furniture or under tables, and put the candy bowl away. Remove decorations promptly, especially fake webs that can trap wildlife outdoors and small ornaments a pet could swallow. Check the quiet room's occupant: a dog or cat that hid all evening should come out for water and a calm check, and any animal that is still trembling or refusing food in the morning deserves a vet's call. Save the vet's number and the poison control number in the phone permanently, not just for one night. Next Halloween, the preparations will take ten minutes instead of an hour, because the safe places, the numbers, and the habits will already exist.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is Halloween candy dangerous for dogs and cats?**

Yes, chocolate is dangerous for both cats and dogs, and dark and baking chocolate are the most dangerous, while xylitol in sugar-free candy and gum can cause serious problems. Wrappers are dangerous too, because foil or plastic can irritate the gut or cause a blockage. Keep all candy high, closed, and behind a door, and call a vet or the ASPCA Poison Control Center on (888) 426-4435 at once if a pet eats any.

**Should cats stay indoors on Halloween?**

Yes, all cats should be kept indoors from the afternoon of 31 October until the morning after, and black cats especially, because PETA warns that black cats can be targeted by cruel pranksters on Halloween. Keep cats in a closed room with food, water, a litter tray, and a bed before the doorbell starts ringing, so an open front door cannot become an escape route into a noisy street.

**Can I take my dog trick-or-treating?**

No, PETA advises against it, because the crowds, costumes, noise, and sweets dropped on pavements make the streets stressful and dangerous for a dog on Halloween night. Keep all but the most social pets in a separate room during trick-or-treating, with a familiar bed, water, a chew, and soothing music or television to mask the doorbell. The walk can wait for a quiet morning.

**Is it safe to dress my pet in a Halloween costume?**

Costumes can stress animals, restrict their movement, and carry choking hazards, so PETA advises leaving costumes to humans. If a costume is used, it must not limit movement, sight, breathing, or barking, and must have no dangling or chewable pieces; a simple bandana worn briefly for a photograph is the safest compromise. Remove any costume at the first sign of distress, and never dye fur or use human makeup on a pet.

**What Halloween decorations are dangerous for pets?**

Fake spiderwebs can trap wildlife and entangle pets, lit candles and jack-o'-lanterns can burn or be knocked over, glow sticks can sicken a pet that chews one, and cords and small ornaments can be chewed or swallowed. Use flameless candles and battery lights, keep webs high or skip them, keep cords covered and out of reach, and remove decorations promptly after the holiday.
