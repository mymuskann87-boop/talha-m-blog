Feel the engine slow down under your feet. The boat rocks on grey Atlantic water, and there it is, close enough to touch, or so you think: a green ridge of rock and forest rising out of the sea, about 33 kilometres off the coast of São Paulo, Brazil. A lighthouse stands on top. Birds circle the trees. It looks like the opening scene of a holiday film.

Then the captain cuts the engine completely, and nobody reaches for a rope, an anchor bag or a picnic basket. Your boat has travelled as far as it is allowed to travel. Because the pretty island in front of you is Ilha da Queimada Grande, known around the world as Snake Island, and landing on it is forbidden.

## Why your boat stops here

No ticket exists for this place. No tour company can sell you a walk on that shore, and no fisherman is allowed to drop you off for a quick look around. Brazil closed the island to the public, and the only people permitted to set foot on it are Brazilian Navy personnel and a small number of selected researchers whose visits are approved in advance by ICMBio, the Chico Mendes Institute for Biodiversity Conservation, the federal body that administers the island.

Even those rare research landings run under strict safety rules. Reports of sanctioned visits describe tiny teams, official waivers to collect data, and a doctor travelling with the group, because the nearest hospital is a boat ride away and a serious bite cannot wait. The navy, which looks after the automated lighthouse, patrols the waters as well. So you stay on the deck, and the island stays where it has always stayed: near enough to see, too dangerous to touch.

Ask yourself why a government guards an empty island so carefully. The answer lives in the trees in front of you, and it is watching the same birds you are watching.

## Who actually lives here

The island covers just 43 hectares, roughly 60 football fields of rock, grass and rainforest. On paper, its population is zero. No families, no farmers, no keepers. Yet the place is crowded, because nearly every snake you would meet here belongs to one species that exists nowhere else on the planet: the golden lancehead pit viper, scientific name *Bothrops insularis*.

Picture it from the deck through binoculars, the way researchers describe it. A slim snake, usually around 70 centimetres long, coloured in pale gold and yellow-brown tones that melt into dead leaves and sunlit branches. It climbs. It waits in low trees and shrubs. It is, by every serious account, a patient hunter built for one job, and the job is not troubling you. Golden lanceheads here feed mostly on migratory birds that stop on the island during their long seasonal flights, since the island holds almost no ground animals worth hunting.

One smaller neighbour shares the island: Sauvage's snail-eating snake, a harmless, non-venomous species that minds its own business among the leaves. Everything else you have heard about this place, the swarms, the carpets of scales, the instant death at every step, needs a calmer look. Starting with the number you have definitely heard before.

## The number everyone gets wrong

You have probably read the famous claim: one snake for every square metre of Snake Island. Repeat that across the whole island and you would get a frightening figure, and indeed, older guesses once put the population at around 430,000 snakes. It makes a wonderful headline. It is not what scientists found when they finally counted properly.

The first systematic study of the golden lancehead population put the real total at roughly 2,000 to 4,000 snakes, concentrated almost entirely in the rainforest part of the island. That is still an astonishing crowd for 43 hectares, and inside the densest forest patches, a snake in a given square metre is believable. But as a description of the whole island, the famous claim stretches a local patch into a total, and the total it produces is more than a hundred times too big.

Hold on to that correction, because it changes the whole story. A place with 430,000 snakes sounds unstoppable. A place with perhaps 3,000 snakes, all of one species, all in one small forest, sounds like something that could vanish. You are looking at the second kind of place.

## A prisoner of the rising sea

Now rewind your imagination about 11,000 years. The green ridge in front of your boat is not an island yet. It is a hill on the edge of the mainland, and snakes move freely between the hill and the forests of Brazil. Then the last ice age loosens its grip, the seas climb, and the water closes over the low land behind the hill. The hill becomes an island. The snakes on it become prisoners.

Generation after generation, the trapped snakes changed. With no mammals to hunt on the ground, they turned their attention upward, to the birds passing through. That single change in the menu explains the weapon they carry today. Most venomous snakes can afford to bite prey, release it, and follow the scent trail until the venom finishes the job. A bird gives you no such luxury. A bitten bird that stays in the air for even a minute is a meal lost forever. So the golden lancehead's venom evolved to work extremely fast, striking the prey's body almost at once, and by many accounts it is several times more potent than the venom of its closest mainland relatives.

I find that logic quietly amazing: the deadliest thing about this island is not cruelty or excess, it is a dinner problem that evolution solved too well.

## Venom that can kill, and venom that heals

Respect the danger, though, because it is real. Golden lancehead venom attacks blood and tissue at speed. Accounts of its effects list kidney failure, internal bleeding and tissue death around the bite, and the venom is reported to be able to kill within an hour. Roughly seven percent of people bitten by this snake have died, according to reporting on the species, which is why no sane captain lets a passenger swim for that shore.

Then comes the twist you would never guess from the deck of your boat. The same family of snake chemistry that endangers life has also saved millions of them. One of the world's most widely used blood-pressure medicines, captopril, was designed from bradykinin-potentiating peptides found in the venom of the Brazilian pit viper *Bothrops jararaca*. Be precise here, because writers often blur the point: the jararaca is a mainland cousin of the golden lancehead, not the island snake itself. Credit belongs to the wider lancehead family, and to the Brazilian scientist Sérgio Ferreira, whose work on that venom in the 1960s opened the door. Researchers are now studying lancehead venoms for possible treatments for heart disease and blood clots as well.

So the forest in front of you is a pharmacy and a hazard in the same body, and that is a large part of why scientists beg for permits to study it rather than simply seal it off and forget it.

## The stories you should not swallow whole

From your boat, the lighthouse invites a story, and the coast of Brazil is happy to supply one. Local tales say that snakes slipped through the windows of the lighthouse keeper's home one night in the early twentieth century and killed him and his family, after which the light was automated and the island emptied. It is a chilling tale. It is also, in the words of careful reporting, a legend. What records support is more ordinary: a lighthouse was built in 1909, keepers once lived on the island, and the last human residents left after the light was automated in the 1920s.

The same caution covers the buried pirate treasure that storytellers love to hide in the island's caves, and the lone fisherman found dead in his boat after picking bananas ashore. These tales travel from video to video because they are exciting, not because documents stand behind them. Enjoy them the way you would enjoy a ghost story on deck at night, and then let them go. The verified facts, a species found nowhere else, trapped by a rising sea, armed with medicine-grade venom, are strange enough without decoration.

## Why a forbidden island still needs guarding

Here is the final turn of your trip, and it flips everything you assumed on the way out. The golden lancehead is officially critically endangered. Its numbers are believed to be falling, pushed down by habitat damage, disease, the inbreeding that haunts any small isolated population, and illegal collectors who risk the forbidden landing because a single rare snake can be sold on the black market. The animal that keeps the world away with its venom cannot keep away the few people willing to steal it.

That, in the end, is why your boat stops where it stops. The ban protects visitors from the snakes, certainly. But more and more, it protects the snakes from us: from disturbance, from traffickers, from the simple pressure of human feet in a forest that exists only once, in one place, on Earth. Some places do not need a gate and a ticket office. Some places need a locked door and a captain who cuts the engine early. In my view, Snake Island is the clearest example on the planet of a place we serve best by never arriving.

**Can tourists visit Snake Island?**
No. The island is closed to the public. Only Brazilian Navy personnel and selected researchers with approval from ICMBio, Brazil's federal conservation institute, may land, and research visits follow strict safety rules.

The first careful scientific count estimated 2,000 to 4,000 golden lanceheads, mostly in the rainforest area. The older claim of one snake on every square metre of the whole island is a myth when applied to the island as a whole.

**What does the golden lancehead eat?**
Mainly migratory birds that stop on the island during their seasonal journeys. With almost no ground prey available, the snakes became specialist bird hunters, which is why their venom acts so fast.

There is no solid record of it. Keepers did live on the island after the lighthouse was built in 1909, and they left once the light was automated. The story of the family killed by snakes is told as a legend.
