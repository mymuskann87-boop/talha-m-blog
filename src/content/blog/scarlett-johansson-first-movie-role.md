Every major star has a first step, and the interesting ones are rarely glamorous. Scarlett Johansson's first step was a small role in a film that flopped, played when she was nine years old, after years of auditioning for commercials and a stage debut she had already made at eight. There was no red carpet, no breakout, and no sign of the Marvel superhero or the Oscar nominations to come. There was a child who wanted to act, parents who supported her, and a long apprenticeship in small parts that taught her the craft before fame ever arrived.

The facts of that apprenticeship are recorded by Encyclopaedia Britannica, which traces Johansson's path from her first roles to her breakthrough in 2003. The story is worth telling carefully, because searchers asking about her first movie usually want two answers: the literal first film, and the first film that mattered. ## A New York childhood built for acting

Scarlett Johansson was born on 22 November 1984 in New York City, the daughter of an architect and a producer, and the youngest of four siblings with her twin brother Hunter. She was raised in New York City, and she developed an interest in acting at a young age, auditioning for commercials by the age of seven. Britannica records those details as the starting point of a career that began in childhood and never paused.

The training followed quickly. Soon after the commercial auditions, she enrolled at the Lee Strasberg Theatre Institute, and later attended the Professional Children's School in Manhattan to continue honing her craft. These were not casual classes. The Lee Strasberg Institute teaches the method tradition of acting, and the Professional Children's School is built for young performers balancing school with professional work. Johansson was being trained as an actor while still a child, and the training shows in the steadiness of her earliest screen work.

Her stage debut came before her film debut. At the age of eight, she appeared alongside Ethan Hawke in the Off-Broadway production *Sophistry*. A stage debut at eight, opposite an established film actor, is a serious beginning: live theatre forgives nothing, and a child who can hold a stage at eight has already learned the discipline that film sets demand.

## North: the film debut at nine

At nine years old, Johansson made her film debut in *North*, released in 1994, directed by Rob Reiner. Britannica states the fact plainly: at nine she made her film debut in Rob Reiner's *North* (1994). The role was small — she played Laura Nelson — and the film itself was a notorious failure, criticised widely on release and remembered now mostly as a curiosity. None of that mattered to the nine-year-old in it. A film debut is a film debut, and it put her on a set, in front of a camera, doing the job she had trained for.

The year matters for the question searchers ask. *North* in 1994 is the answer to "what was Scarlett Johansson's first movie," and the age matters too: nine years old, a year after her stage debut, and two years after she began auditioning for commercials. The path was orderly and unhurried — commercials, stage, small film role — and it was built by a family that treated acting as a craft to learn, not a lottery to win.

The film's failure also taught an early lesson that served her well: a bad film does not end a career. Young actors are often defined by their first credit, and Johansson's first credit was in a flop. She kept working, kept training, and the flop became a footnote rather than a verdict. That resilience is the real story of the early years.

## Just Cause and the small parts that followed

After *North*, Johansson appeared in *Just Cause* in 1995, a thriller starring Sean Connery. Again the role was small, and again the film did little for her career directly. But the pattern of the years 1994 to 1996 is the pattern of a working child actor: small parts in real productions, alongside major stars, learning the rhythms of a film set and building a resume credit by credit.

These are the years that separate a child star from a child actor. A child star is famous young and learns the craft later, if at all. A child actor works young and learns the craft first, and the fame, if it comes, arrives on top of a foundation. Johansson's foundation was built in these small parts: showing up, knowing lines, hitting marks, and watching how Connery, Hawke, and Reiner worked.

The discipline of those years explains the confidence of what came next. When the first real opportunity arrived, Johansson was not a beginner hoping for luck. She was a twelve-year-old with three years of professional experience, stage training, and a film set's worth of patience.

## Manny & Lo: the first breakthrough

The first breakthrough came with *Manny & Lo* in 1996, a coming-of-age film about orphaned sisters who run away. Britannica calls it her first breakthrough, and the description fits: Johansson played Manny, one of the two sisters, in a leading role that carried the film. She was eleven or twelve during production, playing a character with weight, grief, and humour, and the performance was noticed.

The film's subject matter was serious — two girls, one pregnant, surviving on their own — and the role asked for emotional range that small parts never require. Johansson delivered it, and the independent film world took note. A breakthrough in an independent film does not bring fame, but it brings the next thing that matters more: the attention of directors who cast the films that do.

*Manny & Lo* is the answer to the second question searchers ask: not the first film, but the first film that showed what she could do. Every actor has both answers, and the distance between them — two years, from *North* to *Manny & Lo* — measures the apprenticeship. Johansson served hers quickly, and she served it well.

## The Horse Whisperer and Ghost World

The next step was critical acclaim. In *The Horse Whisperer* (1998), Johansson played a teenager who has suffered a disfiguring accident, opposite Robert Redford, who also directed. Britannica records that she earned critical acclaim for the role, and the film's scale — a major studio production, a beloved director, a leading part — moved her from the independent world into the mainstream's view.

She was thirteen or fourteen, playing trauma, recovery, and a difficult relationship with a horse and a whisperer, in a film that asked for stillness and subtlety. The performance held its own against Redford, and the acclaim it earned made her a known young actor rather than a promising one. The difference is subtle and important: promise is potential, and acclaim is proof.

In 2001, she gained further attention as Rebecca, the cynical teen outcast in *Ghost World*, a film that became a cult favourite. The role was the opposite of the wounded teenager in *The Horse Whisperer*: sharp, funny, and detached, a character who saw through the adult world and said so. The range between the two performances — vulnerability and cynicism, played three years apart — showed a young actor with unusual breadth, and the industry noticed again.

## Lost in Translation: the adult breakthrough

Johansson came into her own playing adult roles in 2003, with two films released in the same year. In Sofia Coppola's *Lost in Translation*, she played Charlotte, a young woman bored with her life and her marriage, who forms a complicated relationship with a middle-aged man played by Bill Murray, in Tokyo. In *Girl with a Pearl Earring*, she played Griet, a housemaid who bewitches the painter Johannes Vermeer, played by Colin Firth. Britannica names both as the roles in which she came into her own as an adult actor.

She was eighteen, playing adults, and the performances carried a maturity that surprised audiences who still thought of her as a child actor. *Lost in Translation* in particular became a defining film: quiet, funny, melancholy, and carried by Johansson's ability to say little and mean much. The child actor had become a leading actor, and the apprenticeship that began with commercials at seven had reached its destination.

The years after 2003 built the career the world knows: Woody Allen's *Match Point* in 2005, her first big-budget action film *The Island* in the same year, and in 2010 her debut as Natasha Romanoff, the Black Widow, in *Iron Man 2*, a role she carried through eight Marvel films to *Black Widow* in 2021. In 2019, she earned her first Academy Award nominations, for *Jojo Rabbit* and *Marriage Story*. All of it traces back to a nine-year-old in a failed film, who kept working.

## What the early career teaches

Johansson's early career is a lesson in patience. The debut was small, the next parts were small, and the breakthroughs came in steps — *Manny & Lo*, then *The Horse Whisperer*, then *Ghost World*, then the double arrival of 2003. No single moment made her. The accumulation did, built on training, family support, and a willingness to take small parts seriously.

For young actors and their parents, the lesson is practical: train first, work small, and let the craft grow faster than the fame. For audiences, the lesson is to look past the first credit. A film debut is a beginning, not a verdict, and the most interesting careers are the ones where the beginning is the least interesting part. Johansson's beginning was a flop at nine. The career that followed is the answer to anyone who judges a star by a first step.

## Awards, the stage, and the years after

The adult career brought honours the child career never promised. In 2010, Johansson made her Broadway debut in *A View from the Bridge* and won a Tony Award for best featured actress. In 2019, she earned her first Academy Award nominations, for supporting actress in *Jojo Rabbit* and lead actress in *Marriage Story*. She returned to blockbuster work with *Jurassic World Rebirth* in 2025, and in the same year made her directorial debut with *Eleanor the Great*, about a 94-year-old woman played by June Squibb. The range of those credits — stage award, Oscar nominations, blockbuster lead, first film as a director — is the harvest of the apprenticeship that began at seven. A career built slowly, on training and small parts, can grow in directions a fast fame never reaches.
