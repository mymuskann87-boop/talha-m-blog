Forests are often called the lungs of the Earth, a phrase that is memorable but not quite accurate. Forests do produce oxygen through photosynthesis, but most of the oxygen we breathe comes from the long history of photosynthesis in oceans and on land, stored in the atmosphere over millions of years. What forests do, powerfully and urgently, is store carbon, influence rainfall, cool the land, protect soils and water, and shelter an extraordinary share of the planet's species. When forests are healthy, they slow climate change. When they are cleared or degraded, they release carbon and lose those services, often quickly.

The climate story of forests is not only about planting trees. It is about keeping existing forests standing, because an old forest stores far more carbon than a young plantation of the same area, and about restoring degraded land with the right trees in the right places. It is also about understanding limits: forests alone cannot solve climate change while fossil fuel emissions continue to rise, and planting trees in the wrong ecosystem, such as a natural grassland, can do harm. This guide explains the mechanisms clearly, separates helpful claims from exaggerated ones, and looks at what individuals, communities, and countries can actually do.

The scale is worth grasping first. Forests cover roughly one third of the world's land area, and they hold a large share of carbon stored on land, in trees, roots, dead wood, leaf litter, and especially soil. Tropical forests, boreal forests in the far north, and temperate forests each work differently, and each faces different threats, from agricultural expansion and logging to fire, drought, pests, and warming itself. A global view must therefore be combined with local understanding, because a solution that works in one forest type may fail in another.

## How Trees Store Carbon and Slow Warming

Trees store carbon through photosynthesis. Leaves absorb carbon dioxide from the air, use sunlight energy to combine it with water, and produce sugars that build wood, leaves, and roots, while releasing oxygen as a by-product. As long as the tree lives and grows, much of that carbon stays locked in its trunk, branches, and roots. When leaves fall, some carbon enters the soil, where it can remain for decades or centuries if the soil is undisturbed. This is why a forest is a carbon store as well as a carbon absorber: it holds carbon already captured, not only the carbon it captures each year.

The distinction between storing and absorbing matters for policy. A mature forest may absorb relatively little extra carbon each year, because growth is balanced by decay, but it stores an enormous amount that would be released if it were cleared or burned. A young, fast-growing plantation absorbs carbon quickly, but stores far less at first, and if it is harvested after a few years, much of that carbon returns to the air. Protecting an old forest therefore prevents a large, immediate emission, while planting new trees creates a slower, future benefit that depends on the trees surviving for decades. Both matter, but they are not interchangeable.

Soil carbon is often overlooked, yet it is crucial, especially in boreal forests and peatlands. In cold northern forests, decomposition is slow, so carbon accumulates in soil and peat over thousands of years. Disturbing that soil, by draining peat, ploughing, or severe fire, can release carbon that took millennia to store. In tropical forests, most carbon is in living trees rather than soil, so clearing and burning releases it rapidly as carbon dioxide. In both cases, the lesson is the same: the carbon store is fragile, and once released, it cannot be quickly recaptured by replanting alone.

Forests also affect climate through the carbon cycle's timing. When a forest is cleared for farming, the carbon is released over years as wood decays or burns, while the farm that replaces it stores far less carbon. When a forest regrows, carbon is recaptured slowly, over decades, and the full store may take a century or more to return. This time lag is why preventing deforestation is often described as more urgent than planting new trees: avoided emissions happen now, while regrowth benefits arrive later, and the climate responds to cumulative emissions over time.

## Forests, Rainfall, and the Water Cycle

Forests do more than store carbon; they help make rain. Trees draw water from soil through their roots and release it as water vapour from their leaves, a process called transpiration. In large forests such as the Amazon, this recycled moisture contributes substantially to rainfall downwind, so that rain falling in one part of the forest may have been transpired by trees hundreds of kilometres away. Clearing forest can therefore reduce rainfall locally and downwind, making the remaining forest drier, more fire-prone, and less able to sustain itself, a feedback loop that worries scientists studying tropical deforestation.

At smaller scales, forests influence local weather by cooling the air through evaporation, by roughening the surface so winds mix differently, and by releasing tiny particles that can help cloud droplets form. These effects vary by forest type and by season, and they are harder to measure than carbon storage, but field studies and models consistently show that large-scale forest loss tends to warm and dry the local climate, even before global warming is considered. A deforested landscape often becomes hotter in the day, cooler at night, and more variable in rainfall, which harms crops as well as wildlife.

Forests also protect water quality and reduce flooding. Tree roots hold soil in place, leaf litter slows rain as it hits the ground, and forest soil acts like a sponge, absorbing water and releasing it slowly into streams. When forest is replaced by bare soil or concrete, rain runs off faster, carrying soil into rivers, increasing flood peaks, and reducing dry-season flows. Watershed protection is therefore a climate adaptation benefit as well as a conservation benefit: healthy forests make communities more resilient to both floods and droughts, even though they cannot prevent extreme weather entirely.

It is important to be honest about limits here. Forests influence rainfall, but they do not control it. Ocean temperatures, global wind patterns, and topography also matter greatly, and planting a small woodland will not guarantee rain for a farm. The strongest rainfall effects come from large, intact forest landscapes, which is another reason to protect existing forests rather than assuming that scattered tree planting can replace them. Local benefits are real, but they scale with the size and health of the forest.

## Cooling, Albedo, and Why Location Matters

Forests cool the land surface through shade and evaporation, which is why walking into a forest on a hot day feels noticeably cooler than standing in an open field. This local cooling benefits people, livestock, and crops, and urban trees are widely used to reduce heat in cities, where shade and transpiration can lower street temperatures by several degrees. In rural landscapes, forest patches can protect crops and water sources from heat stress, and shelterbelts can reduce wind erosion and evaporation from fields.

There is, however, a counter-effect called albedo, which is how much sunlight a surface reflects. Dark forest canopies absorb more sunlight than snow or light-coloured grassland, so in snowy northern regions, expanding forest onto open snow-covered land can warm the climate overall, even though the trees store carbon, because the darker surface absorbs more solar energy. In the tropics, by contrast, forest expansion generally cools the climate, because the carbon and rainfall benefits outweigh the albedo effect, and because tropical forests are already dark and humid. In temperate regions, the balance is intermediate and depends on the specific site.

This is why blanket claims such as planting trees anywhere always cools the planet are misleading. The right tree in the right place, in a tropical or temperate area where forest naturally grows, usually helps. Planting dense dark conifers on snowy tundra or on natural grassland that is already a valuable ecosystem may not help and may harm biodiversity. Restoration should therefore start from the question of what ecosystem belongs there, not from a target number of trees. Natural regeneration, where native forest regrows on its own with protection from grazing and fire, often outperforms plantations in biodiversity and carbon storage over time, although it can be slower at first.

Fire complicates the picture further. As climates warm, many forests face more frequent and severe fires, which release stored carbon and can convert forest to shrubland or grassland. Managing fire risk through fuel reduction, prescribed burning where appropriate, and protecting forest edges is therefore part of climate strategy, not a separate issue. A forest that burns severely may still regrow, but repeated severe fires can prevent recovery and turn a carbon store into a carbon source, a shift that has been observed in some boreal and tropical regions.

## Deforestation, Degradation, and What Is Lost

Deforestation, the permanent conversion of forest to another land use such as farming, mining, or urban expansion, is the most visible threat, but degradation, the gradual loss of forest quality without full clearing, is also serious. Selective logging, fuelwood collection, grazing, road building, and repeated small fires can thin a forest, reduce its carbon store, fragment wildlife habitat, and make it more vulnerable to further damage, even if it still looks green from above. Satellite monitoring now distinguishes these processes better than before, but degradation remains harder to track than outright clearing.

The drivers of deforestation vary by region. In tropical areas, commercial agriculture for commodities such as beef, soy, palm oil, and timber is a major driver, often linked to global supply chains. In other regions, smallholder farming, charcoal production, and infrastructure expansion play larger roles. No single policy can address all drivers, which is why effective forest protection combines law enforcement, land rights for local and Indigenous communities, sustainable livelihoods, supply-chain transparency, and restoration of degraded land so that farming does not need to expand into intact forest.

The biodiversity cost of forest loss is inseparable from the climate cost. Forests shelter more than half of the world's land species, many found nowhere else, and fragmentation isolates populations, reduces genetic diversity, and increases extinction risk. A forest that loses its species also loses functions: pollinators, seed dispersers, predators that control pests, and fungi that help trees absorb nutrients. Climate and biodiversity are therefore not separate crises to be solved in sequence; protecting a forest addresses both at once, while a plantation of a single fast-growing species addresses neither fully.

It is also important to recognise that forest loss is not only a tropical issue. Boreal forests face logging, mining, and fire, temperate forests face urban expansion and pests, and mangrove forests, which store large amounts of carbon in their soils and protect coasts from storms, have been cleared for aquaculture and development in many countries. A global forest strategy must include all these types, with attention to the communities who live in and depend on them.

## Restoration, Protection, and Honest Expectations

Restoration can take many forms, from protecting natural regrowth to planting native species, restoring wetlands and mangroves, and improving management of working forests. The most successful projects share common features: they use native species suited to the site, they involve local people in planning and benefit-sharing, they protect the area from grazing, fire, and further clearing for long enough for trees to establish, and they monitor survival rather than counting only seedlings planted. A project that plants a million trees but loses most of them within a year has achieved little, while a project that protects natural regrowth over a decade may achieve far more with less fanfare.

Protection of existing forests is usually the highest priority. This includes legal protected areas, Indigenous territories, community forests, and sustainable forestry that maintains canopy cover and soil. Evidence consistently shows that forests managed by Indigenous peoples and local communities, with secure land rights, often have lower deforestation rates than similar forests without such rights. Supporting those rights is therefore both a human rights issue and a climate strategy, and it is more effective than imposing protection from outside without local consent.

Individual actions also matter, but they should be kept in proportion. Reducing consumption of products linked to deforestation, choosing certified timber and paper where credible certification exists, eating in ways that reduce pressure on forest land, and supporting reputable restoration projects can all help. Planting a tree is a meaningful symbolic and ecological act, but it does not cancel out a large carbon footprint on its own, and offset claims should be treated with scepticism unless they are verified, additional, and long-lasting. The largest climate benefit of forests comes from systemic protection and from reducing fossil fuel emissions alongside forest action, not from tree planting as a substitute for emission cuts.

Finally, expectations about forests and climate should be realistic. Forests are a vital part of the solution, but they are not a magic solution that allows emissions to continue unchanged. Scientists estimate that forest protection and restoration could provide a substantial share of the emission reductions needed, but only if combined with rapid cuts in fossil fuels, changes in agriculture, and protection of other ecosystems such as peatlands and grasslands. Forests buy time and reduce harm; they do not remove the need to transform energy and food systems.

## Mangroves, Peatlands, and Forests Beyond Trees

When people think of forests, they often picture tall trees on dry land, but some of the most carbon-rich forest ecosystems grow in water or on waterlogged soil. Mangrove forests, which grow along tropical and subtropical coasts, store large amounts of carbon in their muddy soils, protect shorelines from storm surges and erosion, provide nurseries for fish and crabs, and support coastal livelihoods. Because mangrove soils are waterlogged and low in oxygen, decomposition is slow, so carbon accumulates over centuries. Clearing mangroves for aquaculture, development, or fuelwood releases that soil carbon and removes storm protection at the same time, a double loss that is hard to reverse quickly.

Peatland forests, especially in Southeast Asia and in northern bogs, are similarly important. Peat is partially decomposed plant material that accumulates in waterlogged conditions, forming a carbon store that can be many metres deep and thousands of years old. Draining peat for agriculture or plantations exposes it to air, causing it to decompose and release carbon dioxide, and dried peat becomes highly flammable, leading to severe fires that release even more carbon and cause dangerous air pollution. Protecting peatlands, by keeping them wet and avoiding drainage, is therefore a high-priority climate action, even though peatlands cover a relatively small area compared with other forests.

Urban forests and agroforestry, where trees are integrated into farms and cities, deserve mention as well. Urban trees cool streets, filter air, support mental health, and manage stormwater, while agroforestry, such as shade trees over coffee or cocoa, or shelterbelts around fields, can improve soil, provide income diversity, and store carbon without taking land out of farming entirely. These systems store less carbon per hectare than intact tropical forest, but they bring benefits directly to people where they live and work, and they show that forest action is not only about remote wilderness. A complete forest strategy therefore includes wilderness protection, restoration, and trees in human landscapes, each with its own role.

## My Take:

My honest opinion is that forests teach a lesson about value that our economy often misses: the most valuable thing a forest does may be the thing it does quietly, every day, without a price tag. It stores carbon, makes rain, cools air, holds soil, and shelters life, all at once, without needing to be harvested or converted to be useful. When we clear a forest for a single product, we trade that bundle of services for one commodity, and we usually regret the trade later, when floods, droughts, or soil loss arrive. I think we should judge forest decisions by the full bundle, not by the price of timber or farmland alone.

I also believe the distinction between protecting old forests and planting new ones should be taught far more widely. Planting trees is photogenic and easy to celebrate, while protecting an existing forest is less visible and harder to turn into a campaign slogan, yet protection is usually more urgent and more effective in the short term. I would like to see every tree-planting campaign paired with a protection commitment, so that new planting does not become an excuse for continued clearing elsewhere. A net gain in tree numbers means little if old, carbon-rich, biodiverse forest is replaced by young, single-species plantations.

On restoration, my view is that local knowledge matters more than global targets. A community that knows which native species grow well, where water flows, and how grazing and fire behave will design a better project than an outside organisation with a generic planting list. I think funding should follow that knowledge, with long-term support for monitoring and maintenance, rather than short-term funding that rewards planting numbers and disappears before trees mature. A forest is a decades-long commitment, and our funding should reflect that timescale.

I am also cautious about carbon offset claims involving forests. Offsets can be legitimate when they are real, additional, verified, and permanent, but many have failed on one or more of those tests, because forests burn, are cleared later, or would have regrown anyway. I think individuals and companies should treat forest offsets as a supplement to cutting their own emissions, never as a replacement, and should demand transparent evidence about survival, land rights, and long-term protection. Without that scrutiny, offsets risk becoming a way to feel good while emissions continue.

Finally, I believe forests offer hope, but not easy hope. The hope is that protecting and restoring forests is a solution we already understand, with benefits for climate, water, wildlife, and people, and that it can start immediately, without waiting for a new technology. The hard part is that it requires restraint: leaving some forests alone, changing how we farm and consume, and respecting the rights of people who live in forests. That restraint is difficult in a world that rewards expansion, but it is also the most human part of the solution, because it asks us to value a living system for what it is, not only for what we can take from it.

I also think forest policy should be judged by outcomes on the ground, not by pledges on paper. Many countries and companies have promised to end deforestation or to plant billions of trees, but satellite data often shows clearing continuing, sometimes in the same landscapes where pledges were made. I think independent monitoring, transparent reporting, and accountability for supply chains are essential, because without them, pledges become a substitute for action rather than a step toward it. Consumers and voters can help by asking for evidence: is deforestation actually falling in this region, are local communities benefiting, and are planted trees surviving after five years?

Another angle is the time dimension of forest benefits, which is often compressed in popular discussion. A newly planted forest will not store as much carbon as an old forest for decades, and a forest protected today prevents emissions today, while a forest planted today delivers most benefits in the future. Climate policy must therefore balance present and future: protect now to avoid immediate emissions, and plant now to build future stores, while cutting fossil fuel emissions now to reduce the warming that threatens both. Thinking in decades, not in news cycles, is essential for forest strategy, because trees operate on a timescale far longer than political terms.

I also want to highlight the role of Indigenous knowledge in forest management. Indigenous peoples have managed forests for millennia through practices such as controlled burning, selective harvesting, and protection of sacred groves, and modern science increasingly recognises the value of that knowledge for biodiversity and fire management. Supporting Indigenous land rights and incorporating traditional knowledge, with consent and benefit-sharing, is not only a matter of justice; it is a practical strategy that has been shown to reduce deforestation. I think forest projects that ignore local knowledge are likely to fail, while those that respect it are likely to endure.

Finally, I believe forests remind us that climate action is not only about technology, but about relationships: with land, with other species, and with future generations. A forest is a relationship between trees, soil, water, wildlife, and people, sustained over centuries, and breaking that relationship for short-term gain has costs that extend far beyond carbon. Restoring forests is therefore also about restoring relationships, by giving land time to heal, by respecting the people who live there, and by accepting that some benefits cannot be rushed. That slower, relational view may be less exciting than a technological fix, but it is, I think, more honest and more durable.

One last thought: every forest decision is also a decision about the future, because trees planted or protected today will shape climate, water, and wildlife for decades after the people who made the decision are gone. That long horizon should encourage humility and care, rather than quick fixes or short-term profit, and it should remind us that the forests we enjoy today were protected, often unknowingly, by previous generations. Passing on healthy forests is a responsibility, not just an opportunity, and it begins with the simple choice to value a standing forest for all it does.

Keep that lesson in mind whenever a new claim or trend appears: check the evidence, consider who benefits from the claim, and choose the option that is safe, sustainable, and suited to your own situation rather than the loudest option online.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do forests really produce most of our oxygen?
No, not directly. Forests produce oxygen through photosynthesis, but most atmospheric oxygen accumulated over millions of years, with a large contribution from ocean photosynthesis. Forests are more important for carbon storage, rainfall, cooling, and biodiversity than as a short-term oxygen supply.

### Is planting trees enough to stop climate change?
No. Tree planting helps, but it cannot offset continued fossil fuel emissions on its own. Protecting existing forests, restoring degraded land with native species, and rapidly cutting emissions are all needed together.

### Why is protecting an old forest better than planting a new one?
An old forest already stores a large amount of carbon in trees and soil, which would be released if cleared. A new plantation stores little at first and takes decades to build a comparable store, and only if it survives and is not harvested quickly.

### How do forests affect rainfall?
Trees release water vapour through transpiration, which contributes to cloud formation and rainfall, especially in large forests. Clearing forest can reduce local and downwind rainfall, making the remaining forest drier and more fire-prone.

### Can planting trees ever harm the climate?
Yes, in the wrong place. Planting dark trees on snowy land can reduce reflectivity and warm the climate, and planting on natural grasslands can harm biodiversity. Restoration should use native species in ecosystems where forest naturally belongs.

### What is the difference between deforestation and degradation?
Deforestation is permanent conversion of forest to another use, such as farming. Degradation is a gradual loss of forest quality from logging, fire, grazing, or fragmentation, without full clearing, but it still reduces carbon storage and wildlife habitat.
