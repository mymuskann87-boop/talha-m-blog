Distance is a hard test for love. Human relationships rely on regular, physical closeness: a hand held, a meal shared, a walk taken together without planning it. Take that closeness away, and even a strong relationship can feel thin, like a voice on a phone that is always about to cut out. Yet love over a long distance is becoming more common, and many couples make it work, not by pretending distance does not matter, but by working at closeness in new ways.

This post sets out practical advice for making a long-distance relationship work, following the five tips in Cupla's guide, rewritten here in plain words, with extra advice on trust, honesty and planning. None of it is magic. All of it takes effort from both people, on purpose, again and again.

## Why Distance Feels So Hard

The first step is to admit the difficulty honestly. Modern technology has made staying in contact easier than ever, with messages, photos and video calls available at any hour. Yet a screen cannot replace sitting on the same sofa. Cupla notes that about three million Americans live apart from their spouse at some point in their marriage, and that 75 percent of college students have engaged in a long-distance relationship. Distance is normal. Struggling with it is normal too.

Couples go long distance for many reasons: work, money, family, health or study. The reason matters less than the feeling it creates. Both people miss the small, unplanned moments: cooking together, walking to the shops, falling asleep in the same room. A long-distance relationship must find ways to replace those moments, or at least to keep the connection alive until they return.

## Agree On A Shared Endgame

The foundation of all long-distance advice is a shared endgame. Long distance is easier when both people know the reasons for the separation and, more importantly, share an expectation of when they will be back together physically. That expectation does not need a fixed date on day one, but it does need honesty. Are you apart for six months of study? For a work contract with an end date? Until a visa arrives? Both people should be able to say the answer in the same words.

Have the conversation explicitly, and keep having it. Priorities and plans change, and without the ability to check in casually on the sofa, it is important to set aside specific time to talk about the big questions: why you are apart, whether it is still working, and what the next step is. Honest and open discussions, even hard ones, are far more effective than assumptions and guessing about what a partner wants. A shared endgame gives hard days a purpose. Without one, distance can feel endless, and endless distance wears love down.

## Share The Small Daily Moments

Technology has replaced letters and weekly phone calls with instant contact, and that is a gift long-distance couples should use fully. Texts, photos and video calls make it possible to share even the smallest parts of a day. Cupla's advice is to keep sharing those small parts on purpose. The minutiae matter more over distance than most people expect.

Send the photo of your lunch. Send a short video of the street on your way home. Send a long, detailed message about a boring meeting, a funny stranger, a song you heard. By sharing these details, you make sure your partner feels involved in your life even when they are far away. A relationship is built from hundreds of small moments, not from a few grand gestures. In a long-distance relationship, those moments must be sent, because they cannot be witnessed.

## Read The Same Book, Hear The Same Song

A useful bonus tip from Cupla is to find ways to share an activity across the distance. If your partner is reading a book, try reading it too, or suggest one to read together. If they are listening to a particular playlist, put on your headphones and listen to it as well, then talk about it afterwards. Watch the same film on the same night. Cook the same recipe in two kitchens and compare results.

These shared activities weave two lives together even when the people in them are apart. They give you something to talk about beyond "how was your day", and they create memories that belong to both of you, not to one life or the other. It is good advice for any relationship, but over long distance it is especially effective, because it turns separate evenings into a shared one.

## Keep Having Date Nights

Distance does not have to end date nights. It only changes them. Pick a classic date night, dinner and a movie, and do it over a video call. Choose a film, start it at the same time, order food from a chain restaurant you are both near, and share the evening as if you were in the same room. It is not the same as being together, and no one should pretend it is. But it is a shared experience, and shared experiences are what keep a couple feeling like a couple.

If films are not your style, try a video call date with a set of deep questions for couples, or a game you can both play online, or a walk together while talking on the phone, each in your own neighbourhood. The form matters less than the habit. A regular date night, kept faithfully, says: you are still my priority, even when my calendar is full and my bed is empty on your side.

## Do Not Find Time, Make Time

This is the advice couples find hardest, and the advice that matters most. Making the effort to create time together is important in any relationship, but over long distance it can feel even harder to find a time that works for both people, especially across time zones, work shifts and study hours.

The simple solution, in Cupla's words, is: do not find time, make time. Be intentional about responding when your partner reaches out. Those scheduled late-night chats before bed become even more important when they are one of the only ways to connect, and being left on read can be hard to swallow at a distance. Be deliberate in small ways: if your partner has an important day coming up, put it in your calendar and ask how it went. If they mention something that excites or worries them, make a note and bring it up later. If you promised a date night, keep the promise. Reliability builds trust faster than words.

## Build Trust With Reliability, Not Promises

Trust is the quiet centre of a long-distance relationship. Without it, every unanswered message becomes a worry, and every late reply becomes a story. Trust grows through honest, open communication, including the hard conversations, and through reliability in small things. Do what you said you would do. Call when you said you would call. Remember what mattered to your partner, and follow up.

Jealousy and suspicion grow in silence. The cure is not constant checking, which exhausts both people, but steady honesty. Say where you are going and when you will be busy. Say when you feel lonely, bored or tempted to withdraw. Say when something is wrong, early, before it grows. A partner who hears the truth regularly has little reason to imagine the worst. A partner who hears nothing will imagine it anyway.

## Talk About Money, Visits And The Hard Parts

Long distance costs money, time and patience, and couples who do not talk about those costs honestly will argue about them instead. Visits need flights or train tickets, time off work, and places to stay. Calls need good connections and quiet hours. Gifts need postage. None of this is romantic, but all of it is real, and a budget for visits, agreed by both people, prevents resentment.

Talk about the hard parts too. Loneliness. Boredom. The friend who does not understand why you stay. The family member who asks when it will end. The fear that you are growing apart, or growing in different directions. These conversations are uncomfortable, and they are necessary. A relationship that can only discuss happy things will not survive the unhappy ones, and distance brings unhappy ones regularly.

## Make Plans For The Grand Return

Planning time together can be almost as enjoyable as the time itself, and over long distance, anticipation counts for a lot. Discuss what you will do when you see each other, and make real, concrete plans, with dates, times and activities ready to go. That planning has two benefits. It means less time planning and more time being present when you are finally together. And it lets each person learn what the other would genuinely enjoy.

Some people like to return to favourite restaurants and familiar places. Others want to try all new things, so the reunion feels fresh. Many like a mix of both. Whatever you choose, make the plans together, keep them, and let the excitement glow through the weeks apart. A date on the calendar is a promise with a shape, and it gives both people something to count down to.

## Know When To Ask For Help, Or To Let Go

Not every long-distance relationship should continue, and honesty includes admitting that. If the shared endgame keeps moving further away, if calls feel like a duty rather than a joy, if trust has broken and cannot be rebuilt, or if one person has stopped making time, it may be time for a painful, respectful conversation about ending. Staying together out of habit, guilt or fear is not loyalty. It is delay.

Ask for help when you need it. Talk to a trusted friend, a counsellor, or a couples therapist who understands distance. There is no shame in needing support for a hard situation. And remember that a relationship that ends well, with kindness and honesty, is not a failure. It is two people choosing the truth over the comfort of pretending. The advice in this post is for couples who both want to try. Trying requires two people, fully, and no advice can replace the second one.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How can you make a long distance relationship work?**

Making a long distance relationship work starts with a shared endgame: agreeing honestly on why you are apart and when you expect to be back together physically, and revisiting that conversation regularly as plans change. Keep sharing small daily moments through texts, photos and video calls, keep regular virtual date nights, make time deliberately rather than waiting to find it, and make concrete plans for reunions. Reliability in small promises, honest communication about hard topics, and a shared plan for the future matter more than grand gestures.

**How do you build trust in a long distance relationship?**

Trust grows through honest, open communication, including hard conversations, and through reliability in small things rather than through promises alone. Do what you said you would do, call when you said you would call, remember your partner's important events and follow up on them, and say where you are going and when you will be busy. Jealousy grows in silence, so steady honesty about loneliness, worries and daily life gives a partner little reason to imagine the worst, while constant checking only exhausts both people.

**What are the best long distance date night ideas?**

The best long distance date nights recreate a shared experience, such as dinner and a film watched at the same time over a video call, with food ordered from a chain restaurant you are both near. Other ideas include a video call date with deep conversation questions, a game played online together, a walk in two neighbourhoods while talking on the phone, cooking the same recipe in two kitchens, or reading the same book and discussing it. The form matters less than the habit: a regular date night kept faithfully says that your partner remains a priority even across distance and busy schedules.

**How often should long distance couples talk?**

There is no single right number, because couples differ in work, study, time zones and need for space, and the quality of contact matters more than the quantity. What matters is agreeing together on a rhythm that suits you both, keeping scheduled calls and date nights reliably, and responding intentionally when your partner reaches out, since scheduled chats become especially important when they are one of the only ways to connect. If one person feels lonely or the other feels crowded, talk about it honestly and adjust the rhythm together, rather than letting resentment build in silence.

**When should a long distance relationship end?**

A long distance relationship may need to end if the shared endgame keeps moving further away with no honest plan, if calls feel like a duty rather than a joy, if trust has broken and cannot be rebuilt, or if one person has stopped making time and effort. Staying together out of habit, guilt or fear is not loyalty, and a painful, respectful conversation about ending can be kinder than pretending. Couples in difficulty should consider talking to a trusted friend or counsellor, and remember that a relationship that ends with kindness and honesty is not a failure.
