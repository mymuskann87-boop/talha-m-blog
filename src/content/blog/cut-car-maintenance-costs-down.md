The notebook in the glove box had a page for every visit to the workshop. Date, mileage, what was done, what it cost. The owner was not a mechanic. He was a schoolteacher who had learned, one expensive lesson at a time, that a car kept on schedule costs less than a car repaired in panic.

The most expensive car repair he ever paid for started as a small noise he ignored for three months. By the time the car stopped on the road, the small noise had become a large bill, a tow truck, and a week without transport. The notebook began the day after that bill. It has saved him money ever since — not by making him clever with engines, but by making him regular with habits.

That is the real secret of car maintenance costs. They are not controlled by one brilliant decision. They are controlled by small, boring, repeated ones.

## The service schedule is cheaper than the repair

Every car comes with a service schedule: oil changes, filter changes, and inspections at set distances or times. It is tempting to stretch those intervals when money is tight. The oil looks fine. The car sounds fine. Another thousand kilometres will not hurt.

Sometimes it does hurt. Engine oil breaks down with heat and time, and old oil stops protecting the engine properly. A missed service rarely announces itself loudly. It shows up months later as wear that cannot be undone, and wear is always priced higher than oil.

Think of servicing as rent for reliability. For example, if a routine service costs 8,000 and an engine repair caused by neglect costs 80,000, the service was not an expense — it was a bargain. Those are example figures, not prices for your car, but the ratio tells the truth: prevention is almost always the cheaper line in the notebook.

Keep the schedule written down where you will see it — the glove box notebook, a phone reminder, a sticker on the windscreen. When the date or distance arrives, go. Regularity is the whole method.

A missed service also weakens your position if you ever sell the car. Buyers trust a stamped book and a tidy notebook far more than a promise that the car was looked after. That trust shows up in the price they are willing to pay. So the notebook does two jobs at once: it keeps the car healthy while you own it, and it protects the value when you hand it over.

## Small checks between services

You do not need to be a mechanic to catch problems early. Three simple checks, done once a month, take ten minutes and prevent a surprising number of bills.

First, tyre pressure. Soft tyres wear out faster, grip worse, and drink more fuel. A pressure gauge costs little, and most fuel stations have air pumps. Check the pressure when the tyres are cold, and match the number in the car's manual or on the driver's door frame — not a guess.

Second, engine oil level. Pull the dipstick, wipe it, dip it again, and read it. Oil below the minimum mark means the engine is running with less protection than it needs. Topping up early is cheap. Running dry is not.

Third, coolant level and any new puddles under the car. A small, steady leak caught early is a hose or a clamp. The same leak ignored becomes an overheated engine on a hot day, and overheating is one of the fastest ways to turn a small bill into a large one.

Write the results in the notebook. A slow fall in oil or coolant over weeks tells a story that one check alone cannot. The notebook turns guesses into patterns.

## Two quotes before a big repair

Small jobs — a wiper blade, a bulb, a top-up — need no ceremony. Big repairs are different. When a workshop names a large figure for brakes, suspension, clutch, or anything involving opening the engine, pause before agreeing.

In my view, the second quote is the cheapest tool in car ownership, because it costs nothing but an hour and it has saved more money than any gadget I have ever bought for a car.

Take the car, or at least the written diagnosis, to one other trusted workshop and ask for their price for the same job. Prices differ for honest reasons — parts quality, labour rates, workshop rent — and sometimes for less honest ones. A second quote tells you which kind you are facing. It also tells you whether the repair is truly urgent or can be planned for next month, which changes everything about how you pay for it.

Ask both workshops the same question: what happens if I wait a month? Some repairs cannot wait — brakes, steering, anything safety-related. Others can, and a repair you can plan is a repair you can save for, instead of borrowing for.

## Fix small problems early

A worn wiper blade costs little and takes minutes. The same blade, left through a rainy season, scratches the windscreen, and a windscreen costs many times more. A small coolant leak is a clamp. An ignored coolant leak is an engine.

Walk around the car once a week and look with fresh eyes. A tyre wearing more on one edge. A light that stopped working. A belt with a crack. A sound that was not there last month. Each one is a small problem sending a polite letter. Answering the letter is cheap. Ignoring it until it shouts is expensive.

This habit costs nothing but attention. It is the same attention the schoolteacher's notebook taught him: notice early, write it down, fix it while it is small.

## Gentle driving saves parts

The way a car is driven is also a maintenance cost, paid in wear. Hard braking wears brake pads faster. Racing away from every signal wears tyres and stresses the engine. Speed over broken roads punishes the suspension.

Gentle driving is not slow driving. It is smooth driving: braking early and softly, accelerating steadily, slowing down for bad patches instead of crashing through them. The parts last longer, the fuel lasts longer, and the car spends fewer days in the workshop. Over a year, the difference is visible in the notebook — fewer brake jobs, fewer tyre changes, fewer surprises.

## Work that belongs to professionals

Some work is not for the driveway, and pretending otherwise is the most expensive mistake of all. Engine internals, brakes, steering, airbags, and anything involving fuel lines or air conditioning gas belong to qualified mechanics with proper tools. A wrong repair on a safety system does not just cost money — it can cost safety.

The rule is simple: checks and small jobs you understand are yours. Anything safety-related, anything you do not fully understand, and anything that needs special tools goes to a professional. Saving money never means gambling with brakes.

## Common questions

### How do car maintenance costs stay low over a year?

Follow the service schedule without stretching it, do small monthly checks on tyres, oil, and coolant, fix small problems early, and drive smoothly. No single trick matters as much as regularity — the notebook habit of writing down every check and every cost turns car care from guessing into planning.

### Is it worth getting a second quote for a car repair?

For any big repair, yes. Prices differ between workshops for honest reasons, and a second quote also tells you whether the job is truly urgent or can be planned. The hour it costs can save a large amount, and it gives you a calmer basis for deciding.

### Which car checks can a non-mechanic safely do?

Tyre pressure, engine oil level, coolant level, lights, wipers, and a weekly walk-around look for leaks, wear, and new noises are all safe for an owner. Anything involving brakes, steering, airbags, fuel lines, or engine internals belongs to a qualified mechanic.

### Does gentle driving really reduce maintenance costs?

Yes, because hard braking, fast acceleration, and rough-road speeding wear out brake pads, tyres, suspension, and engine parts faster. Smooth driving extends the life of those parts, which means fewer replacements over the year and fewer workshop visits.

### What car problem should never be ignored?

Anything safety-related: brakes that feel soft or noisy, steering that pulls or shakes, and any warning light for brakes or engine temperature. These should be checked immediately by a professional, because waiting turns a repair bill into a safety risk.
