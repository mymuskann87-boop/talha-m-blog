Almost everyone gets a headache at some point, and most headaches are not dangerous, but they can still disrupt work, sleep, and daily life. A headache is not a single condition; it is a symptom with many possible causes, from muscle tension and dehydration to migraine, sinus pressure, eye strain, lack of sleep, and, rarely, serious conditions affecting the brain or blood vessels. Understanding the common patterns helps you respond sensibly: when rest, water, and simple measures are enough, when a pattern deserves medical review, and when urgent care is needed.

The brain itself does not feel pain in the way skin does, because it has few pain receptors. Headache pain comes mainly from structures around the brain: blood vessels, membranes covering the brain, muscles of the head, neck, and scalp, nerves, and sinuses. When those structures are irritated, inflamed, stretched, or under tension, they send pain signals that we experience as a headache. Different causes irritate different structures in different ways, which is why headaches vary in location, quality, duration, and accompanying symptoms.

This guide explains common causes in plain language, with general information only, not medical advice. It covers tension-type headaches, migraine, cluster headaches, sinus-related pain, dehydration and lifestyle triggers, medication-related headaches, and warning signs that require urgent assessment. It does not attempt to diagnose individual readers, and it encourages medical review for frequent, severe, or changing headaches, rather than self-treatment with increasing doses of painkillers.

## Tension-Type Headaches: The Most Common Kind

Tension-type headaches are the most common form, and most people will experience them at some point. They are often described as a dull, pressing, or tightening band around the head, on both sides, with mild to moderate intensity that does not usually prevent daily activities, although it can be distracting and tiring. Unlike migraine, tension headaches usually do not cause nausea, vomiting, or severe sensitivity to light and sound, although mild sensitivity can occur. They may last from 30 minutes to several days, with episodic forms occurring occasionally and chronic forms occurring on many days per month.

The causes are not fully understood, but muscle tension, stress, poor posture, lack of sleep, eye strain, and jaw clenching are commonly associated. Prolonged sitting with forward head posture, especially at a screen, can strain neck and scalp muscles, while stress can increase muscle tension and lower pain thresholds. Dehydration, skipped meals, and caffeine changes can also contribute. The term tension does not mean the headache is purely psychological or that the person is simply stressed; it refers to a pattern of muscle and pain-processing involvement that is real and physical, even when stress is a trigger.

Management for occasional tension headaches often involves rest, hydration, gentle movement, heat or cold applied to the neck and shoulders, and, if appropriate, simple over-the-counter pain relief taken as directed and not excessively. Regular physical activity, good sleep, posture breaks, and stress management can reduce frequency over time. Keeping a simple headache diary, noting timing, triggers, sleep, food, and medication, can reveal patterns that are not obvious day to day, such as headaches that always follow long screen sessions or poor sleep.

Frequent tension headaches deserve attention. If headaches occur on 15 or more days per month, they are considered chronic, and they may be linked to medication overuse, mood problems, sleep disorders, or other conditions that need assessment. Increasing painkiller use to cope with frequent headaches can itself cause headaches, a cycle discussed below, so frequent headaches should prompt a clinician review rather than escalating self-medication. A clinician can also check for other causes and discuss preventive strategies, including lifestyle changes and, where appropriate, preventive medication.

## Migraine: More Than a Bad Headache

Migraine is a neurological condition, not simply a severe tension headache, and it affects a substantial share of the population, with women affected more often than men. Migraine attacks typically involve moderate to severe throbbing pain, often on one side of the head, although it can be on both sides or shift sides, lasting from a few hours to up to three days if untreated. Attacks are often accompanied by nausea, vomiting, and sensitivity to light, sound, and smells, and routine physical activity usually worsens the pain, so people often need to rest in a dark, quiet room.

About a quarter of people with migraine experience aura, temporary neurological symptoms before or during the headache, most commonly visual disturbances such as flashing lights, zigzag lines, blind spots, or tingling in the face or hands, or difficulty speaking. Aura usually develops gradually over minutes and lasts less than an hour, and it can be frightening if it occurs for the first time, because it overlaps with symptoms of stroke. New aura, aura lasting longer than an hour, or aura with weakness should be assessed urgently, because other conditions must be excluded, even in people with known migraine.

Migraine triggers vary widely and are highly individual. Commonly reported triggers include stress and stress let-down after a stressful period, lack of or excess sleep, skipped meals, dehydration, certain foods or alcohol, especially red wine for some people, strong smells, bright or flickering lights, weather changes, and hormonal changes around menstruation. Triggers rarely act alone; they often combine, so that a poor night's sleep plus a skipped meal plus stress may trigger an attack when none alone would. Identifying personal triggers through a diary can help, but overly restrictive avoidance of many foods or activities can reduce quality of life without clear benefit, so trigger management should be balanced and individualised.

Migraine treatment has two parts: acute treatment to stop an attack, and preventive treatment to reduce frequency when attacks are frequent or disabling. Acute options range from simple painkillers and anti-nausea medicines to migraine-specific medicines such as triptans, prescribed by a clinician, and newer preventive options include medicines originally developed for other conditions as well as newer migraine-specific treatments. Treatment choices depend on attack frequency, severity, other health conditions, and response to previous treatments, so they should be discussed with a clinician rather than copied from another person's prescription. Early treatment, taken at the start of an attack, is often more effective than waiting until pain is severe.

## Other Common Causes: Sinuses, Eyes, Dehydration, and More

Sinus headaches are often overdiagnosed. True sinus headaches occur with sinus inflammation, usually during a cold or sinus infection, and involve pressure or pain in the forehead, cheeks, or around the eyes, with nasal congestion, discharge, and sometimes fever. However, many headaches attributed to sinuses are actually migraine, because migraine can also cause nasal congestion, facial pressure, and pain around the eyes. A key distinction is that sinus headaches usually accompany clear nasal symptoms and infection, while migraine often involves nausea, light sensitivity, and throbbing pain that worsens with activity. If facial pain is persistent, one-sided, or accompanied by fever and coloured discharge, medical assessment is appropriate.

Eye strain, sometimes called digital eye strain, can cause headaches after prolonged close work, especially with uncorrected vision problems, poor lighting, glare, or infrequent blinking during screen use. The headache is often around the forehead or eyes, with tired, dry, or irritated eyes, and it improves with rest, blinking, and looking into the distance. Regular eye checks, correct glasses or contact lenses, adequate lighting, and the 20-20-20 rule, looking at something 20 feet away for 20 seconds every 20 minutes, can help. Eye strain does not usually cause severe, persistent headaches, so headaches that do not improve with rest and eye care deserve further assessment.

Dehydration is a common, preventable contributor. Even mild dehydration can cause headache, fatigue, and difficulty concentrating, especially in heat, after exercise, vomiting, diarrhoea, or alcohol use. Drinking water regularly, rather than waiting until very thirsty, and replacing fluids after sweating or illness, can prevent many dehydration headaches. Hunger and low blood sugar can also trigger headaches, particularly when meals are skipped, so regular, balanced meals matter, especially for people prone to migraine. Caffeine is double-edged: small amounts can help some headaches and are included in some painkillers, but sudden withdrawal after regular high intake can cause headaches, and excessive caffeine can worsen sleep and anxiety, which in turn worsen headaches.

Other causes include jaw problems, such as teeth grinding or jaw joint disorders, which can cause pain around the temples and jaw, neck problems, including muscle strain or cervical spine issues, which can refer pain to the head, and hangover headaches, caused by alcohol's effects on dehydration, sleep, and blood vessels. Hormonal headaches, including menstrual migraine, occur in relation to hormonal changes and may need specific management. Each of these causes has its own pattern, and a headache diary that notes jaw pain, neck stiffness, sleep, alcohol, and menstrual timing can help a clinician distinguish them.

## Medication Overuse and When Headaches Become a Cycle

One of the most important and least known causes of frequent headaches is medication overuse, sometimes called rebound headache. When simple painkillers, or migraine-specific medicines, are taken too frequently, often on 10 to 15 or more days per month depending on the medicine, they can paradoxically cause headaches as their effect wears off, leading to more medication use and more headaches. The headache pattern is often daily or near-daily, dull, and present on waking, improving briefly with medication and returning as it wears off. This cycle can develop gradually, without the person realising that the treatment has become part of the problem.

Breaking the cycle usually requires reducing or stopping the overused medicine under clinical supervision, which may temporarily worsen headaches for days to weeks before improvement occurs. Preventive strategies, lifestyle measures, and, where appropriate, preventive medicines can support this process. The key message is that more painkillers are not always better, and that frequent use should be discussed with a clinician or pharmacist, who can advise on safe frequency, alternative treatments, and a tapering plan if needed. Never stop prescribed medicines suddenly without advice, and never exceed recommended doses in an attempt to control frequent headaches.

Other medical causes of headache, although less common, must be kept in mind. These include high blood pressure that is severely elevated, infections such as meningitis or encephalitis, head injury, bleeding in or around the brain, tumours, and increased pressure inside the skull. These conditions usually have additional features, such as fever, neck stiffness, confusion, weakness, vision changes, vomiting, or a headache that is sudden, severe, or unlike any previous headache, which are warning signs discussed below. The rarity of these causes should not cause panic about every headache, but it does mean that unusual patterns should not be ignored.

Sleep disorders also contribute to headaches. Obstructive sleep apnoea, in which breathing repeatedly pauses during sleep, often causes morning headaches, daytime sleepiness, and snoring, and it is more common in people with obesity or certain anatomical features. Poor sleep quality, insomnia, and irregular sleep schedules can also increase headache frequency. Addressing sleep, through regular sleep times, a dark, quiet bedroom, limiting screens before bed, and assessment for apnoea where indicated, can reduce headaches for many people, and it is often overlooked in favour of focusing only on painkillers.

## Warning Signs: When to Seek Urgent Care

Most headaches are not emergencies, but certain features require urgent medical assessment, and knowing them can save lives. Seek emergency care for a sudden, severe headache that reaches maximum intensity within seconds to a minute, often described as a thunderclap headache, which can indicate bleeding around the brain. Seek urgent care for a headache after head injury, especially with vomiting, confusion, drowsiness, weakness, or unequal pupils. Seek urgent care for headache with fever, neck stiffness, rash, confusion, or sensitivity to light that suggests meningitis, and for headache with weakness, numbness, speech difficulty, vision loss, or confusion that could indicate stroke.

Other warning signs include a new headache in someone over 50, especially with scalp tenderness, jaw pain, or vision changes, which can indicate giant cell arteritis, an inflammation of blood vessels that needs prompt treatment to prevent vision loss. A headache that is progressively worsening over weeks, that wakes a person from sleep regularly, that changes pattern dramatically, or that is accompanied by vomiting, seizures, personality change, or swelling of the optic disc on eye examination, should also be assessed promptly. Headaches in pregnancy, especially with high blood pressure, swelling, or visual changes, require urgent assessment for pre-eclampsia.

A useful rule is: if a headache is unlike any you have had before, if it is the worst headache of your life, if it does not improve as expected, or if it comes with new neurological symptoms, do not wait and do not rely on internet advice. Seek medical care and describe the pattern clearly, including onset, location, quality, duration, triggers, associated symptoms, and medicines taken. Early assessment allows serious causes to be excluded and appropriate treatment to be started, and it provides reassurance when the cause is benign.

For non-urgent but frequent headaches, a routine clinician appointment is still important. Bring a headache diary, a list of medicines including over-the-counter products and supplements, and notes about sleep, stress, diet, and triggers. A clinician can examine you, review medicines, check blood pressure and vision where relevant, and decide whether further tests, such as blood tests or brain imaging, are needed. Most frequent headaches do not require brain imaging, and imaging is usually reserved for warning signs or unusual patterns, so a decision not to scan is often appropriate and should not be taken as dismissal.

## Headaches in Children, Older Adults, and Pregnancy

Headaches affect different age groups differently, and beginners should understand those differences. Children can get tension headaches and migraine, but they may describe symptoms differently, with shorter attacks, pain on both sides more often, and prominent nausea, vomiting, or abdominal pain, sometimes called abdominal migraine, without a clear headache. Children may also have difficulty explaining aura or sensitivity, so parents and clinicians should listen carefully to behaviour changes, such as avoiding light, lying down, or missing school, rather than expecting adult descriptions. Persistent, severe, or changing headaches in children, especially with vomiting, vision changes, or neurological symptoms, require prompt medical assessment, because serious causes, although rare, must be excluded.

Older adults face a different pattern: new headaches after age 50 are more likely to have a secondary cause, such as giant cell arteritis, medication effects, or other medical conditions, and they should be assessed more readily than similar headaches in younger adults. Older adults also take more medicines, increasing the risk of medication-overuse headaches and drug interactions, and they may have other conditions, such as high blood pressure or cervical spine problems, that contribute to headaches. A new headache in an older adult, especially with scalp tenderness, jaw pain, vision changes, or systemic symptoms such as fever or weight loss, should not be dismissed as tension without assessment.

Pregnancy changes headache management significantly, because many medicines are restricted, hormonal changes affect migraine patterns, and new headaches can signal pre-eclampsia, a serious pregnancy condition involving high blood pressure and organ effects. Migraine often improves during pregnancy for some people, but it can worsen for others, and any new, severe, or persistent headache in pregnancy, especially with high blood pressure, swelling, visual changes, or upper abdominal pain, requires urgent assessment. Pregnant people should discuss headache treatment, including safe pain relief and preventive strategies, with their clinician or midwife, rather than self-medicating, and they should seek care promptly for warning signs rather than waiting.

## My Take:

My honest opinion is that headaches are often treated too casually, both by sufferers and by those around them, as if they were a minor inconvenience to be powered through with painkillers. In reality, a headache is a signal from a complex system involving muscles, blood vessels, nerves, sleep, hormones, and mood, and it deserves the same careful attention as pain elsewhere in the body. I think we should take headaches seriously without panicking about them: track them, look for patterns, treat them sensibly, and seek help when they are frequent, severe, or changing, rather than normalising daily pain.

I also believe medication overuse is one of the most under-discussed causes of chronic headache, and it deserves far more public attention. Many people with daily headaches do not realise that the painkillers they take to cope may be perpetuating the cycle, and they increase doses in good faith, only to feel worse. I think pharmacists and clinicians should routinely ask about painkiller frequency when someone reports frequent headaches, and that public health messages should include the simple rule: if you need painkillers for headaches on many days each month, talk to a clinician, rather than simply buying more. That conversation could break a cycle that years of self-medication cannot.

On migraine, my view is that it is still misunderstood and undertreated, partly because it is invisible and partly because it is dismissed as just a headache. Migraine can be disabling, affecting work, family life, and mental health, and it deserves proper diagnosis and treatment, including preventive options when attacks are frequent. I think workplaces and schools should treat migraine as a legitimate health condition, with quiet rest space and flexible arrangements during attacks, rather than expecting people to work through severe pain and nausea. Compassion and accommodation cost little and help a great deal.

I am also struck by how much headache prevention depends on ordinary habits: sleep, hydration, regular meals, movement, posture breaks, and stress management. These habits are not glamorous, and they do not offer the instant relief of a pill, but over weeks they reduce frequency for many people, and they improve general health at the same time. I would encourage readers to invest in those habits before seeking exotic treatments, because they address several triggers at once, and because they are sustainable in a way that daily high-dose painkillers are not.

Finally, I believe knowing warning signs is an essential life skill, not just medical trivia. A thunderclap headache, a headache with weakness or speech difficulty, or a headache with fever and neck stiffness are rare, but recognising them and seeking emergency care promptly can prevent serious harm. I think everyone should know those signs, just as they know the signs of a heart attack or stroke, because early action matters. Most headaches will be benign and manageable, but the ability to distinguish the rare emergency from the common ache is what makes headache knowledge truly valuable.

I also think headache research deserves more funding and attention, because migraine alone affects hundreds of millions of people and causes enormous disability and economic loss, yet it is often underfunded compared with conditions of similar burden. New treatments, including migraine-specific preventive medicines and neuromodulation devices, offer hope, but access varies by country and cost, and many people still rely on older, less effective treatments or on no treatment at all. I think raising awareness of migraine as a neurological condition, not as a minor headache, is a step toward better funding, better workplace accommodation, and better care, and that step starts with how we talk about headaches in everyday life.

Another angle is the stigma around headaches, especially migraine, which are invisible and episodic, so sufferers may be disbelieved at work or school, accused of exaggerating, or expected to work through attacks that would be recognised as disabling if they were visible injuries. That stigma can lead people to hide their condition, delay treatment, and overuse painkillers in secret, worsening outcomes. I believe workplaces and schools should adopt clear policies for migraine and frequent headaches, including rest space, flexible deadlines during attacks, and understanding from managers and teachers, because accommodation is not special treatment; it is fair treatment for a real condition.

I also want to reflect on the role of self-tracking in headache management. A simple diary, whether on paper or in an app, can reveal triggers, patterns, and treatment responses that memory alone misses, and it gives clinicians valuable information for diagnosis and treatment decisions. However, tracking can become obsessive, increasing anxiety about every food, weather change, or sleep variation, and leading to overly restrictive lifestyles. I think tracking should be time-limited and purposeful: track for a few weeks to identify patterns, then use those patterns to make a few targeted changes, rather than tracking indefinitely and fearing every potential trigger. Balance, again, is the key.

Finally, I believe the most important message about headaches is to trust patterns over single events, and to seek help early when patterns change. A single headache after a long, stressful day is usually nothing to worry about, but a headache that becomes more frequent, more severe, different in character, or accompanied by new symptoms deserves attention, even if previous headaches were benign. That principle, noticing change rather than fearing every ache, applies to many health symptoms, and it empowers people to act wisely without living in fear. Headaches are common, usually manageable, and rarely dangerous, but they are worth understanding, tracking, and, when needed, investigating properly.

One last thought: the most empowering headache habit is simple tracking combined with timely help, noting patterns for a few weeks, acting on obvious triggers such as sleep and hydration, and seeking medical review when headaches become frequent, severe, or different. That approach respects both the common, manageable nature of most headaches and the rare, serious causes that must not be missed. With that balance, headaches become less frightening and more understandable, and you gain a practical plan rather than a cycle of worry and painkillers.

Keep that lesson in mind whenever a new claim or trend appears: check the evidence, consider who benefits from the claim, and choose the option that is safe, sustainable, and suited to your own situation rather than the loudest option online.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What causes most everyday headaches?
Most everyday headaches are tension-type headaches, linked to muscle tension, stress, poor posture, lack of sleep, eye strain, dehydration, or skipped meals. Migraine is another common cause, with throbbing pain, nausea, and light sensitivity, while sinus, dehydration, and medication overuse headaches are also frequent.

### How can I tell a tension headache from a migraine?
Tension headaches are usually dull, pressing, on both sides, mild to moderate, and do not prevent activity or cause nausea. Migraine is usually moderate to severe, throbbing, often one-sided, with nausea, light and sound sensitivity, and worsening with activity, sometimes with visual aura before the pain.

### When is a headache dangerous and needs emergency care?
Seek emergency care for a sudden severe thunderclap headache, headache after head injury, headache with fever and neck stiffness, headache with weakness, numbness, speech or vision problems, or a new severe headache unlike any before. These can indicate bleeding, infection, stroke, or other serious conditions.

### Can taking painkillers too often cause headaches?
Yes. Taking painkillers or migraine medicines too frequently, often on 10 to 15 or more days a month depending on the medicine, can cause medication-overuse headaches, with near-daily dull pain that improves briefly with medicine and returns as it wears off. Frequent use should be discussed with a clinician.

### What simple habits help prevent headaches?
Regular sleep, adequate water, regular meals, posture and screen breaks, physical activity, stress management, and limiting alcohol and excessive caffeine help many people. A headache diary can reveal personal triggers, such as poor sleep or long screen sessions, that can then be addressed.

### Should I get a brain scan for frequent headaches?
Not always. Brain imaging is usually reserved for warning signs, unusual patterns, new headaches over age 50, or other concerning features. A clinician can decide based on examination and history, and a decision not to scan is often appropriate for typical tension or migraine patterns.
