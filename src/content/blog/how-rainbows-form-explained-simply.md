A rainbow looks like a painted bridge in the sky, but it is not an object you could touch or walk to. It is light, bent and split by millions of raindrops, seen by an observer standing in exactly the right place. That is why two people standing apart see slightly different rainbows, and why a rainbow seems to move when you move. It is not a thing in the sky; it is an optical effect created by sunlight, water, and your eye working together. Once you understand the three ingredients, sun behind you, rain in front of you, and a low sun angle, rainbows stop being mysterious and become predictable, which makes spotting one even more enjoyable.

Sunlight looks white, but it contains all the colours of the visible spectrum. A raindrop acts like a tiny prism, bending light and splitting it into its colours. When sunlight enters a raindrop, it slows down and bends, a process called refraction. Inside the drop, the light reflects off the back surface, like a mirror, and then bends again as it leaves the drop. Because different colours bend by slightly different amounts, they separate: red bends least and appears on the outer edge, violet bends most and appears on the inner edge. Millions of drops, each sending a slightly different colour to your eye depending on its position, combine to form the continuous arc you see.

## The Three Conditions for a Rainbow

A rainbow needs three conditions at once. First, the sun must be behind you and relatively low in the sky, generally below about 42 degrees above the horizon for a primary rainbow. That is why rainbows are common in late afternoon or early morning, after a shower, when the sun is low and rain is still falling in the distance. At midday, when the sun is high, the rainbow arc would fall below the horizon and you would not see it from flat ground, though you might see one from a mountain or an aeroplane, where the horizon is lower.

Second, there must be rain in front of you, in the air between you and the distant sky. The rain does not need to be falling on you; in fact, you are usually standing in sunshine while rain falls a short distance away. That contrast, sun on your back, rain ahead, is the classic rainbow setup. A garden hose spray, a waterfall mist, or a lawn sprinkler can create the same effect on a small scale, because the physics is identical: sunlight, water drops, and an observer in the right position.

Third, the sky behind the rain should be relatively dark, or at least not too bright, so the colours stand out. A dark rain cloud behind the shower makes the rainbow vivid, while a bright, hazy sky can wash it out. That is why the most spectacular rainbows appear against storm clouds after a shower, when the sun breaks through behind you. If any condition is missing, high sun, no rain ahead, or no direct sunlight, no rainbow appears, no matter how beautiful the sky looks otherwise.

## Refraction, Reflection, and Why Colours Separate

The detailed path of light through a raindrop explains the colour order. Sunlight enters the front of a spherical drop and refracts, bending toward the normal, an imaginary line perpendicular to the surface. Because the drop is water, light slows down, and shorter wavelengths, violet and blue, bend more than longer wavelengths, red and orange. Inside the drop, the light hits the back surface and undergoes total internal reflection, bouncing back toward the front. As it exits, it refracts again, bending further and separating the colours even more. Each drop sends a full spectrum outward, but your eye receives only one colour from each drop, depending on the angle between the sun, the drop, and your eye.

Drops higher in the sky send red light to your eye, because red light leaves the drop at a steeper angle, about 42 degrees from the direction of incoming sunlight. Drops lower in the sky send violet light, at about 40 degrees. Drops in between send orange, yellow, green, and blue. Your brain assembles these separate contributions into a continuous arc, with red on the outside, then orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo, and violet on the inside. The traditional list of seven colours comes from Isaac Newton, who divided the spectrum into seven bands by analogy with musical notes. In reality, the spectrum is continuous, with no sharp boundaries, and different cultures name the bands differently. The seven-colour list is a useful teaching aid, not a physical law.

The same physics explains why a rainbow is an arc, not a straight line or a full band. The set of drops that send a given colour to your eye lies on a cone with its tip at your eye and its axis pointing away from the sun, toward the point opposite the sun, called the antisolar point. Where that cone meets the rain, you see an arc. If the ground did not block the lower part, you would see a full circle. From an aeroplane, or a high mountain with rain below, a full circular rainbow is sometimes visible, which surprises people who have only ever seen arcs from the ground. The circle is the true shape; the arc is what the ground lets you see.

## Primary, Secondary, and Supernumerary Rainbows

The bright rainbow most people see is the primary rainbow, formed by one internal reflection inside each drop. Sometimes a fainter secondary rainbow appears outside the primary one. It is formed by two internal reflections inside each drop, which reverses the colour order: red on the inside, violet on the outside, and makes it dimmer, because light is lost at each reflection. The region between the primary and secondary rainbows often looks darker than the sky on either side. This dark band, called Alexander's band after Alexander of Aphrodisias, who described it in antiquity, occurs because neither single nor double reflection sends light to the eye from drops in that angular region. It is a subtle effect, but once you notice it, you will see it in many double rainbows.

Occasionally, faint extra bands appear just inside the primary rainbow, called supernumerary rainbows. These are pastel, closely spaced arcs, usually pink and green, caused by interference between light waves that take slightly different paths through drops of similar size. They are most visible when raindrops are small and uniform, such as in a light shower. Their presence is a clue about drop size: large, varied drops produce a bright, simple rainbow; small, uniform drops produce supernumerary bands. Photographers often capture them without realising what they are, mistaking them for a camera artefact.

Other rainbow-like effects are related but different. A fogbow, seen in fog, looks white or pale because fog droplets are so small that colours overlap and wash out. A moonbow, or lunar rainbow, forms at night from moonlight, and looks faint or white to the eye, though long-exposure photographs reveal colours. A rainbow seen in spray from a waterfall is a spraybow. All share the same basic physics, sunlight or moonlight, water drops, and observer geometry, with differences in drop size and light brightness explaining their appearance. Understanding one explains them all.

## Why No Two People See the Same Rainbow

A rainbow is personal in a literal, optical sense. Because the arc is defined by the angle between the sun, the drops, and your eye, moving your eye moves the rainbow. Two observers standing metres apart see rainbows formed by different sets of drops, even though they appear to be in the same place. If you walk toward a rainbow, it appears to retreat, because the geometry moves with you. You can never reach the end of a rainbow, and there is no pot of gold, not because the legend is cruel, but because the rainbow has no fixed location. It is a pattern of light that exists only in relation to an observer.

This also explains why a rainbow photographed by two people from different spots looks slightly different, and why a rainbow in a photograph does not show exactly what the photographer saw, because a camera lens sits in a slightly different position from the eye. It also explains a common childhood question: why does a rainbow disappear when the rain stops or the sun goes behind a cloud? Remove the drops or the direct sunlight, and the geometry collapses. The rainbow does not move away or fade like a cloud; it simply ceases to be formed, because one of its ingredients is gone.

The personal nature of rainbows has cultural consequences. Almost every culture has rainbow myths, often involving bridges, serpents, gods, or promises. The Norse Bifrost bridge, the Biblical covenant after the flood, and the Irish pot of gold all treat the rainbow as a thing with a location and a purpose. Science does not diminish those stories; it adds another layer. Knowing that a rainbow is light, geometry, and water does not make it less beautiful. For many people, it makes it more beautiful, because it connects a fleeting sky effect to universal physics that works the same way in a garden hose, a waterfall, and a distant storm.

## Rainbows in Art, Science, and History

The scientific study of rainbows has a long history. Ancient Greek and Roman writers described rainbows and speculated about their causes. In the thirteenth century, scholars such as Theodoric of Freiberg used experiments with water-filled glass spheres to model raindrops and explain the arc. In the seventeenth century, Isaac Newton used prisms to show that white light contains colours, and Rene Descartes calculated the angles of refraction and reflection that produce the primary and secondary bows. Their work turned a mythic object into a measurable phenomenon, and it remains a classic example of how experiment and geometry can explain everyday wonder.

Artists have also used rainbows to signal hope, transition, and the sublime. In landscape painting, a rainbow often marks the moment after a storm, when light returns. In photography, rainbows are prized because they are brief and require the right conditions, making a good rainbow photograph a matter of patience and luck as well as skill. Modern science continues to study rainbows, not only for their own sake, but as a tool: the colours and brightness of a rainbow reveal raindrop size, which helps meteorologists understand rainfall. Polarisation of rainbow light, invisible to the naked eye but measurable with instruments, carries further information about drop shape and composition.

Rainbows also appear in other contexts that surprise people. A rainbow can form in the spray of a garden hose on a sunny day, in the mist above a waterfall such as Niagara or Victoria Falls, and in the spray from a boat. Astronauts and pilots report circular rainbows and glories, related optical effects, from above clouds. Each sighting follows the same rules, which is why a child who learns rainbow geometry with a hose can predict when a real rainbow will appear after a shower. That transfer, from a toy to the sky, is one of the joys of simple physics.

## How to Find and Photograph a Rainbow

Finding a rainbow is a matter of position and timing. Look for a shower that is ending, with the sun breaking through behind you and dark rain still visible ahead. Late afternoon is best, because the sun is low, the arc is high, and the light is warm, which makes colours richer. Face away from the sun, with the rain in front of you, and scan the sky opposite the sun. If the sun is higher than about 42 degrees, do not expect a primary bow from flat ground; wait for the sun to lower, or climb to a high viewpoint. A rainbow app or weather radar can help, but the simplest method is to step outside after rain when the sun appears, turn your back to it, and look.

For photography, a few tips help. Use a wide-angle lens or step back to fit the whole arc, since rainbows are large. A polarising filter can deepen colours by reducing glare, but it can also unevenly darken the sky, so check the result. Expose for the rainbow, not for the bright sky, and take several shots quickly, because rainbows fade as rain stops or clouds move. Include foreground interest, a field, a tree, a building, to give scale, but remember that the rainbow's apparent position will shift if you move, so compose quickly. Most importantly, enjoy the sight first and photograph second; a rainbow lasts minutes, and a memory of watching it fully is worth more than a rushed photograph.

Safety matters too. Do not chase a rainbow into a storm, cross a road without looking, or stand in an exposed place during lightning. A rainbow after a thunderstorm is safe to admire once the storm has passed, but a rainbow during active lightning means the storm is still near. The best rainbow watching is calm, patient, and safe: sunshine on your back, rain ahead, and time to watch colours appear, brighten, and fade.

## My Take:

My view is that rainbows are the perfect introduction to science, because they reward understanding instead of punishing it. Many people fear that explaining a beautiful thing will spoil it, that a rainbow explained becomes a rainbow diminished. I have found the opposite. Knowing that a rainbow is sunlight split by drops, that its circle is hidden by the ground, that its colours depend on your eye, makes each sighting richer, not poorer. You start to notice the dark band between bows, the faint supernumerary arcs, the way the bow moves with you. Explanation does not remove wonder; it multiplies it, by giving you more to notice. That is true for many natural wonders, but rainbows prove it in the most accessible way, because anyone can test the explanation with a hose on a sunny day.

I also think rainbows teach a useful lesson about perspective. Two people can look at the same sky and, in a strict optical sense, see different rainbows. Neither is wrong, and neither is lying. Their rainbows differ because their positions differ. That is a gentle model for disagreement in general. Often we argue as if there is one view that must be correct, when in fact two honest observers, standing in different places, quite literally see different things. Science does not resolve that by declaring one person wrong; it explains why both views arise from the same physics and the same geometry. If we carried that attitude into other disagreements, we might listen more and insist less, while still trusting that an underlying reality, sunlight, drops, angles, explains both views.

There is also a lesson about conditions. A rainbow does not appear because you want it, or because the sky is pretty. It appears when three conditions align, and no amount of wishing substitutes for them. That discipline is worth learning. Much of life works the same way: a result needs its ingredients, its timing, and its position, and frustration often comes from demanding the result while missing one ingredient. Rainbow hunting teaches patience and observation: check the sun, check the rain, turn your back to the light, wait. Those are not just photography tips; they are a habit of mind. Look for the conditions, not only the outcome, and you will find more of what you seek, in weather and elsewhere.

Finally, I think rainbows deserve their cultural place as symbols of hope, not because they are magic, but because they appear after rain. The sequence matters: storm, then sun, then colour. A rainbow does not prevent the storm, and it does not promise that no storm will come again. It marks a moment when light and water coexist, briefly and beautifully, after difficulty. That is a more honest kind of hope than a promise that trouble will never return. It says: after rain, light can still split into colour, and you, standing in the right place, can see it. That is enough. It is enough to step outside after a shower, turn away from the sun, and look, not for gold at the end, but for the arc itself, which was never a destination, but a way of seeing.

I want to add a practical reflection for parents and teachers, because rainbows are often a child's first science question. Answer it with an experiment, not only with words. Take a hose, a spray bottle, or a glass of water in sunlight, and let the child move until colours appear. Let them discover that moving changes the rainbow, that the sun must be behind them, that the spray must be in front. That discovery will stay with them longer than any definition, because they found it themselves. Science taught as a list of facts fades; science taught as a way to predict and test what you will see next lasts. A rainbow, brief and beautiful, is the ideal first experiment, because it costs nothing, it works in a garden, and it leaves a child looking at the sky with new eyes, which is, I think, the best outcome any explanation can hope for.

There is also a lesson about beauty and scarcity. A rainbow lasts minutes, sometimes seconds, and it cannot be scheduled, stored, or replayed on demand. That brevity is part of its power. We value it precisely because it is fleeting, and because seeing one requires being in the right place at the right time, looking in the right direction. Modern life trains us to expect everything on demand, recorded, and repeatable, but a rainbow refuses that bargain. It appears when conditions align, not when we press a button, and it fades whether or not we photographed it. Learning to appreciate something that cannot be owned or repeated is a useful counterweight to a culture of capture and control. Sometimes the best response to beauty is simply to watch it, without trying to preserve it, and to accept that watching is enough.

I think rainbows also deserve a place in how we teach children about the sky, because they connect everyday weather to physics without any equipment. A child who has seen a rainbow in a hose spray understands refraction before they know the word, and they understand that light has colours hidden inside it, that water can split light, and that their own position matters. Those are not trivial ideas; they are the foundations of optics, and they can be learned in a garden, in minutes, for free. Schools spend considerable effort teaching abstract science from textbooks, while the sky offers a free, spectacular demonstration after every suitable shower. The missing step is often just pointing it out: turn around, sun behind you, rain ahead, look opposite the sun. That one sentence, taught to a child, can turn every future shower into a potential discovery, and that habit of looking, of predicting from conditions, is the core of scientific thinking itself.

One more practical reflection is worth keeping: a rainbow is a reminder to look behind you, not only ahead. You find it by turning away from the sun, toward the rain you have already passed through, and that reversal is unusual. Most of life rewards looking forward, toward goals and plans, but a rainbow rewards looking back at the storm that has just ended, with light behind you. That small physical act, turning around, carries a useful metaphor. After a difficult period, it is worth pausing, turning, and noticing what the light does with what remains, rather than rushing immediately to the next task. The colours will not last, the rain will clear, and the moment will pass, but noticing it fully, for a minute, is a skill worth practising. It costs nothing, it requires no equipment, and it leaves you, briefly, more attentive to the world than you were before you looked up.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How do rainbows form in simple terms?**
Sunlight enters raindrops, bends and splits into colours, reflects off the back of the drop, and bends again on exit. Millions of drops together form the coloured arc you see.

**Why do rainbows appear after rain?**
You need sun behind you and rain in front of you. After a shower, the sun often breaks through while rain still falls in the distance, creating exactly those conditions.

**Why is a rainbow curved?**
The drops that send each colour to your eye lie on a cone centred on the point opposite the sun. That cone appears as an arc, and would be a full circle if the ground did not block the lower part.

**Can you touch or reach the end of a rainbow?**
No, a rainbow has no fixed location. It moves with the observer, because it is formed by the geometry of sun, drops, and your eye, not by an object in the sky.

**What is a double rainbow?**
A secondary rainbow forms outside the primary one from two reflections inside each drop. It is fainter and its colours are reversed, with red on the inside.

**Why do some rainbows look white?**
In fog, droplets are so small that colours overlap and wash out, producing a white fogbow. Moonbows at night also look pale because moonlight is dim, though cameras can reveal colours.
