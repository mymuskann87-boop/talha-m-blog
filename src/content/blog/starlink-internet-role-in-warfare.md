A flat dish, smaller than a suitcase, sits on a roof or beside a tent. There is no cable running to it. There is no phone tower nearby. Yet a soldier can send a message, open a map and talk to a commander many miles away. The connection comes from the sky, from thousands of small satellites passing overhead. That is Starlink, and in the war in Ukraine it has become one of the most important pieces of equipment on the battlefield.

This post explains what Starlink is, how it is used in war, and why losing it, even for a short time, worries commanders so much.

## What Starlink Actually Is

Starlink is a satellite internet service run by SpaceX, the company founded by Elon Musk. Instead of sending the internet through cables under the ground and towers on hills, it beams connections down from a large group of satellites orbiting close to the Earth. Users connect through a small dish, often called a terminal, that talks to the satellites passing above.

The idea is simple to use. As Musk himself has put it, the terminals have two instructions: point at the sky, and plug in. A soldier, a hospital worker or a journalist can carry a terminal in a box, place it outside, and be online within minutes. Where war has destroyed towers and cut cables, that simplicity matters more than speed alone.

## How The Signal Travels

A Starlink terminal on the ground sends a signal up to a satellite passing overhead. The satellite passes the signal on, either to another satellite or down to a ground station connected to the wider internet. Because the satellites move in low orbit, close to the Earth, the connection is fast enough for live video, voice calls and maps.

Thousands of satellites are involved. No single satellite stays overhead for long, so the terminal switches from one to the next as they pass. The result, for the user, feels like ordinary internet, except that it arrives from space and does not depend on local phone lines or mobile towers at all.

## Why Normal Internet Fails In War

In a war zone, ordinary communications are fragile. Mobile towers are tall, easy to find and easy to hit. Underground cables can be cut by shelling or digging. Power cuts silence routers and base stations. When towers fall, whole areas can go dark at once, and the people who need information most, soldiers, medics and civilians, are left without it.

Starlink does not depend on any of that local infrastructure. A dish needs power and a view of the sky. That is all. Destroy one dish and another can be unpacked. There is no single tower whose fall silences a whole region. That resilience is the reason it spread so quickly across Ukraine after Russia's full-scale invasion, when normal networks were damaged or destroyed.

## The Backbone Of Battlefield Communications

In Ukraine, Starlink has become what one expert called the backbone of military communications. George Barros, from the Institute for the Study of War, told the BBC that the network is used across Ukraine's naval, land and air forces. It is not a side tool. It is the connection that holds many operations together.

Soldiers use it to communicate along a long and dangerous front line. Commanders use it to share information quickly. Hospitals, officials, journalists and ordinary families have also depended on it when normal networks went dark. A soldier in a trench, far from any city, can still send a message and see a map because satellites overhead carry his words down to Earth.

## Drones, Artillery And Command Posts

The most important military use is linking different parts of the battle together. Starlink lets drones, artillery and command posts function as a single real-time network, Barros explained, where the time between sensing a target and shooting at it is minimised. A drone operator can see a target in real time through the network. That picture can reach a commander at once. An order can go back just as fast.

Ukraine also relies on the Starlink network to protect its own skies against incoming Russian attacks, especially jet-powered Shahed drones, Barros said. Shahed drones fly at speeds above 198 miles per hour, about 319 kilometres per hour, so chasing them down needs fast, reliable connections. Without the network, each part of the defence would work more slowly, and more slowly can mean too late.

## Starlink And Starshield

There are two related services in use in Ukraine: Starlink, the civilian service, and Starshield, the military service. Which one is used depends on what kind of communication is needed. The distinction matters because it shows that satellite internet in war is not one simple product. It is a set of services, contracts and rules, run by a private company, used by both civilians and armed forces.

That mixture is unusual. In earlier wars, military communications were owned and run by states and armies. In this war, a civilian network, built for homes and businesses, carries signals that soldiers rely on to survive. The same dish that serves a family can serve a command post. The line between civilian and military use is thin, and that thin line is part of why arguments about Starlink become so heated.

## The Argument Over Control

Because Starlink is owned by a private company, decisions about it are made far from the battlefield. Speed limits, service changes or interruptions decided in an office can affect soldiers in a trench within hours. The cited explainer notes that recent limits have already shown how dependent Kyiv is on the network, and with no ready replacement, any restriction or loss of connectivity has major implications for Ukraine's defences. Dependence, not just disruption, is the problem.

## What Happens When The Connection Slows

A slower connection is not a small inconvenience in war. If a drone's video freezes, its operator cannot see the target. If a commander's message arrives late, artillery may fire at the wrong place or not at all. If air defence units cannot share warnings quickly, incoming drones may get through.

That is why reports of limits or outages travel so fast. For ordinary users, a slow evening means a buffering video. For a military relying on a satellite network, a slow hour can mean lost information, missed warnings and greater danger. The stakes are what turn a business decision into a matter of national security.

## Limits And Risks Of Satellite Internet

Starlink is powerful, but it is not magic. Terminals need electricity, and power is scarce in war. The signal needs a clear view of the sky, so deep basements and dense cover can block it. The network depends on satellites, ground stations and company decisions that no army fully controls.

There is also the risk of jamming and interference. Enemies try to block or confuse satellite signals, just as they try to block radios. Starlink's large number of satellites makes it harder to silence than a single tower, but no system is untouchable. War is also a contest over signals, and every connection can become a target.

## Why The World Is Watching

Starlink in Ukraine is watched far beyond Ukraine because it may be a preview of future wars. If a private satellite network can become the backbone of a country's defence, other countries will ask who owns their connections, and what happens if the owner changes the rules. Governments, armies and companies are all learning the same lesson at the same time.

For readers, the key point is simple. A small dish pointing at the sky now does a job that once needed towers, cables and state-run networks. It links drones, guns and commanders into one fast network. And because it is owned by one company, every argument about it is also an argument about who controls the tools of modern war.
