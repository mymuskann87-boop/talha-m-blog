Imagine turning up for work and being told to go home, unpaid, because your employer has run out of permission to pay you. Now imagine that employer is the largest in the country, with millions of workers, and that the work includes national parks, museums, food inspections and passport offices. That, in simple terms, is a United States government shutdown.

Shutdowns sound dramatic, and they are disruptive. But they follow a clear, almost mechanical cause. This post explains what a shutdown is, why it happens, who is affected, and how it ends, using the record set out by USAFacts.

## The Basic Idea In One Sentence

A government shutdown occurs when Congress fails to pass, or the president refuses to sign, a spending bill to fund the federal government's operations. The result is a funding gap. Without legal permission to spend money, many government functions must stop or slow down until funding is restored.

The key word is permission. The government does not usually shut down because it has no money in the bank. It shuts down because the law that allows it to spend money has expired. No spending bill, no spending. That legal rule is what turns a political disagreement in Congress into closed offices across the country.

## Why Congress Must Pass Spending Bills

The United States government is funded through appropriation bills, laws that give agencies permission to spend money for a set period. There are 12 such bills, covering different parts of the government, from defence to health to transport. Each year, Congress is supposed to pass them before the funding year begins on 1 October.

If Congress cannot agree in time, it often passes a short-term funding measure called a continuing resolution. A continuing resolution provides temporary funding while negotiations for a longer-term solution continue. Every shutdown since 1990 has been ended with a continuing resolution, according to USAFacts. When neither a full bill nor a continuing resolution is in place, the funding gap becomes a shutdown.

## Full Shutdowns And Partial Shutdowns

Not every shutdown stops the whole government. A partial government shutdown happens when some, but not all, of the 12 appropriation bills expire without new funding. Which areas are affected depends on which bills remain unsigned. A department whose funding bill has passed keeps working normally. A department whose bill has lapsed must shut down non-essential work.

The longest partial shutdown in American history, according to USAFacts, concerned the Department of Homeland Security. Its funding lapsed beginning on 14 February 2026 and was restored 76 days later, on 30 April. A full shutdown, by contrast, affects every agency that depends on annual appropriations. The distinction matters, because headlines that say "the government shut down" may mean all of it, or only part of it.

## A Short History Of Shutdowns

The current budget process was established in 1976. Since then, the government has had 23 funding gaps, resulting in 13 shutdowns of various lengths. Before the 1980s, funding gaps did not usually result in shutdowns. Agencies would continue operating in the expectation that funding would resume. Since 1982, shortly after the basis for shutdowns was established, funding gaps have led to shutdowns more frequently.

The most recent full shutdown before this post was written lasted from 1 October 2025 to 12 November 2025, a total of 43 days, making it the longest complete shutdown in American history. Before that, the government last shut down in 2019, a shutdown that President Trump said was partly due to disputes over the border wall with Mexico. Each shutdown has its own political cause, but the mechanism is always the same: no funding law, no work.

## Who Is Furloughed And Who Keeps Working

During a shutdown, federal workers fall into two broad groups. Non-essential employees are furloughed, which means they are placed on mandatory temporary unpaid leave until Congress approves funding. Essential employees, whose work protects life and property, continue to work, often without pay until the shutdown ends.

USAFacts lists the groups that keep working: military service members, law enforcement agents and air traffic controllers, among others. Federal employees classed as excepted are in roles whose funding depends on appropriations bills but who are still required to work during a shutdown. Agencies are legally required to compensate excepted employees for their work, even if the money is not immediately available. Furloughed employees generally receive back pay when the shutdown ends, though the wait can be painful for families living from one pay cheque to the next.

## What Stops And What Keeps Going

The services affected depend on the funding legislation in force at the time. Past shutdowns have affected national parks and monuments, federal museums, federal research projects, the processing of certain government benefits, and taxpayer services at the IRS. National parks and museums may close, or stay open with limited services and reduced or no visitor help.

Many essential services continue. Social Security and Medicare benefits keep being paid. The military and federal law enforcement keep working. The US Postal Service keeps delivering mail, because it operates independently and generates its own revenue. Air traffic control continues. Passport services are generally not expected to be affected, though much depends on the length of the shutdown. Taxes must still be paid, and the IRS typically continues processing returns and payments, though refunds and audits may be delayed.

## How The Government Decides What Is Essential

The choice of what stays open is not random. Essential activities and personnel can continue if they are authorised by law, such as Social Security payments, necessary to protect life or property, such as law enforcement, medical care, air traffic control and food benefits, or necessary to run an orderly shutdown, such as issuing furlough notices.

Each agency prepares a contingency plan that lists which workers are essential and which services will pause. Those plans are what turn a funding gap into a concrete list of closed doors and empty desks. They are also why two shutdowns can look different: the plans, the bills that lapsed, and the length of the gap all shape what the public sees.

## The Human Cost Behind The Headlines

Shutdowns are often reported as political games, with winners and losers in Congress. For the people caught in them, they are more personal. A furloughed worker loses income for weeks, with bills that do not wait. A family waiting for a benefit, a permit or a passport faces delay and uncertainty. A small business near a closed national park loses customers. A scientist loses weeks of research that cannot be recovered.

The food assistance programme known as SNAP illustrates the risk. SNAP is considered an essential programme, but its funding relies on annual budgets, and without a budget or a continuing resolution, funds may run out. The programme for women, infants and children, known as WIC, has no permanent funding authority, so benefits could be delayed, reduced or stopped in a long shutdown. The longer a shutdown lasts, the more these risks grow from inconvenience to hardship.

## How A Shutdown Ends

A shutdown ends the way it began: with a law. Congress passes a spending bill or a continuing resolution, and the president signs it. Funding is restored, furloughed workers return, and back pay is arranged. The political argument that caused the shutdown may not be resolved at all. A continuing resolution only buys time, often a few weeks or months, before the same argument returns.

That is why shutdowns often end with a short-term deal rather than a full budget. Both sides can claim they protected their position, while the machinery of government restarts. The pattern has repeated for decades: deadline, deadlock, shutdown, temporary deal, and then the same deadline again. Until the underlying disagreement is settled, the cycle can repeat.

## Why Shutdowns Happen Only In America

Government shutdowns of this kind are unusual in the world. Most countries do not stop government spending when a budget is late. They keep spending at previous levels until a new budget is agreed. The United States is different because of its strict rule that agencies may not spend money without a current appropriation, and because its president and Congress are elected separately and can disagree sharply about spending.

That separation is deliberate. It is part of the system of checks and balances. But it has a price. When the two sides cannot agree, the machinery stops. Understanding that price helps readers see shutdowns for what they are: not accidents, and not the collapse of the state, but the visible result of a political system that requires agreement before the government can pay its own bills.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is a US government shutdown?**

A government shutdown occurs when Congress fails to pass or the president refuses to sign a spending bill to fund the federal government's operations, creating a funding gap that temporarily disrupts several government functions. The government does not shut down because it has no money, but because the law allowing it to spend has expired. Shutdowns are often resolved when Congress passes a continuing resolution, a short-term funding measure, and every shutdown since 1990 has been ended that way, according to USAFacts.

**What happens during a government shutdown?**

During a shutdown, non-essential government services close or operate with reduced staff, some federal employees are furloughed on mandatory temporary unpaid leave, and essential employees such as military members, law enforcement agents and air traffic controllers continue to work, often without pay until funding returns. National parks, federal museums, research projects, some benefit processing and IRS taxpayer services may be affected, while Social Security and Medicare benefits, the military, the Postal Service, air traffic control and passport services generally continue. Furloughed workers generally receive back pay once the shutdown ends.

**What is the difference between a full shutdown and a partial shutdown?**

A partial shutdown happens when some, but not all, of the 12 appropriation bills needed to fund the government expire, so only the departments covered by the lapsed bills are affected. A full shutdown affects every agency that depends on annual appropriations. The longest partial shutdown in US history concerned the Department of Homeland Security, whose funding lapsed on 14 February 2026 and was restored 76 days later on 30 April, while the longest complete shutdown lasted 43 days, from 1 October to 12 November 2025.

**Do federal workers get paid during a shutdown?**

Essential and excepted employees continue to work during a shutdown, and agencies are legally required to compensate them for that work even if funding is not immediately available to pay them. Non-essential employees are furloughed without pay during the shutdown, but they generally receive back pay when Congress approves funding and the shutdown ends. The president and members of Congress continue to be paid, because their salaries are required by the Constitution and classed as mandatory spending rather than annual appropriations.

**How long was the longest US government shutdown?**

The longest complete shutdown in US history lasted 43 days, from 1 October 2025 to 12 November 2025, according to USAFacts. Before that, the government last shut down in 2019, in a dispute partly over the border wall with Mexico. Since the current budget process began in 1976, there have been 23 funding gaps resulting in 13 shutdowns of various lengths, and before the 1980s funding gaps did not usually lead to shutdowns at all.
