Minute 49. Al Nassr against Al Faisaly, in Riyadh, on 23 October 2026. João Félix steps up to take a free kick. Cristiano Ronaldo jumps in the penalty area. The ball strikes the defender Andrei Girotto, and goes into the net. Ronaldo runs off celebrating. The question that followed, and that still has two different answers, is simple: whose goal was it? The Saudi Pro League says it was Ronaldo's 982nd career goal. The statistics company Opta says it was an own goal by Girotto, and Ronaldo's total stays at 981. This post reports both credits, from named sources, takes no side, and explains why football's record-keeping can produce two honest answers to the same question.

## The Match And The Minute

The match was Al Nassr against Al Faisaly, in the Saudi Pro League, on 23 October 2026. Al Nassr won 3-1. The early goal was scored by Al-Hamdan, who put Al Nassr ahead. Al Faisaly pulled one back through Bongonda. The disputed goal came in the 49th minute, and the late third goal for Al Nassr was scored by Ângelo Gabriel. ESPN's report of 24 October 2026 lists those scorers, and beIN Sports' report of 27 October 2026 gives the same scoreline and the same sequence.

The disputed goal came from a set piece. ESPN describes it as a free kick by João Félix, Ronaldo's teammate, and says it was unclear whether Ronaldo got a clear touch on the ball before it deflected off the defender Girotto. beIN Sports describes it as a delivery from João Félix that struck the defender, and says Ronaldo's header struck Girotto. The two descriptions differ in one detail — free kick or cross, header or touch — but they agree on the essential fact: the ball hit Girotto, and then went in. Whether Ronaldo touched it clearly enough, before the deflection, is the question on which the two counts disagree.

## The League's Credit: 982 For Ronaldo

The Saudi Pro League, which runs the competition, credited the goal to Ronaldo. beIN Sports reported on 27 October 2026 that the Saudi league's tally put Ronaldo at 982 career goals, 18 away from 1,000, and that the league had credited Ronaldo with the goal against Al Faisaly. The league's decision is the official record for the competition. It is the count that appears in the league's own statistics, and it is the count that Ronaldo himself celebrated, running off with his arms outstretched as the goal went in.

The league's credit follows a common practice in football: when a shot or a header is on its way towards goal and is deflected by a defender, the goal is often credited to the attacker, unless the deflection clearly changes the direction of the ball away from goal. The league's scorers' committee, or its equivalent, will have judged that Ronaldo's action was the decisive one, and that the deflection off Girotto did not change the outcome. That judgement is a matter of interpretation, and it is the league's right to make it for its own competition.

## Opta's Credit: Own Goal By Girotto

Opta, the statistics provider whose data is used by ESPN and by most broadcasters, recorded the goal differently. ESPN reported on 24 October 2026 that Opta adjudged the goal to be an own goal by Girotto, and that Opta's tally left Ronaldo at 981 career goals. beIN Sports, on 27 October, reported the same split: the Saudi Pro League credited Ronaldo, while Opta recorded it as an own goal by Girotto.

Opta's rules for own goals are stricter. If a defender's touch is the decisive deflection, and the attacker's touch is not clear, Opta may record the goal as an own goal. Opta's decision is not a judgement about who deserved the goal, or who celebrated it. It is an application of a statistical rule, designed to be consistent across thousands of matches and competitions. The rule is applied the same way whether the player is Ronaldo or a defender in a lower division. That consistency is why broadcasters use Opta, and why its count differs from the league's count in this case.

## What The Two Sources Actually Said

It is worth stating exactly what each source reported, because the dispute is often simplified in retelling. beIN Sports, on 27 October 2026, wrote that Ronaldo's header from a João Félix delivery struck defender Andrei Girotto, that the Saudi Pro League credited Ronaldo with the goal, taking him to 982, and that Opta recorded it as an own goal by Girotto, leaving the total at 981. ESPN, on 24 October 2026, wrote that Ronaldo celebrated his 982nd goal, that it may yet go down as an own goal, that the free kick left it unclear whether Ronaldo got a clear touch before the deflection off Girotto, and that Opta adjudged it an own goal.

Neither source says the other is wrong. Both report the disagreement. ESPN's phrase, "may yet go down as an own goal", captures the uncertainty: the final statistical record may settle on one count, but at the time of reporting, both counts were in use. This post follows the same approach. It reports both credits, attributes each to its source, and does not declare one to be the true count. The reader who wants a single number must choose a source. The honest answer is that there are two.

## The Precedent: Pepe In 2010

This is not the first time Ronaldo's goal count has been disputed over a deflection. In 2010, playing for Real Madrid against Real Sociedad, Ronaldo took a free kick that deflected off his teammate Pepe and went into the net. The referee awarded the goal to Pepe. The Spanish sports newspaper MARCA, which keeps its own scoring records, credited the goal to Ronaldo. The same pattern has repeated in 2026: a set piece, a deflection, an official award to one player, and a different credit from another source.

The precedent shows that the problem is not new, and it is not about Ronaldo alone. It is about the rules for crediting goals when the ball touches more than one player. Different bodies apply different rules, and the rules are not always public or consistent. The referee's decision, the league's decision, the newspaper's decision, and the statistics provider's decision can all differ, and each is defensible under its own rules. The 2010 case did not settle the question of who decides a goal. The 2026 case has not settled it either.

## Why Goal Credits Differ

Goal credits differ because there is no single, universal rule for deciding who scored a goal when the ball is deflected. The Laws of the Game, set by the International Football Association Board, do not define a detailed rule for statistical credit. Leagues, broadcasters, and statistics providers each make their own rules. Some credit the attacker if the ball was heading towards goal. Some credit the defender if the defender's touch is the last decisive touch. Some follow the referee's decision. Some apply a statistical rule designed for consistency across competitions.

The difference matters most for records. For the match result, it does not matter: Al Nassr won 3-1, whatever the credit. For Ronaldo's career total, it matters by one goal, which is the difference between 981 and 982, and between 18 and 19 goals to 1,000. For the history books, it matters because the 1,000th goal, when it comes, will be counted from one of these two starting points, and the two counts will point to different matches as the milestone. The record-keepers will have to choose, or the milestone will be celebrated twice, once on each count.

Statistics providers sometimes revise theirs too. Until a revision is announced, both counts remain in use, and any report that gives only one number without naming its source is incomplete. This post will not be updated to declare a winner, because the task of this post is to report the dispute, not to resolve it.
