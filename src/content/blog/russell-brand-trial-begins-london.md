The grey suit. The dark sunglasses. His wife's hand held tight in his.

When Russell Brand walked up the steps of Southwark Crown Court in London this week, the cameras flashed the way they have flashed for him for twenty years. But this time, he was not arriving at a film premiere or a comedy show. He was arriving at the fight of his life.

Inside that courtroom, twelve ordinary people — eight men and four women — will decide whether one of Britain's most famous entertainers spends his future as a free man, or in a prison cell. The trial has now begun. It could run for six weeks, maybe longer. And almost everything about it is extraordinary.

Here is the whole story, in simple words: what he is accused of, what he says, who is standing beside him, how a boy from a small English town became this famous, and what happens next.

## The quick facts

- **Full name:** Russell Edward Brand
- **Born:** 4 June 1975, in Grays, Essex, England — he is 51 years old
- **Job:** Comedian, actor, author and online broadcaster
- **Wife:** Laura Gallacher, married in 2017
- **Children:** Three — daughters Mabel and Peggy, and a young son
- **The charges:** Seven counts, from six women — three of rape, three of sexual assault, one of indecent assault
- **His plea:** Not guilty to every charge
- **The court:** Southwark Crown Court, London, before Mr Justice Bennathan

## What happened in court this week

The trial formally began on Monday. First, the jury was chosen. Then the judge gave them a warning that tells you how big this case is: do not search for this story on the internet, do not read old news about it, and judge only what you hear inside this room.

On Wednesday, the prosecution opened its case. The prosecutor told the jury that the man in the dock had treated women as a way to get what he wanted, and described him in the hardest words a courtroom allows at the start of a trial. The details of the allegations, reaching back more than twenty-five years, are now being laid out one by one.

Outside the court, Brand stopped for the reporters waiting for him. His words were calm: he said he felt concerned, but hopeful the process would lead to good outcomes for everybody. Then he walked inside to listen to the case against him.

One thing must be said clearly, because fairness matters: these are allegations. Brand has pleaded not guilty to every single charge, and in the eyes of the law he is innocent unless the jury decides otherwise. The women making the claims cannot be named — under British law, people who report sexual offences receive lifelong anonymity.

## The charges, explained simply

There are seven charges, and they come from six different women, in two groups.

The first group arrived in April 2025. Four women accused Brand of offences between 1999 and 2005 — one in a hotel in Bournemouth, where he had performed at a Labour Party conference, and others in London, connected to the television and radio studios where his young career was taking off.

The second group arrived in December 2025. Two more women, with allegations from 2009 in London, added one more rape charge and one more sexual assault charge.

Added together: three counts of rape, three counts of sexual assault, and one count of indecent assault, covering a full decade, 1999 to 2009. He pleaded not guilty to the first set in May 2025, and not guilty to the new charges in February 2026. Now all seven will be decided in this one trial.

## His answer: "It was never non-consensual"

Brand has never hidden from the accusations — he has fought them in public from the first day. When the first charges came, he released a video to his millions of followers. In it, he admitted his past openly: he had been a drug addict, a sex addict, and, in his own word, an imbecile. But a rapist, he said, he never was. Everything, he insists, was consensual — agreed to by everyone involved.

That is the line his defence will hold for the whole trial: a wild past, yes. A criminal one, no. The jury's job is to decide which version of those long-ago years is the truth.

## How did we get here? The 2023 turning point

For years, the stories existed only as rumours around a famous man. That changed in September 2023, when three big British news organisations — The Sunday Times, The Times, and Channel 4's Dispatches programme — published a joint investigation after speaking to several women.

Within days, London's Metropolitan Police opened an investigation. Detectives spent more than a year gathering evidence before the Crown Prosecution Service — the office that decides who gets charged in England — brought the charges in 2025.

So this courtroom battle is not sudden. It has been building for three years, step by step, headline by headline, to this week in Southwark.

## Who is Russell Brand? The rise of a wild star

Russell Edward Brand was born on 4 June 1975 in Grays, a working town in Essex, east of London. He was an only child. His parents separated when he was a baby, and his mother raised him, through years when she herself was fighting serious illness.

Fame found him in the mid-2000s. British audiences first loved him as the chaotic, big-haired host of Big Brother's Big Mouth, then as a BBC Radio 2 presenter. Hollywood followed: he stole scenes in Forgetting Sarah Marshall, led Get Him to the Greek, and played a rock star in Rock of Ages. His stand-up shows sold out, and his books became bestsellers.

But the public Brand was only half the story. Behind the fame sat years of drug addiction, which he has written and spoken about with unusual honesty. He has been clean for many years now, and that recovery became the centre of his second act.

## The reinvention: podcasts, faith and millions of followers

When mainstream television moved on, Brand did not disappear. He rebuilt himself online, talking about politics, addiction, wellness, and, increasingly, his Christian faith. His following today is enormous — millions subscribe to his videos and channels, and they do not treat him as a comedian any more. To many of them, he is a truth-teller fighting powerful systems.

That is what makes this trial so unusual. This is not only a court judging one man's past. In the eyes of his followers, and in the eyes of his critics, it is also a public test of two completely different Russell Brands. The next weeks will show which one the jury believes.

## The family standing beside him

Through all of it, one person has stayed visibly at his side: his wife.

Laura Gallacher is a writer and entrepreneur, the sister of television presenter Kirsty Gallacher and the daughter of famous golfer Bernard Gallacher. Her story with Brand began long before the wedding — they first met in 2007, when she was 19, but the romance was brief. Life took them apart. He married the biggest pop star on the planet. She built her own life. Then, in 2015, they bumped into each other by chance on a walk beside an east London canal — and this time, both of them knew it was serious.

They married in August 2017, in a private ceremony near Henley-on-Thames. Three children followed: Mabel, born in 2016, Peggy in 2018, and a son in 2023, whose arrival came with health worries and surgery in his first year. The family now lives in Florida, where Brand based his new career.

Before Laura, there was the marriage the whole world watched: pop superstar Katy Perry. They wed in a lavish ceremony in India in October 2010 — and barely a year later, he ended the marriage with a text message on New Year's Eve, a detail Perry later shared publicly. The divorce was final in 2012. That chapter feels like a different lifetime now, but it is part of why this trial is front-page news on every continent: for a few years, he was not just famous — he was half of the most talked-about couple in entertainment.

This week, it was Laura who held his hand on the courthouse steps.

## What happens next

Now the slow, serious part begins. Prosecutors will call their witnesses, one by one. Brand's lawyers will question every memory, every date, every detail — in a case about events up to twenty-seven years old, memory is everything. Brand himself may give evidence, or may not; that decision will be one of the trial's biggest moments.

Estimates for the length of the trial run from six weeks to as many as ten. At the end of it, twelve people will sit in a small room and answer seven questions, one for each charge. Guilty, or not guilty.

If he is cleared, he walks out to rebuild his life with his name restored by a jury. If he is convicted on the most serious counts, a man who once filled cinemas and stadiums is looking at a long prison sentence, and one of the most remarkable falls in modern celebrity history.

The cameras will be on those courthouse steps every single day until then. So will we.
